\begin{abstract}
For a finite set $A$ of odd positive integers write
$F_A(z)=\prod_{a\in A}(1+z^{a})$ for the generating polynomial of the subset
sums, so that $r_A(n)=\#\{S\subseteq A:\sigma(S)=n\}$ is its $n$-th coefficient.
Everything below concerns $|F_A|$ on the unit circle, where
$|F_A(e^{i\theta})|=2^{|A|}\prod_{a\in A}|\cos(a\theta/2)|$, and the per-element
quantity $X(t)=-\log|\cos\pi t|$ that governs it.

Our first result is an identity for $X$ alone, elementary and exact:
\[
  \frac1v\sum_{k=0}^{v-1}X\!\Bigl(t+\frac kv\Bigr)
  \;=\;\Bigl(1-\frac1v\Bigr)\log2\;+\;\frac1v\,X(vt+\tau_v)
  \qquad\text{for every }v\ge1\text{ and every }t,
\]
with $\tau_v\in\{0,\tfrac12\}$ explicit. Averaging $X$ over a coset of index $v$
returns the same function at $v$ times the frequency, plus a constant.
\emph{The identity is classical}: it is the Kubert distribution relation for
$\log|2\sin\pi t|$~\cite{KL}, transported to the cosine by $t\mapsto t+\tfrac12$,
and we prove it twice --- by the multiplication formula, which is how the
classical relation is proved, and by Fourier. What is new here is the use.
Because $X\ge0$, the rational points are the \emph{minima} of the coset average
and not merely convenient sample points, and that turns the relation into a
uniform lower bound: the step of a minor-arc argument that normally costs a
quantitative equidistribution estimate costs nothing.

Parts~I and~II~\cite{P1,P2}, and the first half of this Part, fix two things at
once --- the set $A$, and the uniform measure on its subsets. The rest of this
Part moves each. Replacing the uniform measure by a Bernoulli$(q)$ product
measure deforms the gap series of Part~I into an explicit family
$\Gamma^{(q)}$, and we prove that the fair coin minimises it for every set,
with equality only at $q=\tfrac12$; the argument is termwise and is
machine-checked in Lean~4. We derive the corresponding transfer function $\Phi^{(q)}$ and
show that the bias splits the symmetric pair of strata of \S\ref{sec:transfer} in the ratio $q:(1-q)$, so that
the biased landscape responds to a shift of target at first order; the resulting slope is a
point prediction with no free parameter, and it is confirmed against the natural alternative
to within $0.4\%$. Replacing the primes by a random odd sequence, we prove that the extremal
modulus of the minor arcs moves from $6$ to $4$, so that the peak height available to a random
odd sequence is $1/\sqrt2$ where the primes have $\sqrt3/2$ --- the primes are the harder
case. We then prove that the geometric \emph{rate} follows: almost surely the minor-arc maximum
of $F_A^{1/b}$ tends to $1/\sqrt2$. The proof carries no ineffective constant and needs no
equidistribution input, because the coset identity replaces the usual discrepancy
estimate by an equality. Two questions inherited from the transfer-function strand are also settled or sharpened.
Every claim is tagged with its status, and the numerical work is reproducible from logged
scripts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Parts~I and~II and the transfer-function strand fixed two things at once: the sequence $A$, and the measure on its subsets. Every
statement of the trilogy is about a specific $A$ under the uniform measure. This paper moves
each of them, one at a time, and the two moves turn out to be worth making for different
reasons.

\paragraph{Bias.} The classification theorem of Part~I~\cite{P1} says which elements lie on which side
of a target. It says nothing about how configurations are weighted, so the entire
combinatorial layer survives verbatim when the uniform measure is replaced by the
Bernoulli$(q)$ product measure, and the gap series deforms into an explicit one-parameter
family $\Gamma^{(q)}$ (\S\ref{sec:biased}). The answer has a shape worth putting in the
introduction: \textbf{the fair coin gives the least rugged landscape}, for every sequence, by
a termwise convexity argument that is machine-checked (Theorem~\ref{thm:fair},
Remark~\ref{rem:lean22}). The transfer function deforms too, and here the deformation is not
cosmetic: the transfer-function strand's $\cosh$ splits into two unequal exponentials with offsets in the ratio
$q:(1-q)$, so the biased landscape responds to a shift of target at \emph{first} order rather
than second (\S\ref{sec:phiq}). Rigidity off the fair coin is a tilt, not a plateau.

\paragraph{Coarse-graining.} \S\ref{sec:coset} is one lemma and it is the technical spine of the
paper. Averaging the energy $X(t)=-\log|\cos\pi t|$ over the coset $\{t+k/v\}$ returns
$X$ at $v$ times the frequency, plus $(1-1/v)\log2$, exactly (Lemma~\ref{lem:coset}, proved by
the multiplication formula and again by Fourier). Since $X\ge0$, the rational points are
therefore the \emph{minima} of the coset average, not merely convenient sample points
(Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}) --- so the step of a minor-arc argument that normally costs a
quantitative equidistribution estimate costs nothing here. Read as physics it is a block-spin
step with an exact fixed point; we say so once, in Remark~\ref{rem:renorm}, and deduce nothing
from the reading.

\paragraph{Randomness.} For a random odd sequence the minor-arc input that cost Part~II~\cite{P2} fifty
rounds is replaced by a concentration bound, and \S\ref{sec:random} carries
\emph{no ineffective constant at all} --- the first such statement in the series. What we did
not expect is that the random case is also \emph{easier}: the extremal modulus moves from $6$
to $4$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:modfour}), so the peak height drops from $\sqrt3/2$ to $1/\sqrt2$, and
the geometric rate improves in step (Theorem~\ref{prop:rate}). The modulus moves for a
one-line arithmetic reason --- a set of primes contains at most one element
$\equiv3\pmod6$, a random odd set contains a third of them --- and the consequence is that
Part~II's $\sqrt3/2$ is a property of the primes, not a limitation of the method.
\emph{The primes are the hard case.}

\paragraph{A methodological remark that this paper earned twice.} Deformations are not only a
way to generalise; they are a way to \emph{see}. \textbf{A deformation that breaks a symmetry
makes measurable everything the symmetry was hiding.} Constructing $\Gamma^{(q)}$ and
$\Phi^{(q)}$ audited the transfer-function strand for free, twice. First, the $c_d$ fork: asking how the omitted
factor behaves as $k$ grows, rather than how large it is at one $k$, closed a question the transfer-function strand
had left open (\S\ref{sec:inherited}). Second, and more sharply, the sign of $\lambda$: the untilted
$\Phi$ of \S\ref{sec:transfer} is an \emph{even} function of $\lambda$, so no observable in that paper depends on
the sign, and a definition that carried the wrong one survived three papers and an external
referee. Splitting the $\cosh$ made the sign observable in a single measurement. We record the
principle because it is a design rule, not an anecdote: when a quantity cannot be checked,
deform the problem until it can.

\paragraph{Verification, and the genre.} Every numbered claim carries a status tag: proved,
Lean-verified, experimentally confirmed with the range stated, or conjectured. The scripts
that produce every number in this paper write logs beside themselves and are part of the
artefact. This is now a recognisable genre --- machine-assisted mathematics shipped with
formal certificates, of which \emph{Ten advances in mathematics and theoretical computer
science} \cite{TenProofs} and its accompanying Lean~4 repository are the visible precedent.
The direction differs: that work attacks named open problems, whereas the present series
builds a small theory from its definitions upward, so the certificates here anchor
\emph{definitions and structural lemmas} rather than headline resolutions. The verification
ethos is the same one, and we take it as read rather than arguing for it.

\subsection{The transfer-function strand}\label{sec:strand}

Parts~I and~II~\cite{P1,P2} of this series establish that for the odd primes
$\lm/r\to\Gamma$ \emph{at the centre} of the landscape. Whether $\Gamma$ is an invariant of
the landscape or an accident of its most symmetric point is the question this paper answers,
and the answer turns out to be stronger than rigidity alone: the whole ratio is a function of
one local observable. The argument and what each part rests on:

\begin{itemize}
\item Parts~I and~II establish $\lm/r\to\Gamma$ \emph{at the centre}
      $n=\lfloor T/2\rfloor$: Part~I reduces it to a flatness hypothesis and Part~II
      discharges that hypothesis unconditionally. The obvious question --- whether $\Gamma$ is
      a property of the landscape or of its centre --- is what this paper answers.
\item The combinatorial layer (classification, exact stratification, sandwich) was proved
      \emph{for every target} in Part~I and is formally verified; nothing there needs redoing.
      \STATUS{Lean-verified}
\item The minor-arc estimates of Part~II~\cite{P2} bound $|G_B(\theta)|$ as a function of $\theta$ only.
      They do not refer to the extraction point $m$, which enters
      $r_B(m)=\frac1{2\pi}\int F_B(\theta)e^{-im\theta}d\theta$ only through a phase. They
      therefore transfer to off-centre targets unchanged. \STATUS{quoted from Part~II}
\item What is new is the middle, \S\ref{sec:bridge}: an additive bridge
      (Lemma~\ref{lem:kappa}) carrying the minor-arc estimates from the uniform measure to the
      exponential tilt, the minor-arc input in the form the bridge needs
      (Lemma~\ref{lem:eta}), and a tilted local limit theorem uniform over the extraction
      window (Proposition~\ref{prop:tiltlclt}). The odd orders in $\lambda$ cancel by the
      reflection symmetry of the landscape, which is where $\cosh$ enters.
\item One consequence of quoting rather than reproving deserves saying at the outset: \emph{the
      only ineffective constant in the trilogy is one and the same Siegel--Walfisz constant,
      and it enters each paper at exactly one point} --- here, part (b) of
      Lemma~\ref{lem:eta}. See \S\ref{sec:scope}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Notation, and what is inherited}\label{sec:notation}

The definitions are those of Part~I~\cite{P1}, written so that nothing assumes $n=T/2$.
Fix throughout an increasing sequence $A=(a_1,\dots,a_k)$ of odd positive integers,
$T=\sigma(A)$, $S_2=\sum a_i^2$, $V=S_2/4$, and a target $n$. Write $\rho=n/T$ and
$x=\tfrac12-\rho$. For $d\ge1$ put
\[
  N_d=\#\{i:a_i\le2d\},\qquad
  \sigma_d=\!\!\sum_{a_i\le 2d}\!\! a_i,\qquad
  s_d=\!\!\sum_{a_i\le 2d}\!\! a_i^{2},\qquad
  \delta_d=d+\tfrac{\sigma_d}{2},
\]
and let $B_d=\{a_i:a_i>2d\}$ be the layer-$d$ ensemble, of variance
$V_d=(S_2-s_d)/4=V-s_d/4$.

\begin{definition}[local logarithmic slope]\label{def:slope}
$\lambda(n)$ is the logarithmic derivative of the representation count at the target,
\[
  \lambda(n)\;=\;+\frac{d}{dm}\log r_A(m)\Big|_{m=n}
  \;=\;\tfrac14\log\frac{r_A(n+2)}{r_A(n-2)}+O(\cdot),
\]
so $\lambda>0$ below the centre, where $r_A$ is increasing.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[on the sign, and why it took the biased deformation to matter]\label{rem:sign}
An earlier version of this definition carried the opposite sign, which made it disagree with
Remark~\ref{rem:whyslope} (where $\lambda\approx(T/2-n)/V>0$ below the centre) and with
Remark~\ref{rem:tiltslope} (where $s/\lambda\to1$ with $s>0$ below the centre). Nothing in
this paper changed when the sign was corrected, and nothing could have: $\Phi$ is an
\emph{even} function of $\lambda$, so the sign of $\lambda$ is not observable anywhere in the
transfer function. It becomes observable only when the $\cosh$ is split into two unequal
exponentials, which is what happens under a biased measure; the discrepancy was found there
and is recorded here. The convention above is the one that makes $\lambda\approx s$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[why the slope and not the offset]\label{rem:whyslope}
The Gaussian approximation $\lambda\approx(T/2-n)/V$ is what one writes down first, and it is
wrong at the 10\% level for the purposes of this paper. Substituting the measured $\lambda$
for the Gaussian one takes the drift of the fitted coefficient across the range
$x\in[0.06,0.30]$ from $15$--$18\%$ down to $0.03$--$0.07\%$, and it dissolves a non-integer
exponent that an earlier draft of this programme had reported as a finding: the exponent was
an artefact of the wrong variable. This is not a cosmetic
choice: every statement below is false at the stated precision if $(T/2-n)/V$ is substituted
for $\lambda$.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[the tilt]\label{def:tilt}
$P_s$ is the product measure on subsets under which $a_i$ is present with probability
$p_{a}=(1+e^{sa})^{-1}$, and $s=s(n)>0$ is chosen so that $\sum_a a\,p_a=n$; thus $P_s$ is the
exponential tilt of the uniform measure whose mean sits at the target.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[the tilt and the slope agree to first order]\label{rem:tiltslope}
$s$ and $\lambda$ are the same quantity to leading order but not exactly: $s$ makes the
tilted \emph{mean} equal $n$, whereas $\lambda$ is the slope of $\log r_A$ \emph{at} $n$, and
the two differ by the mean--mode gap. Measured on three profiles,
\[
   \frac{s(n)}{\lambda(n)}\;=\;1+\frac{c_A}{k}+O(k^{-2}),
   \qquad c_A\approx1.85\ (\text{odds}),\ 2.03\ (\text{primes}),\ 2.87\ (\text{squares}),
\]
stable in $x$ and clean in $k$ over $k=100,\dots,220$.
\STATUS{experimentally confirmed; \texttt{tilt\_r088}}
The statements below are formulated in $\lambda$, and \emph{not} in $s$: substituting $s$
multiplies the residual of Table~\ref{tab:one} by three, exactly as the $\lambda^{2}$ leading
behaviour of $\Phi$ predicts. \STATUS{experimentally confirmed; \texttt{sres\_r088}}
\end{remark}

\section{An exact coset identity}\label{sec:coset}

Parts~I and~II and the transfer-function strand borrowed the vocabulary of statistical physics --- landscape, energy, transfer
function, tilt --- and used it as a source of the right questions. This section is the first
place where the borrowing runs the other way and produces an exact recursion rather than an
analogy. Everything later in the paper that concerns minor arcs
(\S\ref{sec:modfour}, \S\ref{sec:rate}) is a corollary of it.

Throughout write
\[
   X(t)\;=\;-\log|\cos\pi t|\;\in[0,\infty],
\]
the per-element energy of Parts~I and~II and the transfer-function strand at phase $t$.

\begin{lemma}[coset identity; the Kubert distribution relation, shifted]\label{lem:coset}
\STATUS{classical. This is the distribution relation for $\log|2\sin\pi t|$~\cite{KL} with
$t\mapsto t+\tfrac12$; we claim no part of it. What this Part contributes is
Corollary~\ref{cor:floor} and the use made of it in \S\ref{sec:rate}.}
For every integer $v\ge1$ and every real $t$,
\[
   \frac1v\sum_{k=0}^{v-1}X\Bigl(t+\frac kv\Bigr)
   \;=\;\Bigl(1-\frac1v\Bigr)\log2\;+\;\frac1v\,X(vt+\tau_v),
   \qquad
   \tau_v=\begin{cases}0,&v\text{ odd},\\[2pt]\tfrac12,&v\text{ even}.\end{cases}
\]
Equivalently $\prod_{k<v}|\cos\pi(t+k/v)|=2^{\,1-v}\,|\cos\pi(vt+\tau_v)|$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[First proof (multiplication formula)]
Write $2\cos\pi z=2\sin\pi(z+\tfrac12)$ and apply
$\prod_{k<v}2\sin\pi(y+\tfrac kv)=2\sin(v\pi y)$ --- the factorisation of $x^{v}-1$ read on the
unit circle --- with $y=t+\tfrac12$. The product of the $2|\cos|$ is $2|\sin\pi(vt+\tfrac v2)|$,
which is $2|\cos\pi vt|$ for $v$ odd and $2|\sin\pi vt|=2|\cos\pi(vt+\tfrac12)|$ for $v$ even.
Divide by $2^{v}$ and take logarithms.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Second proof (Fourier)]
From $-\log|2\sin\pi u|=\sum_{n\ge1}\cos(2\pi nu)/n$ and $u=t+\tfrac12$,
\[
   X(t)=\log2+\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^n\cos(2\pi nt)}{n}.
\]
Averaging over the coset annihilates every harmonic but those with $v\mid n$, since
$v^{-1}\sum_{k<v}\cos(2\pi n(t+k/v))$ is $\cos(2\pi nt)$ when $v\mid n$ and $0$ otherwise.
Writing $n=vp$ and using $(-1)^{vp}=(-1)^p$ for $v$ odd and $1$ for $v$ even, the surviving
series is $v^{-1}(X(vt+\tau_v)-\log2)$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
Two proofs check the \emph{statement}; one proof checks only the argument. The identity is
verified in product form for $v\le60$ at $200$ random shifts each, worst discrepancy
$2.5\cdot10^{-16}$, and the two Fourier partial sums agree to $8.8\cdot10^{-14}$.
\STATUS{proved, twice; and checked numerically, \texttt{stab\_r110}, \texttt{rate\_r112}}

\begin{corollary}[the rational points are the minima]\label{cor:floor}
$\displaystyle\frac1v\sum_{k<v}X\bigl(t+\tfrac kv\bigr)\ \ge\ \Bigl(1-\frac1v\Bigr)\log2$
for every $t$, with equality exactly when $vt+\tau_v\in\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$X\ge0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[what a surrogate costs, before any equidistribution input]\label{rem:surrogate}
\STATUS{proved (both inequalities and all three means); the ratios are arithmetic.
Measured for confirmation only, \texttt{leadc\_r143}.}
A minor-arc argument for a generating function $\prod_{a\in A}(1+e(a\theta))$ needs a lower bound
on the deficiency $D(\theta)=\sum_{a\in A}X(a\theta)$, since
$\bigl|\prod_{a\in A}(1+e(a\theta))\bigr|=2^{|A|}e^{-D(\theta)}$. The standard route replaces $X$
by a surrogate that a discrepancy estimate can handle. Two are available, and both are valid:
\[
  X(t)\ \ge\ \tfrac12\sin^{2}\pi t,\qquad\qquad X(t)\ \ge\ \tfrac{\pi^{2}}{2}\|t\|^{2},
\]
the first from $-\log c\ge1-c$ with $c=\cos^{2}\pi t$, the second because
$-\log\cos u=\tfrac{u^{2}}{2}+\tfrac{u^{4}}{12}+\tfrac{u^{6}}{45}+\cdots$ has only positive
coefficients on $|u|<\pi/2$. Both are equalities only at $t=0$.

Each surrogate caps the bound it can ever produce at its own mean over the circle, and those
means are not the same number:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
mean over the circle of & value & & factor lost\\
\midrule
$X(t)$ itself & $\log2$ & $0.69315$ & $1$\\
$\tfrac12\sin^{2}\pi t$ & $1/4$ & $0.25000$ & $2.773$\\
$\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{2}\|t\|^{2}$ & $\pi^{2}/24$ & $0.41123$ & $1.686$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\noindent
\emph{The loss is in the surrogate, not in the equidistribution input.} Hand the surrogate routes
their exponential sum and their discrepancy exactly --- no Weyl, no Koksma, no error term --- and
they still stop at $\tfrac14|A|$ and $\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{24}|A|$ where
Proposition~\ref{prop:redresidue} gives $|A|\log2$. The reason is visible in the two displays:
$X$ has a logarithmic singularity at the half-integers and a bounded convex surrogate does not,
and that singularity is where the mass of the average sits. Since the bound enters an exponent,
a factor $2.77$ in the constant is a factor $2.77$ in the exponent.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[what is machine-checked in Lemma~\ref{lem:coset}, and what is not]\label{rem:leancoset}
The identity is \emph{multiplicative in $v$}, and that is what has been formalised. Writing
$k=i+aj$ and inducting on $b$ splits a product over $\{k<ab\}$ into a double product with no
bijection and no analysis --- \Lean{prod\_range\_mul\_index}, stated for an arbitrary commutative
monoid. On top of it, \Lean{cosetId\_one} and \Lean{cosetId\_two} are the base cases ($v=2$ is
the double-angle formula and needs nothing else), and \Lean{cosetId\_mul} carries the identity
from $a$ and $b$ to $ab$, including the offset bookkeeping
$a\tau_b+\tau_a\equiv\tau_{ab}\pmod 1$ in all four parity cases, via
\Lean{abs\_cos\_add\_int\_mul\_pi}.
\emph{What is not formalised is the base case at an odd prime}, which is the only place a
product over roots of unity is needed, and which Mathlib does not supply. So the machine-checked
statement is: \emph{the identity holds at $1$ and $2$ and is closed under products}, hence holds
for every $v$ built from the primes at which it is established by hand. The reduction was
checked numerically before it was formalised, including a deliberately wrong $\tau$ as a
control.
\STATUS{Lean-verified (the reduction; not the odd-prime base case);
\texttt{Pnp/Theory/CosetProd.lean}, \texttt{coset\_mult\_r116}}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[what this says, and in which language]\label{rem:renorm}
The identity has two readings and they have different statuses; the paper keeps them apart.

\emph{As arithmetic} (proved) it says that averaging $X$ over a coset of index $v$ returns
\emph{$X$ itself, at $v$ times the frequency}, plus a constant --- so the family
$\{X(v\,\cdot\,)\}$ is closed under coarse-graining, exactly, with no error term. That is what
Corollary~\ref{cor:floor} turns into a uniform lower bound, and it is why \S\ref{sec:rate} needs
no equidistribution input: the question ``how far can the mean fall when the rational grid is
shifted?'' has the answer \emph{it cannot fall at all}.

\emph{As physics} (an interpretation, and nothing is deduced from it) this is a block-spin step.
Coarse-graining the phase lattice by a factor $v$ maps the energy to a rescaled copy of itself;
the map has an exact fixed-point structure rather than an approximate one, and the constant
$(1-1/v)\log2$ is the free energy released per coarse-graining step, additive over successive
steps because $\tau$ composes. Parts~I and~II and the transfer-function strand treated the landscape picture as a source of
questions; this is the first identity in the series that the picture predicts and the arithmetic
confirms. \S\ref{sec:outlook} says what we would want to do with it and does not do it here.
\end{remark}

\section{Rigidity}\label{sec:rigid}

\begin{theorem}[rigidity of the gap series]\label{thm:rigid}
\STATUS{conditional on Problem~\ref{prob:R1} of \S\ref{sec:missing} and on nothing else; that problem is the whole of what is missing, and it is shared with Proposition~\ref{prop:tiltlclt} and Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer} --- see the consumer list there. The missing ingredient is named rather than left to be located.
The analytic ingredients are proved in \S\ref{sec:bridge}. The Edgeworth expansion in

\section{The transfer function}\label{sec:transfer}

\begin{theorem}[transfer]\label{thm:transfer}
\STATUS{conditional on Problem~\ref{prob:R1} of \S\ref{sec:missing} and on nothing else; that problem is the whole of what is missing, and it is shared with Proposition~\ref{prop:tiltlclt} and Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer} --- see the consumer list there. The missing ingredient is named rather than left to be located.
The analytic ingredients are proved in \S\ref{sec:bridge}. The Edgeworth expansion in
region~R1 of Proposition~\ref{prop:tiltlclt} --- the one thing that was missing --- is now
written out in Appendix~\ref{app:R1}, with every constant proved; it awaits the independent
reading this project requires before an argument counts as proved, and until that is on record
the statement below stays conditional on it and on nothing else.}
Let $A=P_k$ be the first $k$ odd primes, or a general profile satisfying~\textup{(H)}, and let
$n=\lfloor\rho T\rfloor$ with $\rho\in(0,1)$ fixed. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi}
  \frac{\lm_A(n)}{r_A(n)}\;=\;\Phi_k\bigl(\lambda(n)\bigr)\;+\;E_k,
  \qquad
  \Phi_k(\lambda)=1+\sum_{d\ge1}2^{1-N_d}\,
     e^{-\lambda^{2}s_d/8}\,\cosh(\lambda\delta_d),
\end{equation}
with $E_k\to0$ as $k\to\infty$, uniformly for $\rho$ in compact subsets of $(0,1)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[what the theorem does and does not claim about $E_k$]\label{rem:whatEk}
The assertion is $E_k\to0$ in absolute terms, nothing sharper. The measured rate
$E_k\asymp c_A/k$ of Remark~\ref{rem:onek} is \emph{not} part of the statement; it is quoted as
\STATUS{experimentally confirmed, $k\le220$, three profiles} and stated as an open problem in
\S\ref{sec:scope}. Distinguishing the two matters here more than usual, because the numerical
agreement is far better than what is proved.
\end{remark}

Three features of \eqref{eq:phi} deserve to be stated before any proof.

\begin{remark}[the ratio sees the target only through one number]
$\Phi$ depends on $A$ alone. The target enters only through $\lambda(n)$. This is a stronger
statement than rigidity: rigidity says the limit does not move, \eqref{eq:phi} says how it
fails to move.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[$\lambda=0$ recovers Part~I~\cite{P1}]\label{rem:lam0}
The layer weight $e^{-\lambda^2s_d/8}$ is $1$ at $\lambda=0$, so
$\Phi(0)=1+\sum_{d\ge1}2^{1-N_d}$, which is the window identity
$W_D(A)=\Gamma(A)+(2D+1)2^{-k}$ of Part~I --- already formally verified.
Numerically, $\Phi(0)-\Gamma(A)$ is $0$, $-4.7\cdot10^{-14}$ and $-2.7\cdot10^{-15}$ for the
three sequences of Table~\ref{tab:one}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[each layer is a smaller ensemble]\label{rem:weight}
The factor $e^{-\lambda^2 s_d/8}$ is not a fudge: it is what remains when the Gaussian
normalisations of $B_d$ and of $A$ fail to cancel, because $V_d=V-s_d/4$. Expanding to second
order gives the coefficient of $\lambda^2$ as $\delta_d^2-s_d/4$ rather than $\delta_d^2$.
In the notation of \S\ref{sec:notation}, layer $d$ is the ensemble $B_d$ with the small
elements removed, so its variance is $V_d=V-s_d/4$ rather than $V$; the weight
$e^{-\lambda^{2}s_d/8}$ is exactly the price of that difference, and
Remark~\ref{rem:mushift} derives it from the ratio of partition functions rather than
fitting it. The correction is termwise smaller than the term it corrects,
$s_d/4\le d\sigma_d/2<\delta_d^{2}$, so every term of the series stays positive and the
correction never overshoots.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The bridge to Part~II}\label{sec:bridge}

Both theorems reduce to the same five parts. \textbf{P1} is the exact combinatorial
stratification, proved for every target in Part~I \STATUS{Lean-verified}; \textbf{P3} is a
local limit theorem for the tilted ensemble of Definition~\ref{def:tilt}; \textbf{P4} is the
assembly, in which the odd orders in $\lambda$ cancel by the reflection symmetry
$\lm(n)=\lm(T-n)$ \STATUS{Lean-verified}; \textbf{P5} is the error budget, which converges
exactly under~(H). Only \textbf{P2} is new: the minor-arc estimates of Part~II~\cite{P2} are proved for
the \emph{uniform} measure, and P3 needs them for the tilted one.

Write $\widetilde G_B(\theta)=\prod_{a\in B}\bigl(1-p_a+p_ae(a\theta)\bigr)$ for the tilted
generating function and $S_B(\theta)=\sum_{a\in B}e(a\theta)$ for the linear exponential sum.

\begin{lemma}[the bridge]\label{lem:kappa}
Put $t_a=4p_a(1-p_a)$ and $t_{\min}=\min_{a\in B}t_a$. For every $\theta$,
\[
  \bigl|\widetilde G_B(\theta)\bigr|^{2}
  =\prod_{a\in B}\Bigl(1-t_a\sin^{2}(\pi a\theta)\Bigr)
  \;\le\;\exp\Bigl(-t_{\min}\!\!\sum_{a\in B}\!\sin^{2}(\pi a\theta)\Bigr)
  \;=\;\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{t_{\min}}{2}\bigl(b-\operatorname{Re}S_B(\theta)\bigr)\Bigr).
\]
Consequently, if $\operatorname{Re}S_B(\theta)\le\eta\,b$ then
$|\widetilde G_B(\theta)|\le\exp\bigl(-t_{\min}(1-\eta)b/4\bigr)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The identity $|1-p+pe(\phi)|^{2}=1-4p(1-p)\sin^{2}(\pi\phi)$ is a computation;
$t_a\ge t_{\min}$ and $1-u\le e^{-u}$ give the middle step; and
$\sum_a\sin^{2}(\pi a\theta)=\tfrac12\bigl(b-\operatorname{Re}S_B(\theta)\bigr)$ is the
double-angle formula summed.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[why the bound is additive, and a bound that is not available]\label{rem:noKappa}
It is tempting to transfer multiplicatively, as $|\widetilde G_B(\theta)|\le|G_B(\theta)|^{\kappa}$
with $G_B$ the untilted product, since that would carry every exponent of Part~II across at
once. \textbf{No such $\kappa>0$ exists.} At any $\theta$ with $a\theta\in\tfrac12+\mathbb Z$ for
some $a\in B$ the untilted factor $\cos(\pi a\theta)$ vanishes, so the right-hand side is $0$,
while the tilted factor equals $\sqrt{1-t_a}>0$. Equivalently
$\log(1-ty)/\log(1-y)\to0$ as $y\to1$. The underlying inequality
$1-ty\le(1-y)^{t}$ is false for every $t\in(0,1)$ --- $s\mapsto(1-y)^{s}$ is convex and
therefore lies \emph{below} its chord $1-sy$, with deficit exactly $-(1-t)$ at $y=1$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:kappa} avoids the issue by never dividing by a quantity that vanishes.
\STATUS{refuted numerically on a $40\,575$-point grid and structurally; \texttt{kappa\_r088}}
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[the minor-arc input]\label{lem:eta}
\STATUS{(a) proved, Lemma~\ref{lem:etaa}; (b) proved, Lemma~\ref{lem:etab}; (c) quoted from Part~II}
Let $A=P_k$ and $N=a_k$. Then
\[
  \operatorname{Re}S_A(\theta)\;\le\;\Bigl(\tfrac12+o(1)\Bigr)b
  \qquad\text{uniformly for }\tfrac1{2N}\le\theta\le1-\tfrac1{2N}.
\]
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] \emph{$q\in\{1,2\}$.} Lemma~\ref{lem:etaa} below.
\item[(b)] \emph{$3\le q\le(\log N)^{A_0}$.} Lemma~\ref{lem:etab} below.
\item[(c)] \emph{$q>(\log N)^{A_0}$.} This is the case $n=1$ of Step 2 of
  \Xlab{paper2}{prop:deepminor} in Part~II, which gives
  $|S_A(\theta)|\ll N(\log N)^{3-A_0/2}+N^{4/5}(\log N)^{3}$, i.e.\
  $\operatorname{Re}S_A/b\ll(\log N)^{4-A_0/2}=o(1)$ for $A_0>8$.
  \STATUS{quoted from Part~II, verbatim}
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[the two degenerate denominators]\label{lem:etaa}
Let $q\in\{1,2\}$, $\theta=a/q+\beta$ with $\|\beta\|\ge1/(2N)$. Then
$\operatorname{Re}S_A(\theta)\le\tfrac12b$ for all large $N$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $S_A(\theta)=\sum_{p\le N}e(pa/q)e(p\beta)$. For $q=1$ the first factor is $1$; for
$q=2$ it is $(-1)^{p}=-1$ on every odd prime, so $S_A=-\sum_{p\le N}e(p\beta)$ and it suffices
in both cases to bound $|\sum_{p\le N}e(p\beta)|$. Partial summation against
$\pi(t)=\operatorname{li}(t)+O(te^{-c\sqrt{\log t}})$ gives
\[
  \sum_{p\le N}e(p\beta)=\int_2^{N}e(t\beta)\frac{dt}{\log t}
    +O\bigl(Ne^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}(1+|\beta|N)\bigr),
\]
and integrating by parts once more, $\bigl|\int_2^{N}e(t\beta)\,dt/\log t\bigr|
\ll1/(|\beta|\log N)$. With $\|\beta\|\ge1/(2N)$ this is $\ll N/\log N\asymp b$ with an
absolute implied constant, and the constant is $\le\tfrac12$ once $N\|\beta\|$ exceeds an
absolute threshold; below that threshold, i.e. for $\|\beta\|\asymp1/N$, the integral is
$(1+o(1))b\cdot\widehat{\chi}(\beta N)$ with $\widehat\chi$ the Fourier transform of the
indicator of $[0,1]$, whose real part is $\le\tfrac12$ off the principal arc. For $q=2$ the
sign is reversed, so $\operatorname{Re}S_A\le0$ near $\theta=\tfrac12$ --- the helpful
direction. No Siegel--Walfisz is used.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[the small-$q$ ring]\label{lem:etab}
Let $3\le q\le(\log N)^{A_0}$, $(a,q)=1$, and $\theta=a/q+\beta$ with
$|\beta|\le1/(q\tau)$, $\tau=N(\log N)^{-A_0}$. Then
\[
  S_A(\theta)\;=\;\frac{\mu(q)}{\varphi(q)}\,W(\beta)\;+\;O\!\bigl(N e^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}\bigr),
  \qquad W(\beta)=\int_2^{N}e(t\beta)\,\frac{dt}{\log t},
\]
and $|W(\beta)|\le\operatorname{li}(N)=(1+o(1))b$. Consequently
$\operatorname{Re}S_A(\theta)\le\bigl(\tfrac12+o(1)\bigr)b$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Sort the primes $p\le N$ by their residue class. The $\omega(q)=O(\log q)$ primes dividing $q$
contribute $O(\log q)=o(b)$, so
\[
  S_A(\theta)\;=\;\sum_{\substack{r\bmod q\\(r,q)=1}}e\!\Bigl(\frac{ra}{q}\Bigr)
    \sum_{\substack{p\le N\\p\equiv r\,(q)}}\!\!e(p\beta)\;+\;O(\log q).
\]
By Siegel--Walfisz, $\pi(t;q,r)=\operatorname{li}(t)/\varphi(q)+O(te^{-c\sqrt{\log t}})$
uniformly for $q\le(\log N)^{A_0}$; partial summation against $e(t\beta)$ turns this into
\[
  \sum_{\substack{p\le N\\p\equiv r\,(q)}}\!\!e(p\beta)
  \;=\;\frac{W(\beta)}{\varphi(q)}
     \;+\;O\!\bigl(Ne^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}(1+|\beta|N)\bigr),
\]
and $|\beta|N\le(\log N)^{A_0}/q$, so the error is $O(Ne^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}(\log N)^{A_0})
=O(Ne^{-c'\sqrt{\log N}})=o(b)$: a fixed power of $\log N$ cannot compete with
$e^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}$. Summing over $r$ and using the Ramanujan sum
$\sum_{(r,q)=1}e(ra/q)=c_q(a)=\mu(q)$ for $(a,q)=1$ gives the display. Finally
$\mu(q)/\varphi(q)$ is real and $\varphi(q)\ge2$ for $q\ge3$, so
$\operatorname{Re}S_A(\theta)\le\bigl(|\mu(q)|/\varphi(q)\bigr)b(1+o(1))\le
\bigl(\tfrac12+o(1)\bigr)b$; Lemma~\ref{lem:six} says where the $\tfrac12$ is attained.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[where the ineffectivity is]\label{rem:whereC1}
The Siegel--Walfisz step in Lemma~\ref{lem:etab} is the only inexplicit estimate in
\S\ref{sec:bridge} --- Lemmas~\ref{lem:kappa}, \ref{lem:etaa}, \ref{lem:six} and the Vaughan
quotation in Lemma~\ref{lem:eta}(c) are all effective --- and it is the same constant that
Part~II isolates as $C_1$; see Remark~\ref{rem:oneconstant}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[what would make $C_1$ effective]\label{rem:grh}
Two escapes are worth recording, because $C_1$ is the only inexplicit quantity in the
trilogy. \emph{Under GRH}, the Siegel--Walfisz input of Lemma~\ref{lem:etab} may be replaced
by $\pi(t;q,r)=\mathrm{li}(t)/\varphi(q)+O\bigl(t^{1/2}\log t\bigr)$ with an absolute implied
constant, uniformly for $q\le t^{1/2}$. The range Lemma~\ref{lem:etab} actually uses,
$q\le(\log N)^{A_0}$, is far inside that, so on GRH the constant becomes effective and every
constant in the trilogy is explicit. \emph{Unconditionally}, the ineffectivity is confined to
the possible existence of a Siegel zero for a single modulus $q\le(\log N)^{A_0}$: for every
modulus with an explicit zero-free region the estimate is already effective. The inexplicit
constant is therefore not a feature of the argument but a hostage to one hypothetical zero,
and the range it is held over is only $(\log N)^{A_0}$, not $N$. Neither escape is used
below; the theorems are stated unconditionally.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[the middle range, and why no constant enters]\label{rem:principal}
Inside $|\theta|<1/(2N)$ Lemma~\ref{lem:eta} says nothing, and it does not have to: there
$|a\theta|\le\tfrac12$ for every $a\in A$, so $\sin^{2}(\pi a\theta)\ge4a^{2}\theta^{2}$ and
Lemma~\ref{lem:kappa} gives the quadratic bound
$|\widetilde G_{B}(\theta)|\le\exp(-4t_{\min}V_d\theta^{2})$ directly. Cutting at the
LCLT-natural radius $\theta_0=(\log k)/\sqrt{K''_d}$ of \S\ref{sec:p3}, this is
$\le\exp(-t_{\min}(\log k)^{2})$ on the whole of $\theta_0\le|\theta|\le1/(2N)$ ---
superpolynomially below the density scale $(K''_d)^{-1/2}$, with no free constant anywhere.
The crossover at $\theta=1/(2N)$ survives as the boundary against
Lemma~\ref{lem:eta}, forced by $|a\theta|\le\tfrac12$ rather than chosen; measured, the
quadratic bound has already reached $e^{-0.01b}$ by $\theta N=0.18$--$0.42$ across three
profiles. \STATUS{experimentally confirmed; \texttt{gap\_r090}}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[what part (c) does \emph{not} need]\label{rem:nocover}
Part~II must control every harmonic $n\theta$ with $n\le N_0$, and pays for it with a covering
lemma and the parameter $Q_2=(\log N)^{A_0+A_0'}$. Here only $n=1$ occurs, so the dichotomy in
Lemma~\ref{lem:eta} is on the single denominator $q_1(\theta)$ and no covering lemma is needed.
The quotation in (c) is therefore valid on a strictly larger set of $\theta$ than Part~II's
type~(ii).
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[the extremiser of the additive estimate]\label{lem:six}
$\displaystyle\max_{q\ge3}\frac{\mu(q)}{\varphi(q)}=\frac12$, and the maximum is attained at
$q=6$ and at no other $q$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\varphi(q)=2$ holds exactly for $q\in\{3,4,6\}$, where $\mu$ takes the values $-1$, $0$, $+1$;
so among these the maximum is $\mu(6)/\varphi(6)=1/2$. Every other $q\ge3$ has $\varphi(q)\ge4$
and hence $\mu(q)/\varphi(q)\le1/4<1/2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[the same $q=6$, twice]\label{rem:sixagain}

\subsection{The tilted local limit theorem, and the assembly}\label{sec:p3}

Write $K''_d=\sum_{a\in B_d}a^{2}p_a(1-p_a)$ and $\mu_d(s)=\sum_{a\in B_d}a\,p_a$ for the
variance and mean of $B_d$ under $P_s$, and $K^{(j)}_d$ for the higher cumulants.

\begin{proposition}[tilted LCLT, uniform over the window]\label{prop:tiltlclt}
\STATUS{R2 and R3 proved (they quote Lemmas~\ref{lem:kappa} and~\ref{lem:eta}); R1 is
Problem~\ref{prob:R1} of \S\ref{sec:missing}, the classical local limit computation with
$p_a$-weights, written here to the level of its cumulant budget. Conditional on that problem
and on nothing else.}
For every layer $d\le D(k)$ and every $m$ with $|m-\mu_d(s)|\le W(k)$,
\[
  P_s\bigl[\textstyle\sum_{B_d}=m\bigr]
  =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi K''_d}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(m-\mu_d)^{2}}{2K''_d}\Bigr)\bigl(1+O(\varepsilon)\bigr),
  \quad
  \varepsilon=\frac{|K'''_d|}{(K''_d)^{3/2}}+\frac{K^{(4)}_d}{(K''_d)^{2}}
     +\frac{W(k)\,|K'''_d|}{(K''_d)^{2}},
\]
uniformly in $d$ and $m$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Invert $\widetilde G_{B_d}$ over $[-\pi,\pi]$ and split at
$\theta_0=(\log k)/\sqrt{K''_d}$ and at $1/(2N)$.

\emph{R1, $|\theta|\le\theta_0$.} Expand
$\log\widetilde G_{B_d}(\theta)=i\mu_d\theta-\tfrac12K''_d\theta^{2}
-\tfrac{i}{6}K'''_d\theta^{3}+\tfrac1{24}K^{(4)}_d\theta^{4}+O(K^{(5)}_d\theta^{5})$.
Since $|K^{(j)}_d|\le\sum_{a}a^{j}$, on $|\theta|\le\theta_0$ every term beyond the quadratic
is $o(1)$ and the standard Edgeworth argument gives the display with the stated $\varepsilon$;
the third term contributes the $W|K'''|/(K'')^{2}$ piece over a window of width $W$.

\emph{R2, $\theta_0\le|\theta|\le1/(2N)$.} Remark~\ref{rem:principal}: the contribution is
$\le\exp(-t_{\min}(\log k)^{2})$, superpolynomially below $(K''_d)^{-1/2}$.

\emph{R3, $|\theta|\ge1/(2N)$.} Lemmas~\ref{lem:kappa} and~\ref{lem:eta}:
$|\widetilde G_{B_d}|\le\exp(-t_{\min}(1-\eta)b/4)$, exponentially small, against measure
$O(1)$.

Uniformity in $d$: $B_d$ differs from $A$ in $N_d\le N_{D(k)}=(\log k)^{O(1)}$ elements of size
$\le2D(k)$, so every cumulant shifts by a relative $O(s_{D}/S_2)\to0$ and one $\varepsilon$
serves all layers. The lattice is of step $1$ and the parity weighting is quoted from Part~II.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[the $\mu_d$-shift, and why the layer term is what it is]\label{rem:mushift}
This is the step that produces $\Phi$, so it is worth doing once in full. Since
$\sum_{a\in A}a\,p_a=n$ and $p_a=\tfrac12-\tfrac{sa}{4}+O((sa)^{3})$ for the small elements,
\[
  \mu_d(s)\;=\;n-\!\!\sum_{a\le 2d}\!\!a\,p_a\;=\;n-\frac{\sigma_d}{2}+w+\dots,
  \qquad w:=\frac{s\,s_d}{4},
\]
so the two evaluation points of the stratification sit at
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mushift}
  (n+d)-\mu_d=+\delta_d-w,
  \qquad
  (n-d-\sigma_d)-\mu_d=-\delta_d-w .
\end{equation}
Feeding \eqref{eq:mushift} into Proposition~\ref{prop:tiltlclt}, undoing the tilt through
$r_{B_d}(m)=P_s[\sum_{B_d}=m]\,Z_{B_d}(s)\,e^{sm}$, and using
$sd+s\sigma_d/2=s\delta_d$ to pair the two points:
\[
  r_{B_d}(n+d)+r_{B_d}(n-d-\sigma_d)
  =2\,Z_{B_d}e^{sn-s\sigma_d/2}\,
   \frac{e^{-(\delta_d^{2}+w^{2})/2K''_d}}{\sqrt{2\pi K''_d}}
   \cosh\Bigl(\delta_d\Bigl(s+\frac{w}{K''_d}\Bigr)\Bigr).
\]
Dividing by $r_A(n)\sim Z_Ae^{sn}/\sqrt{2\pi K''}$ and using
$Z_{B_d}e^{-s\sigma_d/2}/Z_A=2^{-N_d}\prod_{a\le2d}\cosh(sa/2)^{-1}
=2^{-N_d}e^{-s^{2}s_d/8}(1+\dots)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:layerterm}
  \frac{r_{B_d}(n+d)+r_{B_d}(n-d-\sigma_d)}{r_A(n)}
  =2^{1-N_d}\,e^{-\lambda^{2}s_d/8}\,\cosh(\lambda\delta_d)\cdot
   e^{-\delta_d^{2}/2K''_d}\,\bigl(1+O(\varepsilon)\bigr),
\end{equation}
with $\lambda=s+w/K''_d=s\bigl(1+s_d/(4K''_d)\bigr)$ --- which is exactly the per-layer slope
drift of $1+O(s_d/S_2)$ measured earlier. \textbf{The first three factors of
\eqref{eq:layerterm} are the general term of $\Phi$ in \eqref{eq:phi}}; the odd orders in
$\lambda$ cancel because the two points are $\pm\delta_d$ about the same centre, which is the
reflection symmetry $\lm(n)=\lm(T-n)$ in analytic dress. \STATUS{Lean-verified (the symmetry)}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[the one factor $\Phi$ omits]\label{rem:cd}

$E_k$ and not to the transfer function. Measured at $k=180$: the ratio of the left side of

\begin{remark}[why the constants cannot be imported]\label{rem:closing}
What Proposition~\ref{prop:tiltlclt} leaves open is not a mechanism but a list of explicit
Edgeworth constants in region R1, so it is natural to ask whether they can be quoted rather
than derived. The natural source is the Bernoulli-part-extraction literature, which produces
local limit theorems with explicit universal constants for non-identically distributed lattice
summands, and $\sigma(S)=\sum a_iX_i$ is a sum of independent lattice summands. We checked,
and the answer is no, for two reasons; we record them because a reader will otherwise ask the
same question.

Write, following Giuliano and Weber \cite{GW},
$\vartheta_X=\sum_k\mathbb{P}\{X=k\}\wedge\mathbb{P}\{X=k+1\}$ and
$\Theta_n=\sum_j\vartheta_{X_j}$; their local limit theorem carries the error
$C\Theta_n^{-1/2}(H_n+\Theta_n^{-1})$ plus a term
$(\log\Theta_n/(\operatorname{Var}\cdot\Theta_n))^{1/2}$, with $H_n$ a Berry--Esseen distance,
and is useful exactly when
$(\operatorname{Var}/\Theta_n)^{1/2}(H_n+\Theta_n^{-1})\to0$.

\emph{First, the hypothesis is void here.} Our summands are $X_j=a_jX_j'$ with $a_j$ odd and
at least $3$, so the law of $X_j$ charges the two integers $0$ and $a_j$ and no two
\emph{adjacent} ones. Hence $\vartheta_{X_j}=0$ exactly, for every $j$ and every $p$, so
$\Theta_n=0$ and the bound reads $\le\infty$. Their own remark names this degenerate case.

\emph{Second, the obvious repair does not help.} One can block the sequence and apply the
method to block sums, whose laws do charge adjacent integers --- but only once a block is
large enough to have more subset sums than its span, and for blocks of \emph{large} elements
that takes a long time: $\{7,9,11\}$ has subset sums $\{0,7,9,11,16,18,20,27\}$, no two
adjacent. Measured, the smallest final block with $\vartheta>0$ has size $7,11,15$ and more
than $20$ at $k=16,32,64,128$, growing like $\log k$, so blocking buys $\Theta_n\lesssim
k/\log k$. And then the applicability condition fails outright: our weights grow, so
$\operatorname{Var}\asymp S_2\asymp k^{3}$ while $\Theta_n\lesssim k$ and $H_n\asymp
k^{-1/2}$, giving $(\operatorname{Var}/\Theta_n)^{1/2}(H_n+\Theta_n^{-1})\asymp k^{1/2}$.
Measured, that quantity runs $48.9,101.4,222.5,495.1$ for the odd numbers and
$94.1,195.1,441.0,1034.2$ for the primes at $k=16,32,64,128$ --- increasing, not tending to
zero. \STATUS{experimentally confirmed; \texttt{bpart\_r110}}

The obstruction is the same feature that makes the present local limit theorem worth proving:
the summands carry weights $a_j\asymp j$, so the variance grows like the cube of the number of
summands rather than linearly, and a method calibrated to the latter has nothing to give.
\emph{The Edgeworth constants of R1 have to be derived, not imported.}
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[the $\alpha=1$ threshold]\label{prop:alphalb}
Let $A=A_k$ consist of $k$ distinct odd integers with $a_i\asymp c\,i^{\alpha}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] If $\alpha<1$ then \textup{(H)} fails: $\sum_{d\ge1}2^{-N_d}\delta_d^{2}\to\infty$.
\item[(ii)] If $\alpha\ge1$ then \textup{(H)} holds, with a bound depending on $\alpha$ and $c$
      alone.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) The $a_i$ are distinct odd integers, so $a_k\ge2k-1$; with $a_k\asymp ck^{\alpha}$ this
forces $c\gg k^{1-\alpha}$, so $a_1\asymp c\to\infty$. For $d<a_1/2$ no $a_i$ is $\le2d$, hence
$N_d=0$, $\sigma_d=0$, $\delta_d=d$, and
\[
  \sum_{d\ge1}2^{-N_d}\delta_d^{2}\;\ge\;\sum_{d<a_1/2}2d^{2}\;\asymp\;a_1^{3}
  \;\asymp\;k^{3(1-\alpha)}\;\longrightarrow\;\infty .
\]
(ii) For $\alpha\ge1$ one has $a_1=O(1)$ and $N_d=\#\{i:a_i\le2d\}\gg d^{1/\alpha}$, while
$\delta_d=d+\sigma_d/2\ll d^{1+1/\alpha}$, so the general term is
$\ll2^{-c'd^{1/\alpha}}d^{2+2/\alpha}$, which is summable: an exponential in a positive power
of $d$ beats a polynomial. The bound involves $\alpha$ and $c$ and not $k$.
\end{proof}

\section{The biased invariant}\label{sec:biased}

Let $P_q$ be the product measure under which each $a_i\in A$ is present independently with
probability $q\in(0,1)$, and write $\lm_q(n)$, $r_q(n)$ for the $P_q$-measures of the sets
of strict local minima and of ground states at the target $n$. The target is taken at the
$q$-tilted centre $n_q=\lfloor qT\rceil$.

\begin{definition}[biased gap series]\label{def:gq}
\[
  \Gamma^{(q)}(A)\;=\;1+\sum_{d\ge1}\Bigl[q^{N_d}+(1-q)^{N_d}\Bigr].
\]
\end{definition}

At $q=\tfrac12$ every bracket is $2\cdot2^{-N_d}$ and $\Gamma^{(1/2)}$ is the window series
$W_D$ of Part~I~\cite{P1}, hence $\Gamma(A)+(2D+1)2^{-k}$.

\begin{remark}[why the classification survives the deformation]\label{rem:measurefree}
Part~I's classification is measure-free: at error $d$, an overshooting configuration is a
strict local minimum exactly when every $a\le2d$ is \emph{excluded}, and an undershooting one
exactly when every $a\le2d$ is \emph{included}. Under $P_q$ those two events have probability
$(1-q)^{N_d}$ and $q^{N_d}$, which is Definition~\ref{def:gq}. Writing
$r^{q}_B(m)=\sum_{U\subseteq B,\ \sigma(U)=m}q^{|U|}(1-q)^{|B|-|U|}$ and factorising $P_q$
across $I_d$ and $B_d$,
\[
  \mathrm{over}_q=(1-q)^{N_d}r^{q}_{B_d}(n+d),\quad
  \mathrm{under}_q=q^{N_d}r^{q}_{B_d}(n-d-\sigma_d),\quad
  r_q=\!\!\sum_{J\subseteq I_d}\!\!q^{|J|}(1-q)^{N_d-|J|}r^{q}_{B_d}(n-\sigma(J)),
\]
and the binomial sum over $J$ is exactly $1$, so a flat $r^{q}$ gives the $d$-th term of
Definition~\ref{def:gq}. \STATUS{derived; the assignment verified per stratum, see
Remark~\ref{rem:assign}}
\end{remark}

\subsection{The fair coin is the flattest}

\begin{lemma}[termwise minimality]\label{lem:termwise}
For every integer $N\ge0$ the function $f_N(q)=q^{N}+(1-q)^{N}$ on $(0,1)$ is symmetric about
$q=\tfrac12$ and convex, and $f_N(q)\ge f_N(\tfrac12)=2^{1-N}$, with strict inequality for
$q\ne\tfrac12$ as soon as $N\ge2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$f_N(1-q)=f_N(q)$ is immediate. For $N\ge2$,
$f_N'(q)=N\bigl[q^{N-1}-(1-q)^{N-1}\bigr]$ and
$f_N''(q)=N(N-1)\bigl[q^{N-2}+(1-q)^{N-2}\bigr]>0$, so $f_N$ is strictly convex; and
$t\mapsto t^{N-1}$ is strictly increasing, so $f_N'<0$ on $(0,\tfrac12)$ and $f_N'>0$ on
$(\tfrac12,1)$. For $N\in\{0,1\}$, $f_N$ is the constant $2$ or $1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[formally verified]\label{rem:lean22}
Lemma~\ref{lem:termwise} is machine-checked. \Lean{termwise\_min} gives the inequality for
every $N$ and every $q\in[0,1]$, \Lean{termwise\_min\_strict} its strictness for $N\ge2$ and
$q\ne\tfrac12$, and \Lean{termwise\_min\_iff} the equality case as a biconditional; the
normalisation $2\cdot(\tfrac12)^N=2^{1-N}$ is \Lean{two\_mul\_half\_pow}. The non-strict
bound is proved twice, once from convexity of $x^N$ on $[0,\infty)$ and once from Bernoulli's
inequality (\Lean{termwise\_min'}), so the two proofs check the \emph{statement} against each
other and not merely the tactic script. No \texttt{sorry}, and no axioms beyond
\texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice}, \texttt{Quot.sound}.
\STATUS{Lean-verified; \texttt{Pnp/Theory/TermwiseMin.lean}}
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[the fair coin minimises the invariant]\label{thm:fair}
For every finite increasing sequence $A$ of odd positive integers with $|A|\ge2$ and every
$q\in(0,1)$,
\[
  \Gamma^{(q)}(A)\;\ge\;\Gamma^{(1/2)}(A),
\]
with equality if and only if $q=\tfrac12$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Sum Lemma~\ref{lem:termwise} over $d$. For strictness it suffices that some $d\le D$ has
$N_d\ge2$: take any $d\ge(a_2-1)/2$, which is at most $D=(a_k-1)/2$ because $a_2\le a_k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[what the theorem says]\label{rem:physics}
$q(1-q)$ is the per-element variance of the coin. Biasing the coin in either direction
lowers that variance and raises the invariant: \textbf{the more predictable the coin, the more
rugged the landscape}. The effect is exactly symmetric in $q\leftrightarrow1-q$, as it must
be, since complementing every configuration exchanges $q$ with $1-q$ and $n$ with $T-n$.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[closed form for the odd numbers]\label{cor:oddsclosed}
For $A=(1,3,\dots,2k-1)$ one has $N_d=d$ for $d\le k$, so in the limit $k\to\infty$
\[
  \Gamma^{(q)}=1+\frac{q}{1-q}+\frac{1-q}{q}=\frac{1}{q(1-q)}-1\;\ge\;3,
\]
with equality only at $q=\tfrac12$: \emph{the invariant of the odd numbers is the reciprocal
of the coin's variance, minus one}. \STATUS{verified against the summed series to
$5.3\cdot10^{-15}$ over $q=0.10,\dots,0.80$; \texttt{e5\_r098}}
\end{corollary}

\section{The biased transfer function}\label{sec:phiq}

\subsection{The sign of $\lambda$, which now matters}

The transfer-function strand's transfer function is even in $\lambda$, so the sign of the slope is not observable
there. Splitting the $\cosh$ makes it observable, so we fix it first:
\[
  \lambda_q(n)\;=\;+\frac{d}{dm}\log r^{q}_A(m)\Big|_{m=n}
  \;=\;\tfrac14\log\frac{r^{q}_A(n+2)}{r^{q}_A(n-2)}+O(\cdot),
\]
positive below the biased centre. This is the convention under which $\lambda\approx s$;
the transfer-function strand's \ref{def:slope} carried the opposite sign and has been corrected, with no
numerical consequence there for exactly the reason just given.

\subsection{The derivation}

Take $P_{q,s}$: element $a$ present with weight $q\,e^{-sa}$, absent with weight $1-q$, so
$p_a=q e^{-sa}/((1-q)+qe^{-sa})$ and $p_a=q-q(1-q)sa+O(s^2a^2)$. Two consequences, both
absent at $q=\tfrac12$:
\begin{itemize}
\item the per-element variance is $a^2p_a(1-p_a)=q(1-q)a^2(1+O(sa))$, so the layer weight of
      the transfer-function strand's \ref{rem:mushift} becomes $e^{-\lambda^2q(1-q)s_d/2}$;
\item removing $I_d$ removes mean $q\sigma_d$, so the two strata no longer sit at $\pm\delta_d$
      about a common centre. They sit at
      \[
        \delta^{+}_d=d+q\sigma_d,\qquad -\delta^{-}_d,\quad \delta^{-}_d=d+(1-q)\sigma_d .
      \]
      \textbf{The bias splits the pair asymmetrically, in the ratio $q:(1-q)$.}
\end{itemize}
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phiq}
  \Phi^{(q)}(\lambda)\;=\;1+\sum_{d\ge1}
  \Bigl[(1-q)^{N_d}e^{\lambda\delta^{+}_d}+q^{N_d}e^{-\lambda\delta^{-}_d}\Bigr]
  e^{-\lambda^{2}q(1-q)s_d/2}.
\end{equation}
\STATUS{derived; three internal checks below are exact, the point prediction is verified}

Three checks are built in, and all three hold to machine zero ($0.00\cdot10^{0}$, two
profiles, $k=60$): $\Phi^{(q)}(0)=\Gamma^{(q)}$ of Definition~\ref{def:gq}; the
complementation symmetry $\Phi^{(q)}(\lambda)=\Phi^{(1-q)}(-\lambda)$, which holds because
$\delta^{+}(q)=\delta^{-}(1-q)$; and $\Phi^{(1/2)}$ is the transfer-function strand's $\Phi$ term by term.
\STATUS{experimentally confirmed; \texttt{e10\_r105}}

\subsection{Tilted rigidity}

\begin{proposition}[the biased landscape responds at first order]\label{prop:tilted}
$\displaystyle \Phi^{(q)\prime}(0)=\sum_{d\ge1}\Bigl[(1-q)^{N_d}\delta^{+}_d-q^{N_d}\delta^{-}_d\Bigr]$,
which vanishes identically at $q=\tfrac12$ and is non-zero otherwise. So rigidity at a biased
measure is not flatness: it is a \emph{tilt} of a definite slope, and the slope is a point
prediction with no free parameter.
\end{proposition}

\STATUS{derived and verified, not proved from a hypothesis-free statement: it is a consequence
of $\Phi^{(q)}$, itself derived by the partition-function argument of \S\ref{sec:phiq} and
confirmed by three exact internal checks. The first-order coefficient is a point prediction
with no free parameter, and \S\ref{sec:e10} tests it against the naive alternative on $36$
discriminating rows --- which is why the two are distinguished here rather than only in
\S\ref{sec:scope}.}

This is what \S\ref{sec:e6} demanded of the derivation, and it is a sharper demand than it
looks. The naive guess --- keep the transfer-function strand's $\delta_d=d+\sigma_d/2$ and merely split the
weights --- gives instead $\sum[(1-q)^{N_d}-q^{N_d}]\delta_d$, and the two differ by exactly
$(\tfrac12-q)\sum_d\sigma_d[q^{N_d}+(1-q)^{N_d}]$: zero at $q=\tfrac12$, and $20$--$32\%$ of
the prediction at the $q$ tested. The measurement therefore decides between them.

\section{Random sequences, unconditionally}\label{sec:random}

Before the theorem, the floor it has to stand on. Let $A$ be drawn from the Cram\'er model on

instance to instance. That is deliberate: $kR_A(k)$ is the $k$-invariant quantity of the transfer-function strand's

There is one place where the random model is \emph{not} like the primes, and it is the place
that matters for the minor arcs. Write

Since $|\cos(\pi a/4)|=1/\sqrt2$ for \emph{every} odd $a$, the modulus-$4$ floor

primes contains exactly one such element, namely $3$, and $3$ leaves $B_d$ at $d\ge2$, so the
primes keep $h_d(6)=\sqrt3/2$; a random odd sequence contains about $|B_d|/3$ of them and
loses the peak at every $d$. Measured over $q\le120$ at $d=1,2,5$: every random instance has
$\max_{q\ge3}h_d(q)=1/\sqrt2$ attained at $q=4$, and the primes have $\sqrt3/2$ at $q=6$.

\noindent
So the random theorem should come out with the geometric rate $1/\sqrt2=0.7071$ per element
rather than the $\sqrt3/2=0.8660$ of Parts~I and~II~\cite{P1,P2}: \emph{the primes are the harder case, and
the modulus-$6$ ripple is a structural feature of the primes that the random model destroys.}

\subsection{The modulus-4 theorem}\label{sec:modfour}

The observation above is not an accident of the ensemble; it is a theorem about residues, and
it is the exact counterpart of Part~II's modulus-$6$ theorem for a different equidistribution
class. Part~II averages $|\cos|$ over the \emph{reduced} residues, which is the right class
for the primes. A random odd sequence is equidistributed over the \emph{odd} residues
instead, so the relevant quantity is a different one.

\begin{definition}\label{def:modd}
For $q\ge2$ let $R_q=\mathbb{Z}/q$ if $q$ is odd and $R_q=\{j:\ j\text{ odd}\}\subset
\mathbb{Z}/q$ if $q$ is even --- for $q$ even and $m$ odd, $m\bmod q$ is odd, so $R_q$ is the
set of residues an odd sequence can occupy. Put
\[
   M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)\;=\;\Bigl(\prod_{j\in R_q}\bigl|\cos(\pi j/q)\bigr|\Bigr)^{1/|R_q|}.
\]
For $r$ coprime to $q$ the map $j\mapsto jr$ permutes $R_q$, so $M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)$ does not
depend on the numerator of the rational $r/q$.
\end{definition}

Everything below is a corollary of the coset identity of \S\ref{sec:coset}, because
$\{j/q:j\in R_q\}$ \emph{is} a coset of $(1/v)\mathbb{Z}$.

\begin{corollary}[the identity in the form \S\ref{sec:rate} uses]\label{cor:cosetRq}
Let $q\ge2$, put $v=q$ for $q$ odd and $v=q/2$ for $q$ even, and let $\sigma=\tfrac12$ if
$q\equiv2\pmod 4$ and $\sigma=0$ otherwise. Then $|R_q|=v$ and, for every real $s$,
\[
   \prod_{j\in R_q}\Bigl|\cos\pi\Bigl(s+\frac jq\Bigr)\Bigr|
   \;=\;2^{\,1-v}\,\bigl|\cos\pi(vs+\sigma)\bigr| ,\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
   \frac1v\sum_{j\in R_q}X\Bigl(s+\frac jq\Bigr)\;\ge\;\Bigl(1-\frac1v\Bigr)\log2 .
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$\{j/q:j\in R_q\}$ is $\{k/v\}_{k<v}$ for $q$ odd and $\{\tfrac1{2v}+\tfrac kv\}_{k<v}$ for $q$
even. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} at $t=s$ and at $t=s+\tfrac1{2v}$ respectively. In the second
case the shift contributes $v\cdot\tfrac1{2v}=\tfrac12$ to the argument, so the total offset is
$\tau_v+\tfrac12$, which is an integer when $v$ is even and $\tfrac12$ when $v$ is odd; and $v$
odd with $q$ even is exactly $q\equiv2\pmod4$. The inequality is
Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[closed form]\label{lem:moddclosed}
$M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)=2^{1/q-1}$ for $q$ odd. For $q=2u$ even,
$M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)=2^{1/u-1}$ if $u$ is even, and $M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)=0$ if $u$ is odd.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $s=0$ in Corollary~\ref{cor:cosetRq}: the right-hand side is $2^{1-v}$ when $\sigma=0$ and
$0$ when $\sigma=\tfrac12$. Take $v$-th roots.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[a second proof of the odd case, with no roots of unity]\label{rem:doubleangle}
For $q$ odd the closed form also follows from the double-angle formula alone, and this is the
route we formalise. Let $P=\prod_{j=1}^{q-1}|\sin(\pi j/q)|$ and $C=\prod_{j=1}^{q-1}|\cos(\pi
j/q)|$. From $\sin2x=2\sin x\cos x$, $\prod_{j=1}^{q-1}|\sin(2\pi j/q)|=2^{q-1}PC$. Since $q$ is
odd, $j\mapsto 2j\bmod q$ is a bijection of $\{1,\dots,q-1\}$ and
$|\sin(2\pi j/q)|=|\sin(\pi\,(2j\bmod q)/q)|$, so the left side is $P$ again. As $P\ne0$,
$C=2^{1-q}$, and $\prod_{j\in R_q}|\cos(\pi j/q)|=C$ because the $j=0$ factor is $1$. Two proofs
of one load-bearing identity check the \emph{statement}; one proof checks only the argument.
This is the branch that is machine-checked, as \Lean{prod\_abs\_cos\_odd}; see
Remark~\ref{rem:leanmodfour}.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[the modulus-4 theorem]\label{thm:modfour}
$\displaystyle\max_{q\ge2}M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)=\frac1{\sqrt2}$, attained at $q=4$ and nowhere
else.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$v\mapsto2^{1/v-1}$ is strictly decreasing. For $q$ odd, $q\ge3$, Lemma~\ref{lem:moddclosed}
gives $M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)=2^{1/q-1}\le2^{-2/3}$. For $q\equiv2\pmod4$ it gives $0$. For
$q\equiv0\pmod4$, write $q=2u$ with $u$ even, $u\ge2$; then
$M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)=2^{1/u-1}\le2^{-1/2}$ with equality exactly when $u=2$, i.e.\ $q=4$. And
$2^{-2/3}<2^{-1/2}$.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:moddclosed} is verified against the product for all $q\le200$, worst discrepancy
$4.4\cdot10^{-16}$; Corollary~\ref{cor:cosetRq} is verified in its product form for $q\le120$ at
$400$ random shifts each, worst discrepancy $4.4\cdot10^{-16}$, and independently at $60$ decimal
digits, worst discrepancy $2.2\cdot10^{-61}$.
\STATUS{proved; and checked numerically, \texttt{mq4\_r107}, \texttt{stab\_r110}}

\begin{remark}[what is machine-checked here, and what is not]\label{rem:leanmodfour}
The two halves of the argument have different statuses and the difference is worth stating.
\emph{The maximisation is formally verified.} Taking the closed form of
Lemma~\ref{lem:moddclosed} as a definition, \Lean{ModdCF\_le} gives
$M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)\le2^{-1/2}$ for every $q\ge2$, \Lean{ModdCF\_eq\_iff} the equality case as
a biconditional, \Lean{ModdCF\_lt\_of\_ne\_four} the strict bound off $q=4$ by a second route,
and \Lean{two\_rpow\_neg\_half} the normalisation $2^{-1/2}=1/\sqrt2$. This is the step where
an error would hide --- a case split over three residue classes and the comparison of
$2^{-2/3}$ with $2^{-1/2}$ --- and it is now checked by machine.
\emph{Of the closed form itself, the odd branch is now formalised and the even branch is not.}
\Lean{prod\_abs\_cos\_odd} proves $\prod_{j=1}^{q-1}|\cos(\pi j/q)|=(1/2)^{q-1}$ for every odd
$q$, by the double-angle route of Remark~\ref{rem:doubleangle} --- which is exactly why that
route is worth having: it uses one bijection and one trigonometric identity, where the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} needs a product over roots of unity that Mathlib does not carry. The even
branch, and Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} in general, remain proved on paper and checked numerically.

\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[which residue classes the distribution relation reaches]\label{prop:twopower}
\STATUS{proved; two lines. Verified for $q\le32$ by direct enumeration,
\texttt{tool\_coset\_r138}.}
$(\mathbb{Z}/q)^{*}$ is an additive coset of a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}/q$ if and only if $q$
is a power of $2$, in which case it is the set of odd residues.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $(\mathbb{Z}/q)^{*}=r+m\mathbb{Z}/q$. Every element of that set is $\equiv r\pmod m$;
both $1$ and $-1$ are units, so $1\equiv-1\pmod m$ and $m\mid2$. If $m=1$ every residue is a
unit, so $q=1$. If $m=2$ every odd residue is a unit, so $q$ has no odd prime factor.
Conversely, for $q=2^{k}$ the units are exactly the odd residues.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[the reduced residues, evaluated]\label{prop:redresidue}
\STATUS{proved; the proof is below and uses only Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} and M\"obius inversion.
\textbf{Awaiting independent verification}, per the convention of this project.
Checked against direct enumeration on $26$ moduli to $10^{-12}$, with two controls that fire;
\texttt{leadc\_r143}.}
Write $v=2^{j}w$ with $w$ odd, and suppose $v\not\equiv2\pmod4$. Then
\[
  \frac1{\varphi(v)}\sum_{r\in(\mathbb{Z}/v)^{*}}X\Bigl(\frac rv\Bigr)
  \;=\;\log2\cdot\Bigl(1-\frac{2\,[w=1]}{2^{j}\varphi(w)}\Bigr)
  \;=\;\begin{cases}
    \bigl(1-2^{1-j}\bigr)\log2, & v=2^{j}\ (j\ge2),\\[2pt]
    \log2 \text{ exactly}, & \text{otherwise, including every odd }v>1.
  \end{cases}
\]
For $v\equiv2\pmod4$ the residue $w$ is a unit only when $w=1$, and $X(w/v)=X(1/2)=+\infty$: the
product $\prod(1+e(a\theta))$ vanishes there rather than being merely small.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $t=0$ in Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} with $v=n$ odd: $\tau_n=0$ and $X(0)=0$, so
$G(n):=\sum_{k\bmod n}X(k/n)=(n-1)\log2$.

For $n$ even the odd residues mod $n$ are the coset $\tfrac1n+\tfrac2n\mathbb{Z}/n$, i.e.\ a coset
of the subgroup of order $n/2$; applying Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} with $v=n/2$ and $t=1/n$ gives
\[
  O(n):=\sum_{\substack{s<n\\ s\ \mathrm{odd}}}X\Bigl(\frac sn\Bigr)
  =\frac n2\Bigl[\Bigl(1-\frac2n\Bigr)\log2+\frac2n X\bigl(\tfrac12+\tau_{n/2}\bigr)\Bigr]
  =\frac{n-2}{2}\log2 \quad (4\mid n),
\]
since $n/2$ even makes $\tau_{n/2}=\tfrac12$ and $X(1)=0$; for $n\equiv2\pmod4$ one has
$\tau_{n/2}=0$ and $X(\tfrac12)=+\infty$, which is the excluded case.

Now let $j\ge2$. A residue is a unit mod $v$ iff it is odd and prime to $w$, and writing $r=ds$
with $d\mid w$ (so $d$ odd, and $r$ odd iff $s$ odd),
\[
  \sum_{r\in(\mathbb{Z}/v)^{*}}X\Bigl(\frac rv\Bigr)=\sum_{d\mid w}\mu(d)\,O\Bigl(\frac vd\Bigr)
  =\frac{\log2}{2}\Bigl[v\sum_{d\mid w}\frac{\mu(d)}{d}-2\sum_{d\mid w}\mu(d)\Bigr]
  =\frac{\log2}{2}\bigl[2^{j}\varphi(w)-2\,[w=1]\bigr],
\]
every term being finite because $4\mid v/d$. Dividing by $\varphi(v)=2^{j-1}\varphi(w)$ gives the
formula. For $j=0$ the same inversion run against $G$ instead of $O$ gives
$\sum_{r\in(\mathbb{Z}/v)^{*}}X(r/v)=\log2\,[\varphi(v)-[v=1]]$, hence exactly $\log2$ on average
for $v>1$. For $j=1$ the term $d=w$ is $O(2)=X(\tfrac12)=+\infty$ and the inversion is
$\infty-\infty$; that is not a technicality but the vanishing case again.\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[the character decomposition; the other components are $L$-values]\label{prop:chardecomp}
\STATUS{proved; the proof is three classical ingredients and a finite interchange.
\textbf{Awaiting independent verification.} Checked to $10^{-39}$ on the even primitive
characters of eight moduli, with the parity control and the vanishing control both firing;
\texttt{lval\_r145}.}
Let $f\ge3$ and let $\chi$ be a primitive Dirichlet character mod $f$, with Gauss sum
$\tau(\chi)=\sum_{a}\chi(a)e(a/f)$. Then
\[
  \sum_{a\in(\mathbb{Z}/f)^{*}}\overline{\chi}(a)\,X\Bigl(\frac af\Bigr)
  =\begin{cases}
     \varphi(f)\cdot\bigl(\text{the value of Proposition~\ref{prop:redresidue}}\bigr), & \chi=\chi_{0},\\[2pt]
     0, & \chi \text{ odd},\\[2pt]
     -\,\tau(\overline{\chi})\,\bigl(1-\chi(2)\bigr)\,L(1,\chi), & \chi \text{ even, }\chi\ne\chi_{0}.
   \end{cases}
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[the factor $1-\chi(2)$, explained, and the attribution that follows]\label{rem:doubleangle}
\STATUS{proved; $\log|2\sin2\pi t|=\log|2\sin\pi t|+\log|2\cos\pi t|$ is the double-angle formula.
Checked to $2\cdot10^{-15}$ at $2\cdot10^{5}$ points, \texttt{annealed\_r147}.}
Writing $T_2f(t)=f(2t)$, the double-angle formula says
$\log|2\cos\pi t|=(T_2-1)\log|2\sin\pi t|$, so $X=\log2-(T_2-1)\log|2\sin\pi t|$. On the
$\chi$-component $T_2$ acts by $\chi(2)$, since $a\mapsto2a$ permutes the units; hence the factor
$1-\chi(2)$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:chardecomp} \emph{is} the eigenvalue of $1-T_2$, and nothing
else. \textbf{The proposition is therefore Dirichlet's classical evaluation of
$\sum_a\chi(a)\log|2\sin(\pi a/f)|$ transported by the double-angle formula}, exactly as
Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} is the classical distribution relation transported the same way. We claim
no part of the evaluation; what we record is where it lands. The proof below is written out
because it is short and because a reader should be able to see the transport rather than take it
on trust.
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}
$X$ is even, so pairing $a$ with $f-a$ kills every odd character. For $\chi$ even and
nontrivial, start from the Fourier series
$\log|2\cos\pi t|=\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^{n+1}\cos(2\pi nt)/n$, valid for $t\notin\tfrac12+\mathbb{Z}$,
which holds at every $a/f$ with $f\ge3$. The sum over $a$ is \emph{finite}, so it may be taken
inside term by term. For primitive $\chi$,
$\sum_{a}\overline{\chi}(a)e(na/f)=\chi(n)\tau(\overline{\chi})$, and its real part is the same
because $\chi$ is even. Hence, the $\log2$ in $X=-\log|2\cos\pi t|+\log2$ dying against
$\sum_a\overline{\chi}(a)=0$,
\[
  \sum_{a}\overline{\chi}(a)X\Bigl(\frac af\Bigr)
  =-\tau(\overline{\chi})\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}\chi(n)}{n}
  =-\tau(\overline{\chi})\bigl(1-\chi(2)\bigr)L(1,\chi),
\]
the last step being $\sum_n(-1)^{n+1}\chi(n)/n=L(1,\chi)-2\sum_{n\ \mathrm{even}}\chi(n)/n
=(1-\chi(2))L(1,\chi)$.\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[a vanishing sum at $p\equiv1\pmod 8$]\label{cor:vanish}
\STATUS{proved, as the case $\chi=\bigl(\tfrac{\cdot}{p}\bigr)$ of
Proposition~\ref{prop:chardecomp}. Verified to $10^{-40}$ for all $p<200$ with $p\equiv1\pmod4$;
the $p\equiv5\pmod8$ half is the control and is nonzero in every case.
\texttt{lval\_r145}.}
For every prime $p\equiv1\pmod 8$,
$\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\Bigl(\frac ap\Bigr)\log\bigl|\cos\tfrac{\pi a}{p}\bigr|=0$,
exactly. For $p\equiv5\pmod8$ the same sum is $-2\tau(\chi)L(1,\chi)\ne0$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $p\equiv1\pmod4$ the Legendre symbol is even, and it is $\pm1$-valued, so
$\chi=\overline\chi$. The factor $1-\chi(2)$ is $1-\bigl(\tfrac2p\bigr)$, which vanishes exactly
when $p\equiv\pm1\pmod8$; combined with $p\equiv1\pmod4$ that is $p\equiv1\pmod8$. For
$p\equiv5\pmod8$ one has $\bigl(\tfrac2p\bigr)=-1$ and the factor is $2$.\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[a class number formula for the coset energy]\label{cor:classnumber}
\STATUS{proved, as Proposition~\ref{prop:chardecomp} composed with Dirichlet's class number
formula. \textbf{Awaiting independent verification.} Verified for all eighteen primes
$p\equiv5\pmod8$ below $320$: the ratio is a positive integer in every case and equals the known
class number in every case, including $h(229)=3$. The fundamental unit is computed independently,
by solving $x^{2}-py^{2}=\pm4$, with no $L$-function anywhere in that half of the check.
\texttt{class\_r146}.}
Let $p\equiv1\pmod4$ be prime, let $h(p)$ be the class number of the real quadratic field
$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt p)$ and $\varepsilon_p>1$ its fundamental unit. Then
\[
  \sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\Bigl(\frac ap\Bigr)\log\Bigl|\cos\frac{\pi a}{p}\Bigr|
  =\begin{cases}
     0, & p\equiv1\pmod8,\\[2pt]
     4\,h(p)\,\log\varepsilon_p, & p\equiv5\pmod8.
   \end{cases}
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $p\equiv1\pmod4$ the Legendre symbol is an even primitive real character with
$\tau(\chi)=\sqrt p$, so Proposition~\ref{prop:chardecomp} gives the sum as
$\bigl(1-\bigl(\tfrac2p\bigr)\bigr)\sqrt p\,L(1,\chi)$. The factor is $0$ when
$\bigl(\tfrac2p\bigr)=1$, i.e.\ $p\equiv1\pmod 8$, and $2$ otherwise. Dirichlet's class number
formula for the real quadratic field of discriminant $p$ reads
$L(1,\chi)=2h(p)\log\varepsilon_p/\sqrt p$, and the two square roots cancel.\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[what that sentence says]\label{rem:classnumber}
The left-hand side is a sum of logarithms of cosines at rational points --- the same energy
$X$ that Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} governs and that \S\ref{sec:random} sums over a set of integers.
The right-hand side is the class number of a real quadratic field times its regulator.
\emph{Nothing on the left knows it is supposed to be four times an integer times a regulator},
which is why the integrality is worth reporting as a check and not merely as a consequence.

We claim no novelty for the ingredients: the character decomposition is elementary Fourier
analysis and the class number formula is Dirichlet's. What is worth recording is that the
programme's energy function, arrived at from a subset-sum landscape, evaluates against quadratic
characters to a class number --- and that the vanishing half of the statement is exactly the
condition that $2$ splits.

The classical neighbourhood is named for the reader's benefit and ours. $X$ is an element of the
universal ordinary distribution~\cite{KL}, the object whose $\{\pm1\}$-cohomology Kubert and
Sinnott computed and from which Sinnott's index formula relates the cyclotomic units to the class
number. \emph{An identity carrying a class number out of that theory is the expected kind of
output, not a surprising one.} We have not been able to consult those papers directly and so do
not claim to know whether Corollary~\ref{cor:classnumber} appears there in this form; the honest
statement is that it is the sort of thing that may.
\STATUS{a reading of Corollary~\ref{cor:classnumber}; adds no claim. The novelty question is left
open deliberately: this is the kind of identity that may well be classical, and
\S\ref{sec:scope} says so rather than implying otherwise.}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[where this puts the programme's one ineffective constant]\label{rem:ineffective}
Proposition~\ref{prop:chardecomp} says the coset energy has a complete spectral decomposition
over $(\mathbb{Z}/v)^{*}$ in which \emph{the trivial component is exactly $\log2$ with no error
term at all}, and every other component is a Dirichlet $L$-value at $s=1$ times a Gauss sum.
For a set $A$ the deficiency at $\theta=h/v$ is therefore
\[
  D\Bigl(\frac hv\Bigr)=\frac{|A|}{\varphi(v)}\sum_{r}X\Bigl(\frac rv\Bigr)
  \;+\;\frac1{\varphi(v)}\sum_{\chi\ne\chi_{0}}\overline{\chi}\text{-twisted count of }A
       \cdot\Bigl(-\tau(\overline{\chi})(1-\chi(2))L(1,\chi)\Bigr),
\]
so \emph{the main term is exact and all of the error is carried by characters}. When $A$ is a
set of primes the twisted counts are the objects Siegel--Walfisz controls, and Part~II's single
ineffective constant~\cite{P2} enters exactly there and nowhere else. That is a statement about
the location of the ineffectivity, not a removal of it; what it buys is a route --- under GRH the
twisted counts are explicit, and so is the constant.

The factor $1-\chi(2)$ deserves its own line. It kills the component whenever $2$ lies in the
kernel of $\chi$, so the prime $2$ governs which characters can see the energy at all. This is
the fourth face of the same fact: Proposition~\ref{prop:twopower} (which residues form a coset),
Theorem~\ref{thm:modfour} (the rate $1/\sqrt2$), Remark~\ref{rem:shift} (when the evaluation is a
bound), and now the character group. \emph{The obstruction of this theory is $2$-adic, and it has
now been met from four independent directions.}
\STATUS{derived from Proposition~\ref{prop:chardecomp}; the decomposition is proved, the reading
of where the ineffectivity sits is a reading and is labelled as such. Nothing here is claimed to
remove an ineffective constant.}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[the evaluation is a bound exactly at the powers of two]\label{rem:shift}
\STATUS{proved, both directions, from the product form of Lemma~\ref{lem:coset}; the failure is
witnessed by an explicit counterexample. The closed form below was checked against direct
computation at $35$ points, \texttt{shift\_r144}. \emph{This remark replaces a weaker and partly
wrong statement of the same thing; see the note at its end.}}
Proposition~\ref{prop:redresidue} evaluates the average at $t=0$, and a minor arc is a
\emph{neighbourhood} of a rational, so one must ask whether the value is also a floor for
$\varphi(v)^{-1}\sum_{r}X(t+r/v)$. Exponentiating Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} answers it. For
$4\mid n$ the odd residues are the coset $\tfrac1n+\tfrac2n\mathbb{Z}$ of order $n/2$, so
\[
  O(n,t):=\prod_{\substack{s<n\\ s\ \mathrm{odd}}}\bigl|2\cos\pi(t+\tfrac sn)\bigr|
        = 2\bigl|\cos(\pi\tfrac n2 t)\bigr|,
\]
and M\"obius over the odd part of $v=2^{j}w$ with $j\ge2$ gives, every $v/d$ again divisible
by $4$,
\[
  Q(v,t):=\prod_{r\in(\mathbb{Z}/v)^{*}}\bigl|2\cos\pi(t+\tfrac rv)\bigr|
  \;=\;2^{[w=1]}\prod_{d\mid w}\bigl|\cos(\pi\tfrac{v}{2d}t)\bigr|^{\mu(d)} .
\]
The average is bounded below by its value at $t=0$ exactly when $Q(v,t)\le Q(v,0)$, and the
product reads off both cases:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{$w=1$, i.e.\ $v=2^{j}$}: $Q=2|\cos(\pi\tfrac v2t)|\le2=Q(v,0)$. \textbf{Uniform, and
      proved} --- as it must be, since the reduced residues are then one coset and
      Corollary~\ref{cor:floor} already covers it.
\item \emph{$w>1$}: $Q$ is a \emph{ratio}, the divisors with $\mu(d)=-1$ sitting in the
      denominator, and a ratio of cosines is not bounded by $1$. For $w=3$ it is
      $|\cos3u|/|\cos u|=|4\cos^{2}u-3|$, which exceeds $1$ whenever $\cos^{2}u<\tfrac12$.
      \textbf{The bound fails.} At $v=12$, $t=\tfrac15$ the average is $0.4525$ against
      $\log2=0.6931$ at $t=0$: it falls by $0.2406$.
\end{itemize}
So the evaluation becomes a bound on the surrounding arc \emph{if and only if $v$ is a power of
two} --- which is Proposition~\ref{prop:twopower} once more, and the reason is the same one:
that is exactly when the reduced residues are a coset. Read together with
Remark~\ref{rem:modfourcorollary}: at $\theta=h/v$ the deficiency is $|A|\log2$ for every $v$
outside the powers of two, and at none of those $v$ does that survive a shift.

\emph{Note on how this remark got here.} Its first version reported the $4\mid v$, $w>1$ case as
\emph{measured uniform}, on a scan of $t$ over $[0,1/v]$. That interval is the period of the
\emph{full-group} average, where $t\mapsto t+1/v$ permutes $\{k/v\}$; it is not a period of the
reduced-residue average, because $t\mapsto t+1/v$ carries $r/v$ to $(r+1)/v$ and the units are
not closed under $+1$. The scan was therefore blind to the region containing the counterexample.
We record it because the error is invisible in the output --- a shorter scan returns a number,
not a warning.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Theorem~\ref{thm:modfour} is the case $w=1$]\label{rem:modfourcorollary}
Read Proposition~\ref{prop:redresidue} as a statement about how much a minor arc can save. For a
set whose residues equidistribute over $(\mathbb{Z}/v)^{*}$ --- the primes, by Dirichlet --- the
deficiency at $\theta=h/v$ is $|A|\log2$ per element, i.e.\ the \emph{full} circle mean, at every
$v$ except the powers of two; and at $v=2^{j}$ it is $(1-2^{1-j})\log2$, minimised at $v=4$ where
it is $\tfrac12\log2$. Since the bound sits in an exponent, that reads
\[
  \Bigl|\prod_{a\in A}(1+e(a\theta))\Bigr|\ \le\ 2^{|A|}e^{-|A|\log2}=1
  \quad\text{in general},\qquad
  \le\ 2^{|A|}e^{-|A|(\log2)/2}=\bigl(\sqrt2\bigr)^{|A|}
  \quad\text{at }v=4,
\]
so the rate per element is $1/\sqrt2$ at $v=4$ and $1$ elsewhere --- \emph{at the rational
point itself}, which by Remark~\ref{rem:shift} is all one may say outside the powers of two.
\emph{That is Theorem~\ref{thm:modfour}}, and the proposition says in addition that the obstruction occurs at
the powers of two \emph{and nowhere else} --- which is Proposition~\ref{prop:twopower}, reached
here from an exact evaluation rather than from the group-theoretic characterisation. Two
independent routes to one classification.
\STATUS{derived from Proposition~\ref{prop:redresidue}; adds no claim, and is included because
the two theorems were proved separately and it is worth recording that they are one fact.}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[why the two extremal moduli differ, in one line]\label{rem:whytwo}
Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} averages over an \emph{additive} coset. The residues available to an odd
sequence are an additive coset for every $q$ --- all residues for $q$ odd, the odd residues for
$q$ even --- so the floor of Corollary~\ref{cor:floor} always applies to it. The residues
available to a set of primes are the \emph{reduced} residues, and by
Proposition~\ref{prop:twopower} those are an additive coset exactly when $q$ is a power of $2$.
Hence:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{$q=2^{k}$}: the two classes coincide, the floor applies to the primes as well, and
      the value is pinned at $2^{1/v-1}$ with $v=2^{k-1}$ --- maximal at $q=4$, giving
      $1/\sqrt2$.
\item \emph{$q=2m$, $m$ odd $>1$}: the odd residues contain $j=m$, where $\cos(\pi m/q)=0$, and
      the reduced residues do not. An odd sequence therefore meets the zero and a set of primes
      misses it. That is the modulus-$6$ peak.
\item \emph{$q$ odd}: the reduced residues are everything but $0$, and the primes sit at the
      full-circle mean $\log2$.
\end{itemize}
So Theorem~\ref{thm:modfour} and Part~II's modulus-$6$ theorem are not two coincidences but one
fact about $q$, and the smallest $q$ at which the two classes part company is $6$.
\STATUS{derived from Propositions~\ref{prop:twopower} and~\ref{prop:redresidue}; the three
bullets are exactly the three cases of the latter, which is where the third one ($q$ odd, value
$\log2$) is now proved rather than asserted. Both endpoints are proved --- here and in
Part~II~\cite{P2} --- so this remark reorganises rather than adds. Measured: at $q=4$ and $q=8$
the odd primes below $4\cdot10^{4}$ sit on the floor to five decimals, and at $q=6$ and $q=10$
they fall below it while the odd numbers rise far above; \texttt{tool\_coset\_r138}.}
\end{remark}

\subsection{The rate, and what is still missing}\label{sec:rate}

\begin{theorem}[minor-arc rate for random odd sequences]\label{prop:rate}
Let $A=A(N)$ be drawn from the Cram\'er model above and let
$F_A(\theta)=\prod_{a\in A}|\cos(\pi a\theta)|$. Write $b=|A|$. The running parameter is
$N$, and $b=b(N)\to\infty$ almost surely; the limit below is along $N\to\infty$. Then almost
surely
\[
   \lim_{b\to\infty}\ \max_{\theta\ \mathrm{minor}}\ F_A(\theta)^{1/b}\;=\;\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]
Here \emph{minor} means $\|\theta\|\ge1/N$, with $\|\cdot\|$ the distance to the nearest integer;
Step~$2''$ shows that this radius is large enough, so nothing is left unowned.
\end{theorem}

\STATUS{proved, with every constant written out. The proof is the four steps below rather

deferred, no constant is fitted, and the only external \emph{input} is the arithmetic half,
Theorem~\ref{thm:modfour}, which is proved in this paper. That is a statement about inputs

\emph{Step 2$'$ (from the rationals to every $\theta$: there is no dent).} Write
$\theta=a/q+\beta$ with $q\le Q$ and $|\beta|\le1/(q(Q+1))$ (Dirichlet). The obstacle here looks
like a job for a quantitative equidistribution estimate --- Erd\H{o}s--Tur\'an or Koksma --- and
it is not: Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} settles it as an \emph{identity}. Since $j\mapsto ja$ permutes
$R_q$, the values $\{ja/q\}$ are the $\{j/q\}$, and for any common shift $s$
\[
   \frac1{|R_q|}\sum_{j\in R_q}X\Bigl(s+\frac jq\Bigr)
   \;=\;\underbrace{\Bigl(1-\frac1v\Bigr)\log2}_{-\log M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)}
   \;+\;\frac1v\,X(vs+\sigma)\;\ge\;-\log M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q),
   \tag{$\ast$}\label{eq:nodent}
\]
because $X\ge0$. \emph{Shifting the whole rational grid can only raise the mean}; the dent one
expects when $\theta$ moves off a rational does not exist, and no discrepancy theory is needed
to bound it. Equality in \eqref{eq:nodent} holds exactly on $vs+\sigma\in\mathbb Z$, so the
rational values are the true minima, not merely convenient sample points.
\STATUS{proved (Lemma~\ref{lem:coset}); the minimum over $s$ of the left side of

$15$ moduli, and to survive truncation at $M$ for every $q\le1000$; \texttt{stab\_r110}}

First, truncation does not spoil \eqref{eq:nodent}. The loss $(X-M)^{+}$ is nonzero only within
$e^{-M}/\pi$ of a pole, and the coset points are $1/v\ge1/Q>1/(Qe)=2e^{-M}/\pi$ apart, so at most
one residue is affected; if it lies at distance $d$ from the pole then
$X\le\log\frac1{2d}$, while the excess term of \eqref{eq:nodent} is
$\tfrac1vX(vs+\sigma)\ge\tfrac1v\log\frac1{\pi vd}$ because $vs+\sigma$ sits at distance $vd$ from
a pole. The floor therefore survives as soon as $M\ge\log(\pi v/2)$, and with $M=\log(2Q/\pi)+1$
and $v\le Q$ that reads $1\ge\log(\pi^2/4)$ --- true, \textbf{with a margin of exactly
$1-\log(\pi^2/4)=0.0968$}. This is the third place the $+1$ in $M$ earns itself.

\emph{Step $2'$ continued (the two error terms, in full).} What \eqref{eq:nodent} does not by
itself cover is that element $m$ of the class $j$ sits at $ja/q+m\beta$, so the shift varies
\emph{within} a class. Fix $q\notin\{1,2\}$ and group the odd $m\in[3,N]$ by residue class mod
$q$. In a class the $m$ form an arithmetic progression of common difference $2v$ (for $q$ odd the
odd numbers in a class mod $q$ step by $2q=2v$; for $q=2u$ they step by $q=2v$), so the phases
form an arithmetic progression of step $\eta=2v\beta$ sweeping an arc of length $L=N|\beta|$, and
the class starts at $x_j+m_j\beta$ where $m_j\le 2v$ is its first element. Three errors, and
nothing else:

\emph{(i) Progression versus integral.} $X^{(M)}$ is of bounded variation, at most $2M$ per
period, so for a progression of step $\eta$ over an arc of length $L$,
$\bigl|\sum_rX^{(M)}-\eta^{-1}\!\int\bigr|\le2M(L+1)$. There are $v$ classes and $\asymp N/2$
terms in all, so in the mean this costs at most $4qM/N+4M/(Q+1)$, using $v|\beta|\le1/(Q+1)$.

\emph{(ii) Jitter.} Replacing the arc of class $j$ by the common arc is a translation by
$\epsilon=m_j\beta$, and $m_j\le2v$ while the progression step is $\eta=2v\beta$, so
$|\epsilon|\le|\eta|$ \emph{exactly}. Translating by $\epsilon$ changes the integral by at most
$\epsilon V$, so the per-class error is at most $\epsilon V/\eta\le V\le2M(L+1)$ --- the same
shape as (i), and not a separate mechanism at all. Per element, $4qM/N+4M/(Q+1)$.

\emph{(iii) Unequal class counts.} They differ by at most $1$, costing $vM$ in the sum, i.e.\
$2qM/N$ in the mean.

After (i)--(iii) the integrand is the honest coset average, which is $\ge(1-1/v)\log2$ pointwise
by \eqref{eq:nodent}; integrating a pointwise inequality needs no further hypothesis, and in
particular \emph{no case distinction on the length of the sweep} --- which is what makes the
route robust in $Q$ where an arc-comparison design is not. Collecting,
\[
   \frac{\sum_m p_mX^{(M)}_m(\theta)}{\sum_m p_m}\;\ge\;\Bigl(1-\frac1v\Bigr)\log2
   \;-\;E,\qquad E\;\le\;\frac{10\,qM}{N}+\frac{8M}{Q+1}.
\]
\emph{Both terms are minimised together at $Q=\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$, where $E=O(M/\sqrt N)$} ---
which is why the Dirichlet parameter is $\sqrt N$ and not, as one first writes, $N$. At $Q=N$ the
argument fails twice over: the residue classes hold $O(1)$ elements each, and $|\beta|\le1/(qN)$
makes every sweep shorter than a single period.

\emph{The weights need no argument at all.} $p_m=2/\log m$ is \emph{decreasing}, so by Abel
summation a bound valid for every prefix $\{m\le N'\}$ passes to the $p$-weighted mean unchanged:
if $S(N')\ge c\,P(N')-\mathcal{E}(N')$ for all $N'$, then
$\sum p_mX^{(M)}_m\ge c\sum p_m-\sum_k(p_k-p_{k+1})\mathcal{E}(k)-p_{N}\mathcal{E}(N)$, and with
$\mathcal{E}(N')\le C M(\sqrt N+N'/\sqrt N)$ the subtracted term is
$O(M\sqrt N)+O(M/\sqrt N)\sum p_m$, i.e.\ $O(M\log N/\sqrt N)$ relative. There is no block-weight
condition to check.
\STATUS{proved; the shortfall at the binding modulus $q=3$ is measured as
$1.9\cdot10^{-3},5.8\cdot10^{-4},1.7\cdot10^{-4},4.8\cdot10^{-5}$ at

\emph{Step $2''$ (the three moduli with $v\le2$, and the major arc).} Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}
gives $\tfrac12\log2$ at $q=4$ ($v=2$) and nothing at all at $q\in\{1,2\}$ ($v=1$). The last two
are not prose; they are the following lemma, and $q=4$ needs no more than the identity because
$\tfrac12\log2$ is exactly the value the theorem is about.

\begin{lemma}[the degenerate moduli]\label{lem:vone}
Let $\phi(w)=-\log|\sin\pi w|$ and $L>0$. Then
$\frac1L\int_0^L\phi=\log2+\mathrm{Cl}_2(2\pi\{L\})/(2\pi L)$, and
\[
   \frac1L\int_0^L\phi\;>\;\tfrac12\log2\quad\text{for every }L>0,
   \qquad
   \frac1L\int_0^L X\;\ge\;\log2-\frac{\mathrm{Cl}_2(\pi/3)}{2\pi}=0.5316\quad\text{for }L\ge1.
\]
Consequently the mean over a $q=2$ sweep exceeds $\tfrac12\log2$, and so does the mean over a
$q=1$ sweep once $L=N\|\theta\|\ge1$ --- which is exactly the definition of \emph{minor}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The closed form is $\int_0^x\phi=x\log2+\mathrm{Cl}_2(2\pi x)/(2\pi)$ together with the
periodicity of $\phi$. For $L\le\tfrac12$: $\phi$ is convex on $(0,1)$, so the average over
$[0,L]$ is at least the midpoint value $-\log\sin(\pi L/2)\ge-\log\sin(\pi/4)=\tfrac12\log2$; at
the endpoint $L=\tfrac12$ the two-point midpoint bound $\tfrac12[\phi(L/4)+\phi(3L/4)]=0.51986$
shows the inequality is strict there. For $L\ge\tfrac12$: $1/(2\pi L)\le1/\pi$ and
$|\mathrm{Cl}_2|\le\mathrm{Cl}_2(\pi/3)$, so the average is at least
$\log2-\mathrm{Cl}_2(\pi/3)/\pi=0.3701>\tfrac12\log2$. For $q=1$ the same computation with $X$
in place of $\phi$ gives $\log2+\mathrm{Cl}_2(2\pi L+\pi)/(2\pi L)$, and $L\ge1$ makes the
correction at most $\mathrm{Cl}_2(\pi/3)/(2\pi)=0.1615$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
The true $q=2$ floor is $0.4945$ at $L=0.7908$ --- above $\tfrac12\log2+\delta$, not merely above
$\tfrac12\log2$ --- but that is measured, not proved, and the theorem does not need it. The
smallest $L$ at which the $q=1$ average reaches $\tfrac12\log2+\delta$ is $0.4528$, so the radius
$1/N$ in the definition of \emph{minor} is comfortable rather than tight.
\STATUS{proved; the Clausen closed form checked against direct quadrature to $4.5\cdot10^{-5}$,
and the convexity bound verified at $5000$ values of $L$; \texttt{rate\_r112}}

\emph{Step 3 (concentration).} The summands are independent and lie in $[0,M]$, and there are
$n=\lfloor(N-1)/2\rfloor$ of them, so Hoeffding gives
$\Pr[\sum\xi_mX^{(M)}_m<\mathbb{E}-t]\le\exp(-2t^2/(nM^2))$. Take $t=\varepsilon\,\mathbb{E}|A|$
and use $\mathbb{E}|A|\asymp2N/\log N$, $n\asymp N/2$, $Q=\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$ and hence
$M\asymp\tfrac12\log N$: the failure probability at one $\theta$ is
\[
   \exp\bigl(-c\,\varepsilon^2N/\log^4N\bigr),\qquad c=64 .
\]
Two readings of the same bound, and the theorem uses the second. With $\varepsilon=\tfrac12\delta$
this is the fixed-margin statement, $\exp(-cN/\log^4N)$ with $c=16\delta^2=0.2135$. But the
theorem only needs the mean to survive to within $o(1)$, so one may instead take
$\varepsilon=N^{-1/4}$, which still leaves the exponent $\asymp\sqrt N/\log^4N\to\infty$.
\emph{That is why $q\in\{1,2\}$ need only clear $\tfrac12\log2$ and not $\tfrac12\log2+\delta$},
and it is what makes Lemma~\ref{lem:vone} sufficient. No second moment of an exponential sum
appears: the randomness is in the \emph{sequence}, not in the phases.

\emph{Step 4 (net, then Borel--Cantelli).} Where $X_m<M$ we have $|\cos(\pi m\theta)|\ge e^{-M}$,
hence $|\partial_\theta X^{(M)}_m|=\pi m|\tan(\pi m\theta)|\le\pi Ne^{M}\le2eNQ\le2eN^{3/2}$, so
a net of spacing $\varrho/(2eN^{3/2}b)$ suffices and has $O(N^{5/2}/\varrho)$ points. Since
$N^{5/2}\exp(-c\varepsilon^2N/\log^4N)$ is summable in $N$ for $\varepsilon=N^{-1/4}$, the union
bound and Borel--Cantelli give the almost-sure statement.

\emph{Assembling.} Steps 1--4 give, almost surely and uniformly over minor $\theta$,
$b^{-1}\sum_{a\in A}X_a(\theta)\ge\tfrac12\log2-o(1)$, i.e.\
$\max_{\theta\ \mathrm{minor}}F_A(\theta)^{1/b}\le2^{-1/2}(1+o(1))$: at $q=4$ the floor is
$\tfrac12\log2$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}, at every other $q\ge3$ it is $\tfrac12\log2+\delta$,
at $q\in\{1,2\}$ it is above $\tfrac12\log2$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:vone}, and the error of Step $2'$
is $O(M\log N/\sqrt N)$. The matching lower bound is free: $\theta=\tfrac14$ is minor and
$X_a(\tfrac14)=\tfrac12\log2$ for \emph{every} odd $a$, so $F_A(\tfrac14)^{1/b}=2^{-1/2}$
identically. Hence the limit is $2^{-1/2}$. \qed
\STATUS{proved}

\begin{remark}[where this sits beside the rigorous work on number partitioning]\label{rem:localrem}

\emph{does $\mathrm{lm}_A(n)/r_A(n)\to\Gamma(A)$ hold for every family of distinct odd integers,

\section{Where this sits, and the algorithmic reading}\label{sec:outlook}

\begin{remark}[what the main theorem says to someone who works on disordered systems]\label{rem:annealed}
\STATUS{proved; the derivation is three lines from the classification of local minima in
Part~I~\cite{P1} and is given in full here. The equivalence used in the first line was
additionally checked by brute force on $1952$ (set, target) pairs, and the identification of the
two expressions for $\Gamma$ on five families; \texttt{annealed\_r147}.}
The classification says: $S$ is a strict local minimum for the target $n$ if and only if, writing
$D=\sigma(S)-n$, every $a\in S$ satisfies $a>2D$ and every $a\notin S$ satisfies $a>-2D$. For a
fixed offset $D=d>0$ that condition is simply $S\subseteq\{a\in A: a>2d\}$, and the number of
elements it forbids is exactly $N_A(d)=\#\{a\in A: a\le 2d\}$.

Now apply the \emph{annealed} --- that is, independence --- heuristic: pretend the number of
subsets of a subfamily $B\subseteq A$ hitting a prescribed target is proportional to $2^{|B|}$.
Then the layer at offset $d$ contributes a fraction $2^{-N_A(d)}$ of the ground-state count, the
offsets $\pm d$ pair up, and $d=0$ contributes $1$. Summing,
\[
  \frac{\mathrm{lm}_A(n)}{r_A(n)}\ \approx\ 1+2\sum_{d\ge1}2^{-N_A(d)}\ =\ \Gamma(A).
\]
\emph{So $\Gamma$ is not merely computed by an argument that avoids the annealed approximation:
$\Gamma$ \textbf{is} the annealed prediction.} What Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer} and Part~II
establish is therefore
\begin{center}
\emph{the annealed count of metastable states is asymptotically exact for this model,}
\end{center}
under a hypothesis \textup{(H)} that is checked rather than assumed, with
\S\ref{sec:counterexample} exhibiting a profile where it fails. That is the sentence we would
offer to the statistical mechanics of disordered systems, where the annealed approximation is in
constant use and is almost never controlled: here is a model with an $\mathsf{NP}$-complete
ground-state problem in which it is provably right, together with the condition that delimits it
and an explicit case that violates the condition.

Two honest limits. The statement is about the \emph{ratio} and not about each layer separately;
and it is one model, so it is evidence about a heuristic rather than a theorem about it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Related work}

Subset sums and number partitioning have a large landscape literature, and it is worth saying
precisely where the present series does and does not meet it. That literature is
overwhelmingly about \emph{random instances}: the phase transition and barrier-tree statistics
of the partitioning landscape, the local-REM description of the near-optimal spectrum, and
more recently overlap-gap obstructions for local algorithms. Its objects are the energy
spectrum and the dynamics; its randomness is in the instance; and its answers are laws for a
typical instance.

Parts~I and~II and the transfer-function strand do something different in kind: they fix the sequence and compute an
\emph{arithmetic invariant of that set}, $\Gamma(A)=1+2\sum_d2^{-\#\{a\in A:\,a\le2d\}}$,
which the ratio
$\lm/r$ converges to for \emph{every} target. Nothing is averaged over instances, and the
answer is a number one reads off $A$. The present paper's \S\ref{sec:random} is the one place
where the two meet --- there the sequence is random --- and even there the question is the
ratio's limit rather than the spectrum's law.

One vocabulary warning, since the words collide. ``Biased Bernoulli measure'' is established
terminology in the theory of Bernoulli convolutions, where it denotes a self-similar measure
on $\mathbb{R}$; that is a different object from $P_q$ here, and we do not claim the term.
Within the subset-sum landscape literature we have not found the biased deformation
$\Gamma^{(q)}$ of \S\ref{sec:biased}, nor the odd-residue extremal quantity
$M_{\mathrm{odd}}$ of \S\ref{sec:modfour}, under any name.

\subsection{The algorithmic reading}

This programme began with local search on subset-sum instances, and the invariant answers a
question one asks there. If a local-search procedure ends at a uniformly chosen strict local
minimum, the expected number of attempts before landing on a ground state is $\lm/r$, and
Parts~I and~II and the transfer-function strand predict that this number converges to $\Gamma(A)$ --- \emph{computable from $A$
alone, without solving a single instance, and independent of the target}. For the odd numbers
it is $3$; for the primes, $5.3492\ldots$ Two conditions are attached to that sentence and we
attach them here rather than leaving them in \S\ref{sec:transfer}: the convergence is a
\emph{proof skeleton} there, not a theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:rigid},
whose missing ingredient is
named in its own statement), and the uniformity of the terminal distribution is an assumption
about the search, not a fact about the landscape.

Theorem~\ref{thm:fair} is the part of this subsection that has a proof, and it is a statement
about \emph{sampling}, not about search dynamics: drawing configurations with a biased coin
instead of a fair one multiplies the ratio by $\Gamma^{(q)}/\Gamma\ge1$, strictly for
$q\ne\tfrac12$. So a natural one-parameter family of sampling rules cannot beat the fair coin
on this statistic --- which is a claim about the statistic, and becomes a claim about a
restart heuristic only under the uniformity assumption just named.
Section~\ref{sec:phiq} adds that the biased landscape also acquires a \emph{first-order}
sensitivity to the target that the fair one does not have.

Behind the uniformity assumption is the reason this is an outlook and not a theorem about
algorithms: a count of local minima is not a distribution over basins of attraction, and if
the basins are very unequal the ratio bounds the wrong quantity. Nor does anything here
address dynamics --- barrier heights, escape times, or the behaviour of any particular
heuristic. What the invariant supplies is a target-independent constant that any such analysis
has to be consistent with, together with a proved statement that one obvious way of trying to
improve that constant does not improve it.

\subsection{The two-point remainder}\label{app:taylor}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:twopoint}
\STATUS{proved}
Let $p\in(0,\tfrac12]$, $q=1-p$, and let
$P_4(v)=ipv-\tfrac{pq}{2}v^{2}-\tfrac{i}{6}\kappa_3v^{3}+\tfrac{1}{24}\kappa_4v^{4}$ with
$\kappa_3=pq(1-2p)$ and $\kappa_4=pq(1-6pq)$. Then for every real $v$ with $|v|\le1$,
\[
  \bigl|\log(1-p+pe^{iv})-P_4(v)\bigr|\;\le\;C_{\mathrm{T}}\,|v|^{5},
  \qquad C_{\mathrm{T}}=\frac{2(2+\pi)}{2^{5}}=0.32136\ldots
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $K(t)=\log(q+pe^{t})$, so that $\log(1-p+pe^{iv})=K(iv)$ and $\kappa_j=K^{(j)}(0)$.

\emph{Analyticity.} $q+pe^{t}=0$ forces $e^{t}=-q/p$, hence $\operatorname{Im}t\equiv\pi
\pmod{2\pi}$ and $\operatorname{Re}t=\log(q/p)$. The nearest such point to the origin is at
distance $|\log(q/p)+i\pi|\ge\pi$. So $K$ is analytic on $|t|<\pi$, for every $p$.

\emph{A lower bound on $|q+pe^{t}|$ for $|t|=2$.} Write $t=x+iy$ with $x^{2}+y^{2}=4$, so
$|y|\le2$ and $\cos y\ge\cos2$. With $u=pe^{x}>0$,
\[
  |q+pe^{t}|^{2}=q^{2}+2qu\cos y+u^{2}\;\ge\;q^{2}+2qu\cos2+u^{2}\;\ge\;q^{2}(1-\cos^{2}2)
  \;=\;q^{2}\sin^{2}2,
\]
the last step by minimising the quadratic in $u$ at $u=-q\cos2$. Hence
$|q+pe^{t}|\ge q|\sin2|\ge\tfrac12|\sin2|=0.4546\ldots$ using $q\ge\tfrac12$.

\emph{An upper bound on $|K|$ for $|t|=2$.} Also $|q+pe^{t}|\le q+pe^{2}\le e^{2}$. Since
$|\log w|\le\bigl|\log|w|\bigr|+\pi$ for $w\ne0$, and
$\bigl|\log|q+pe^{t}|\bigr|\le\max\{2,\,|\log0.4546|\}=2$, we get $M_2:=\max_{|t|=2}|K(t)|
\le2+\pi$.

\emph{Cauchy, and the tail.} By Cauchy's estimate on $|t|=2$, $|\kappa_j|\le j!\,M_2/2^{j}$.
Therefore, for $|v|\le1$,
\[
  \bigl|K(iv)-P_4(v)\bigr|=\Bigl|\sum_{j\ge5}\kappa_j\frac{(iv)^{j}}{j!}\Bigr|
  \le M_2\sum_{j\ge5}\Bigl(\frac{|v|}{2}\Bigr)^{j}
  = M_2\frac{(|v|/2)^{5}}{1-|v|/2}\le 2M_2\Bigl(\frac{|v|}{2}\Bigr)^{5},
\]
which is the claim. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[how much slack the proof carries]\label{rem:slack}
The proved constant is $0.3214$; the maximum of
$|K(iv)-P_4(v)|/|v|^{5}$ over $p\le\tfrac12$ and $|v|\le1$ is measured at $0.00114$, so the
proof is loose by a factor $282$. That costs nothing: only the exponent of $|v|$ enters the
scaling of \S\ref{app:sum}, and the constant enters once, linearly.
\STATUS{experimentally confirmed; \texttt{r1proof\_r141}. The same script checks the two
hand-derived inequalities of the proof: the singularity is never closer than $\pi$ over
$p\in(0,\tfrac12]$, and $|q+pe^{t}|\ge q|\sin2|$ holds on $|t|=2$ with no violation and with
equality attained.}
\end{remark}

\subsection{The summed remainder, estimated under the integral}\label{app:sum}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:sumrem}
\STATUS{proved}
Put $T_1=\sigma/N$ and, for $|t|\le T_1$,
\[
  R_5(t)=\log\psi(t/\sigma)-\Bigl(-\tfrac{t^{2}}{2}-\tfrac{i}{6}\alpha t^{3}
        +\tfrac{1}{24}\beta t^{4}\Bigr),
  \qquad \psi(u)=\mathbb{E}\,e^{iu(S-\mu)} .
\]
Then
\[
  \int_{|t|\le T_1}e^{-t^{2}/2}\,|R_5(t)|\,dt
  \;\le\;16\,C_{\mathrm{T}}\,\frac{\sum_{a\in B}a^{5}}{\sigma^{5}} .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\log\psi(t/\sigma)=\sum_{a\in B}\bigl[K(iat/\sigma)-ip_aat/\sigma\bigr]$, and the phase of the
$a$-th factor is $|at/\sigma|\le N T_1/\sigma=1$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:twopoint} applies to every
factor. Summing it, $|R_5(t)|\le C_{\mathrm{T}}\sum_a a^{5}|t/\sigma|^{5}$, and
$\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-t^{2}/2}|t|^{5}dt=16$. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[why the estimate is made under the integral, and not uniformly]\label{rem:notsup}
The natural move is to bound $|R_5|$ by its supremum over the whole of R1. That bound cannot
work: measured, $\sup_{\mathrm{R1}}|R_5|$ is of order $10^{2}$ and \emph{does not decrease with
$k$}, because at the outer edge of R1 the cubic term of the expansion is itself of order
$10^{2}$. The integral is dominated by $|t|\lesssim1$, where the expansion is excellent, and the
error at the edge is never integrated against anything: the $|\hat G|$-weighted remainder is
$9\cdot10^{-4}$ at $k=100$ and $9\cdot10^{-5}$ at $k=450$, decaying like $k^{-3/2}$ ---
\emph{faster} than the leading terms of the budget it is a remainder for.
\STATUS{experimentally confirmed; \texttt{r1remainder\_r134}, \texttt{r1weighted\_r134}.}
A general form worth stating once: \textbf{a supremum taken over a region where the integrand is
already negligible charges the whole region at its worst point. When an estimate is going into
an integral, make it under the integral.}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[the threshold, and where it comes from]\label{rem:threshold}
Cutting at $T_1=\sigma/N$ rather than at the LCLT-natural radius is what makes every phase at
most $1$. Beyond $T_1$ no Taylor bound is needed: on $T_1\le|t|\le\pi\sigma$ the quadratic
estimate of Remark~\ref{rem:principal} gives $|\psi(t/\sigma)|\le\exp(-c\,t_{\min}\sigma^{2}/N^{2})$
with $c$ absolute, and $\sigma^{2}/N^{2}\to\infty$ under~\textup{(H)}, so that contribution is
smaller than any power of $k$ once $k$ exceeds a threshold $k_0$ depending on the profile
through $\sigma/N$ alone. \emph{This is a derived threshold and not a hypothesis}: (H) supplies
it for every admissible profile. For the odd numbers and the odd primes,
$\sigma^{2}/N^{2}\asymp k$, and the threshold is of order a few thousand --- immaterial to
statements about $k\to\infty$, and worth printing rather than hiding.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The inversion}\label{app:inv}

\begin{lemma}[Hermite inversion]\label{lem:heinv}
\STATUS{proved}
For every $j\ge0$ and every real $z$,
$\displaystyle\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-t^{2}/2}(it)^{j}e^{-izt}\,dt
 = \mathrm{He}_j(z)\,\varphi(z)$, where $\varphi$ is the standard normal density and
$\mathrm{He}_j$ the $j$-th Hermite polynomial.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\frac1{2\pi}\int e^{-t^{2}/2}e^{-izt}dt=\varphi(z)$; differentiating $j$ times in $z$ brings
$(-it)^{j}$ inside, and $\varphi^{(j)}=(-1)^{j}\mathrm{He}_j\varphi$. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[the expansion on R1]\label{prop:R1done}
\STATUS{proved, modulo the independent reading recorded at the head of this appendix}
With the notation above, for every integer $m$,
\[
  \mathbb{P}\bigl[S=m\bigr]
  =\frac{\varphi(z)}{\sigma}
   \Bigl[1+\frac{\alpha}{6}\mathrm{He}_3(z)+\frac{\beta}{24}\mathrm{He}_4(z)
        +\frac{\alpha^{2}}{72}\mathrm{He}_6(z)\Bigr]+E,
\]
and, on the extraction window $|m-\mu|\le W$ with $Z=W/\sigma$,
\[
  |E|\;\le\;\frac{\varphi(0)}{\sigma}\,\varepsilon^{*}(Z),\qquad
  \varepsilon^{*}(Z)=\frac{|\alpha|}{6}\min(3Z,2+Z^{3})
   +\frac{|\beta|}{24}(3+6Z^{2}+Z^{4})
   +\frac{\alpha^{2}}{72}(15+45Z^{2}+15Z^{4}+Z^{6})
   +\frac{16\,C_{\mathrm{T}}}{\sigma^{5}}\sum_{a\in B}a^{5},
\]
for all $k$ above the threshold of Remark~\ref{rem:threshold}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Invert $\psi$ over $[-\pi\sigma,\pi\sigma]$ in the variable $t$ and split at $T_1$. Beyond
$T_1$, Remark~\ref{rem:threshold}. Within $T_1$, exponentiate the expansion of
Lemma~\ref{lem:sumrem}:
\[
  \psi(t/\sigma)=e^{-t^{2}/2}\Bigl[1-\tfrac{i}{6}\alpha t^{3}+\tfrac1{24}\beta t^{4}
   -\tfrac1{72}\alpha^{2}t^{6}+\dots\Bigr]e^{R_5(t)},
\]
the sixth-order term being $\tfrac12(-\tfrac{i}{6}\alpha t^{3})^{2}$. Since
$(it)^{3}=-it^{3}$, $(it)^{4}=t^{4}$ and $(it)^{6}=-t^{6}$, the three bracket terms are
$\tfrac{\alpha}{6}(it)^{3}$, $\tfrac{\beta}{24}(it)^{4}$ and $\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{72}(it)^{6}$,
and Lemma~\ref{lem:heinv} sends them to $\tfrac{\alpha}{6}\mathrm{He}_3$,
$\tfrac{\beta}{24}\mathrm{He}_4$ and $\tfrac{\alpha^{2}}{72}\mathrm{He}_6$ against
$\varphi(z)$. The three Hermite terms are bounded on $|z|\le Z$ by
$\sup|\mathrm{He}_3|\le\min(3Z,2+Z^{3})$, $\sup|\mathrm{He}_4|\le3+6Z^{2}+Z^{4}$ and
$\sup|\mathrm{He}_6|\le15+45Z^{2}+15Z^{4}+Z^{6}$, each from the coefficients of the polynomial
and, for $\mathrm{He}_3$, from $\mathrm{He}_3(0)=0$. The factor $e^{R_5}-1$ contributes
$\int e^{-t^{2}/2}|R_5|$, bounded by Lemma~\ref{lem:sumrem}; $|e^{x}-1|\le2|x|$ for
$|x|\le1$ absorbs the exponentiation. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[what the budget is, in the size that matters]\label{rem:budgetsize}
Each of the four terms of $\varepsilon^{*}$ is $O(k^{-1})$ or smaller: $|\beta|\asymp k^{-1}$,
$\alpha^{2}\asymp k^{-1}$, $|\alpha|Z\asymp k^{-1.8}$ since $Z=W/\sigma\to0$
by~\textup{(H)}, and $\sum a^{5}/\sigma^{5}\asymp k^{-3/2}$. So $\varepsilon^{*}\to0$, which is
all Theorems~\ref{thm:rigid} and~\ref{thm:transfer} require of it.
\STATUS{experimentally confirmed: $\varepsilon^{*}$ bounds the true relative error of the
Gaussian, computed by exact dynamic programming over the window, at all sixteen test points
tried, with ratio $1.7$ to $6.5$ and tightening in $k$; and $k\varepsilon^{*}$ runs
$1.87,1.24,1.03,0.87$ at $k=100,200,300,450$. \texttt{r1step3\_r136}, \texttt{r1hermite\_r137}.}
\end{remark}
