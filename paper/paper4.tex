\begin{abstract}
Papers 1--3 of this series fixed both the sequence and the measure on its subsets. Here we
move each. Along the way the per-element energy $-\log|\cos\pi t|$ turns out to satisfy an exact
coarse-graining identity: its average over a coset of index $v$ is the same function at $v$ times
the frequency, plus $(1-1/v)\log2$. We prove it twice, and it is what the minor-arc argument of
this paper runs on. Replacing the uniform measure by a Bernoulli$(q)$ product measure deforms the gap
series into an explicit family $\Gamma^{(q)}$, and we prove that the fair coin minimises it
for every sequence, with equality only at $q=\tfrac12$; the argument is termwise and is
machine-checked in Lean~4. We derive the corresponding transfer function $\Phi^{(q)}$ and
show that the bias splits paper 3's~\cite{P3} symmetric pair of strata in the ratio $q:(1-q)$, so that
the biased landscape responds to a shift of target at first order; the resulting slope is a
point prediction with no free parameter, and it is confirmed against the natural alternative
to within $0.4\%$. Replacing the primes by a random odd sequence, we prove that the extremal
modulus of the minor arcs moves from $6$ to $4$, so that the peak height available to a random
odd sequence is $1/\sqrt2$ where the primes have $\sqrt3/2$ --- the primes are the harder
case. We then prove that the geometric \emph{rate} follows: almost surely the minor-arc maximum
of $F_A^{1/b}$ tends to $1/\sqrt2$. The proof carries no ineffective constant and needs no
equidistribution input, because the coarse-graining identity replaces the usual discrepancy
estimate by an equality. Two questions inherited from paper 3 are also settled or sharpened.
Every claim is tagged with its status, and the numerical work is reproducible from logged
scripts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Papers 1--3 fixed two things at once: the sequence $A$, and the measure on its subsets. Every
statement of the trilogy is about a specific $A$ under the uniform measure. This paper moves
each of them, one at a time, and the two moves turn out to be worth making for different
reasons.

\paragraph{Bias.} The classification theorem of Part~I~\cite{P1} says which elements lie on which side
of a target. It says nothing about how configurations are weighted, so the entire
combinatorial layer survives verbatim when the uniform measure is replaced by the
Bernoulli$(q)$ product measure, and the gap series deforms into an explicit one-parameter
family $\Gamma^{(q)}$ (\S\ref{sec:biased}). The answer has a shape worth putting in the
introduction: \textbf{the fair coin gives the least rugged landscape}, for every sequence, by
a termwise convexity argument that is machine-checked (Theorem~\ref{thm:fair},
Remark~\ref{rem:lean22}). The transfer function deforms too, and here the deformation is not
cosmetic: paper 3's $\cosh$ splits into two unequal exponentials with offsets in the ratio
$q:(1-q)$, so the biased landscape responds to a shift of target at \emph{first} order rather
than second (\S\ref{sec:phiq}). Rigidity off the fair coin is a tilt, not a plateau.

\paragraph{Coarse-graining.} \S\ref{sec:coset} is one lemma and it is the technical spine of the
paper. Averaging the energy $X(t)=-\log|\cos\pi t|$ over the coset $\{t+k/v\}$ returns
$X$ at $v$ times the frequency, plus $(1-1/v)\log2$, exactly (Lemma~\ref{lem:coset}, proved by
the multiplication formula and again by Fourier). Since $X\ge0$, the rational points are
therefore the \emph{minima} of the coset average, not merely convenient sample points
(Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}) --- so the step of a minor-arc argument that normally costs a
quantitative equidistribution estimate costs nothing here. Read as physics it is a block-spin
step with an exact fixed point; we say so once, in Remark~\ref{rem:renorm}, and deduce nothing
from the reading.

\paragraph{Randomness.} For a random odd sequence the minor-arc input that cost Part~II~\cite{P2} fifty
rounds is replaced by a concentration bound, and \S\ref{sec:random} carries
\emph{no ineffective constant at all} --- the first such statement in the series. What we did
not expect is that the random case is also \emph{easier}: the extremal modulus moves from $6$
to $4$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:modfour}), so the peak height drops from $\sqrt3/2$ to $1/\sqrt2$, and
the geometric rate improves in step (Theorem~\ref{prop:rate}). The modulus moves for a
one-line arithmetic reason --- a set of primes contains at most one element
$\equiv3\pmod6$, a random odd set contains a third of them --- and the consequence is that
Part~II's $\sqrt3/2$ is a property of the primes, not a limitation of the method.
\emph{The primes are the hard case.}

\paragraph{A methodological remark that this paper earned twice.} Deformations are not only a
way to generalise; they are a way to \emph{see}. \textbf{A deformation that breaks a symmetry
makes measurable everything the symmetry was hiding.} Constructing $\Gamma^{(q)}$ and
$\Phi^{(q)}$ audited paper 3 for free, twice. First, the $c_d$ fork: asking how the omitted
factor behaves as $k$ grows, rather than how large it is at one $k$, closed a question paper 3
had left open (\S\ref{sec:inherited}). Second, and more sharply, the sign of $\lambda$: paper
3's $\Phi$ is an \emph{even} function of $\lambda$, so no observable in that paper depends on
the sign, and a definition that carried the wrong one survived three papers and an external
referee. Splitting the $\cosh$ made the sign observable in a single measurement. We record the
principle because it is a design rule, not an anecdote: when a quantity cannot be checked,
deform the problem until it can.

\paragraph{Verification, and the genre.} Every numbered claim carries a status tag: proved,
Lean-verified, experimentally confirmed with the range stated, or conjectured. The scripts
that produce every number in this paper write logs beside themselves and are part of the
artefact. This is now a recognisable genre --- machine-assisted mathematics shipped with
formal certificates, of which \emph{Ten advances in mathematics and theoretical computer
science} \cite{TenProofs} and its accompanying Lean~4 repository are the visible precedent.
The direction differs: that work attacks named open problems, whereas the present series
builds a small theory from its definitions upward, so the certificates here anchor
\emph{definitions and structural lemmas} rather than headline resolutions. The verification
ethos is the same one, and we take it as read rather than arguing for it.

\section{An exact coarse-graining identity}\label{sec:coset}

Papers 1--3 borrowed the vocabulary of statistical physics --- landscape, energy, transfer
function, tilt --- and used it as a source of the right questions. This section is the first
place where the borrowing runs the other way and produces an exact recursion rather than an
analogy. Everything later in the paper that concerns minor arcs
(\S\ref{sec:modfour}, \S\ref{sec:rate}) is a corollary of it.

Throughout write
\[
   X(t)\;=\;-\log|\cos\pi t|\;\in[0,\infty],
\]
the per-element energy of papers 1--3 at phase $t$.

\begin{lemma}[coset identity]\label{lem:coset}
For every integer $v\ge1$ and every real $t$,
\[
   \frac1v\sum_{k=0}^{v-1}X\Bigl(t+\frac kv\Bigr)
   \;=\;\Bigl(1-\frac1v\Bigr)\log2\;+\;\frac1v\,X(vt+\tau_v),
   \qquad
   \tau_v=\begin{cases}0,&v\text{ odd},\\[2pt]\tfrac12,&v\text{ even}.\end{cases}
\]
Equivalently $\prod_{k<v}|\cos\pi(t+k/v)|=2^{\,1-v}\,|\cos\pi(vt+\tau_v)|$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[First proof (multiplication formula)]
Write $2\cos\pi z=2\sin\pi(z+\tfrac12)$ and apply
$\prod_{k<v}2\sin\pi(y+\tfrac kv)=2\sin(v\pi y)$ --- the factorisation of $x^{v}-1$ read on the
unit circle --- with $y=t+\tfrac12$. The product of the $2|\cos|$ is $2|\sin\pi(vt+\tfrac v2)|$,
which is $2|\cos\pi vt|$ for $v$ odd and $2|\sin\pi vt|=2|\cos\pi(vt+\tfrac12)|$ for $v$ even.
Divide by $2^{v}$ and take logarithms.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Second proof (Fourier)]
From $-\log|2\sin\pi u|=\sum_{n\ge1}\cos(2\pi nu)/n$ and $u=t+\tfrac12$,
\[
   X(t)=\log2+\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^n\cos(2\pi nt)}{n}.
\]
Averaging over the coset annihilates every harmonic but those with $v\mid n$, since
$v^{-1}\sum_{k<v}\cos(2\pi n(t+k/v))$ is $\cos(2\pi nt)$ when $v\mid n$ and $0$ otherwise.
Writing $n=vp$ and using $(-1)^{vp}=(-1)^p$ for $v$ odd and $1$ for $v$ even, the surviving
series is $v^{-1}(X(vt+\tau_v)-\log2)$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
Two proofs check the \emph{statement}; one proof checks only the argument. The identity is
verified in product form for $v\le60$ at $200$ random shifts each, worst discrepancy
$2.5\cdot10^{-16}$, and the two Fourier partial sums agree to $8.8\cdot10^{-14}$.
\STATUS{proved, twice; and checked numerically, \texttt{stab\_r110}, \texttt{rate\_r112}}

\begin{corollary}[the rational points are the minima]\label{cor:floor}
$\displaystyle\frac1v\sum_{k<v}X\bigl(t+\tfrac kv\bigr)\ \ge\ \Bigl(1-\frac1v\Bigr)\log2$
for every $t$, with equality exactly when $vt+\tau_v\in\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$X\ge0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[what is machine-checked in Lemma~\ref{lem:coset}, and what is not]\label{rem:leancoset}
The identity is \emph{multiplicative in $v$}, and that is what has been formalised. Writing
$k=i+aj$ and inducting on $b$ splits a product over $\{k<ab\}$ into a double product with no
bijection and no analysis --- \Lean{prod\_range\_mul\_index}, stated for an arbitrary commutative
monoid. On top of it, \Lean{cosetId\_one} and \Lean{cosetId\_two} are the base cases ($v=2$ is
the double-angle formula and needs nothing else), and \Lean{cosetId\_mul} carries the identity
from $a$ and $b$ to $ab$, including the offset bookkeeping
$a\tau_b+\tau_a\equiv\tau_{ab}\pmod 1$ in all four parity cases, via
\Lean{abs\_cos\_add\_int\_mul\_pi}.
\emph{What is not formalised is the base case at an odd prime}, which is the only place a
product over roots of unity is needed, and which Mathlib does not supply. So the machine-checked
statement is: \emph{the identity holds at $1$ and $2$ and is closed under products}, hence holds
for every $v$ built from the primes at which it is established by hand. The reduction was
checked numerically before it was formalised, including a deliberately wrong $\tau$ as a
control.
\STATUS{Lean-verified (the reduction; not the odd-prime base case);
\texttt{Pnp/Theory/CosetProd.lean}, \texttt{coset\_mult\_r116}}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[what this says, and in which language]\label{rem:renorm}
The identity has two readings and they have different statuses; the paper keeps them apart.

\emph{As arithmetic} (proved) it says that averaging $X$ over a coset of index $v$ returns
\emph{$X$ itself, at $v$ times the frequency}, plus a constant --- so the family
$\{X(v\,\cdot\,)\}$ is closed under coarse-graining, exactly, with no error term. That is what
Corollary~\ref{cor:floor} turns into a uniform lower bound, and it is why \S\ref{sec:rate} needs
no equidistribution input: the question ``how far can the mean fall when the rational grid is
shifted?'' has the answer \emph{it cannot fall at all}.

\emph{As physics} (an interpretation, and nothing is deduced from it) this is a block-spin step.
Coarse-graining the phase lattice by a factor $v$ maps the energy to a rescaled copy of itself;
the map has an exact fixed-point structure rather than an approximate one, and the constant
$(1-1/v)\log2$ is the free energy released per coarse-graining step, additive over successive
steps because $\tau$ composes. Papers 1--3 treated the landscape picture as a source of
questions; this is the first identity in the series that the picture predicts and the arithmetic
confirms. \S\ref{sec:outlook} says what we would want to do with it and does not do it here.
\end{remark}

\section{The biased invariant}\label{sec:biased}

Let $P_q$ be the product measure under which each $a_i\in A$ is present independently with
probability $q\in(0,1)$, and write $\lm_q(n)$, $\deg_q(n)$ for the $P_q$-measures of the sets
of strict local minima and of ground states at the target $n$. The target is taken at the
$q$-tilted centre $n_q=\lfloor qT\rceil$.

\begin{definition}[biased gap series]\label{def:gq}
\[
  \Gamma^{(q)}(A)\;=\;1+\sum_{d\ge1}\Bigl[q^{N_d}+(1-q)^{N_d}\Bigr].
\]
\end{definition}

At $q=\tfrac12$ every bracket is $2\cdot2^{-N_d}$ and $\Gamma^{(1/2)}$ is the window series
$W_D$ of Part~I~\cite{P1}, hence $\Gamma(A)+(2D+1)2^{-k}$.

\begin{remark}[why the classification survives the deformation]\label{rem:measurefree}
Part~I's classification is measure-free: at error $d$, an overshooting configuration is a
strict local minimum exactly when every $a\le2d$ is \emph{excluded}, and an undershooting one
exactly when every $a\le2d$ is \emph{included}. Under $P_q$ those two events have probability
$(1-q)^{N_d}$ and $q^{N_d}$, which is Definition~\ref{def:gq}. Writing
$r^{q}_B(m)=\sum_{U\subseteq B,\ \sigma(U)=m}q^{|U|}(1-q)^{|B|-|U|}$ and factorising $P_q$
across $I_d$ and $B_d$,
\[
  \mathrm{over}_q=(1-q)^{N_d}r^{q}_{B_d}(n+d),\quad
  \mathrm{under}_q=q^{N_d}r^{q}_{B_d}(n-d-\sigma_d),\quad
  \deg_q=\!\!\sum_{J\subseteq I_d}\!\!q^{|J|}(1-q)^{N_d-|J|}r^{q}_{B_d}(n-\sigma(J)),
\]
and the binomial sum over $J$ is exactly $1$, so a flat $r^{q}$ gives the $d$-th term of
Definition~\ref{def:gq}. \STATUS{derived; the assignment verified per stratum, see
Remark~\ref{rem:assign}}
\end{remark}

\subsection{The fair coin is the flattest}

\begin{lemma}[termwise minimality]\label{lem:termwise}
For every integer $N\ge0$ the function $f_N(q)=q^{N}+(1-q)^{N}$ on $(0,1)$ is symmetric about
$q=\tfrac12$ and convex, and $f_N(q)\ge f_N(\tfrac12)=2^{1-N}$, with strict inequality for
$q\ne\tfrac12$ as soon as $N\ge2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$f_N(1-q)=f_N(q)$ is immediate. For $N\ge2$,
$f_N'(q)=N\bigl[q^{N-1}-(1-q)^{N-1}\bigr]$ and
$f_N''(q)=N(N-1)\bigl[q^{N-2}+(1-q)^{N-2}\bigr]>0$, so $f_N$ is strictly convex; and
$t\mapsto t^{N-1}$ is strictly increasing, so $f_N'<0$ on $(0,\tfrac12)$ and $f_N'>0$ on
$(\tfrac12,1)$. For $N\in\{0,1\}$, $f_N$ is the constant $2$ or $1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[formally verified]\label{rem:lean22}
Lemma~\ref{lem:termwise} is machine-checked. \Lean{termwise\_min} gives the inequality for
every $N$ and every $q\in[0,1]$, \Lean{termwise\_min\_strict} its strictness for $N\ge2$ and
$q\ne\tfrac12$, and \Lean{termwise\_min\_iff} the equality case as a biconditional; the
normalisation $2\cdot(\tfrac12)^N=2^{1-N}$ is \Lean{two\_mul\_half\_pow}. The non-strict
bound is proved twice, once from convexity of $x^N$ on $[0,\infty)$ and once from Bernoulli's
inequality (\Lean{termwise\_min'}), so the two proofs check the \emph{statement} against each
other and not merely the tactic script. No \texttt{sorry}, and no axioms beyond
\texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice}, \texttt{Quot.sound}.
\STATUS{Lean-verified; \texttt{Pnp/Theory/TermwiseMin.lean}}
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[the fair coin minimises the invariant]\label{thm:fair}
For every finite increasing sequence $A$ of odd positive integers with $|A|\ge2$ and every
$q\in(0,1)$,
\[
  \Gamma^{(q)}(A)\;\ge\;\Gamma^{(1/2)}(A),
\]
with equality if and only if $q=\tfrac12$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Sum Lemma~\ref{lem:termwise} over $d$. For strictness it suffices that some $d\le D$ has
$N_d\ge2$: take any $d\ge(a_2-1)/2$, which is at most $D=(a_k-1)/2$ because $a_2\le a_k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[what the theorem says]\label{rem:physics}
$q(1-q)$ is the per-element variance of the coin. Biasing the coin in either direction
lowers that variance and raises the invariant: \textbf{the more predictable the coin, the more
rugged the landscape}. The effect is exactly symmetric in $q\leftrightarrow1-q$, as it must
be, since complementing every configuration exchanges $q$ with $1-q$ and $n$ with $T-n$.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[closed form for the odd numbers]\label{cor:oddsclosed}
For $A=(1,3,\dots,2k-1)$ one has $N_d=d$ for $d\le k$, so in the limit $k\to\infty$
\[
  \Gamma^{(q)}=1+\frac{q}{1-q}+\frac{1-q}{q}=\frac{1}{q(1-q)}-1\;\ge\;3,
\]
with equality only at $q=\tfrac12$: \emph{the invariant of the odd numbers is the reciprocal
of the coin's variance, minus one}. \STATUS{verified against the summed series to
$5.3\cdot10^{-15}$ over $q=0.10,\dots,0.80$; \texttt{e5\_r098}}
\end{corollary}

\subsection{The bias breaks the reflection symmetry}\label{sec:e6}

\STATUS{experimentally confirmed, shape} Off the $q$-tilted centre the deviation

because the two strata sit at $\pm\delta_d$ about a common centre --- the analytic form of

odd orders need not cancel, and the first-order coefficient is
\[
  L^{(q)}(A)\;=\;\sum_{d\ge1}\delta_d\Bigl[q^{N_d}-(1-q)^{N_d}\Bigr],

\begin{remark}[the range is part of the test]\label{rem:range}
The first measurement used offsets $x\in[0.04,0.20]$ and came out incoherent for the primes at
$q=\tfrac12$ (a quadratic spread of $15.4$). The reason is worth recording because it is a
property of ratio tests and not of this problem. At $q=\tfrac12$ the response is \emph{even} in
$x$, so $\mathrm{dev}-\mathrm{dev}_0$ cannot change sign for the reason under test; at finite
$k$ the vertex of the parabola sits at some $x_*\ne0$, and $\mathrm{dev}-\mathrm{dev}_0$ crosses
zero at $\pm x_*$. Measured, $x_*\approx0.03$, with the signs symmetric in $\pm x$ exactly as
that account predicts. A range containing the crossing makes \emph{both} ratios pass through
zero, so the spread of either is set by how close a grid point landed to $x_*$ --- an accident
of the grid. \textbf{A ratio test must not straddle a zero of its own numerator.} The table
above uses $x\in[0.10,0.30]$, outside $x_*$; the repair is the range, not the omission of the
offending point.
\end{remark}

\section{The biased transfer function}\label{sec:phiq}

\subsection{The sign of $\lambda$, which now matters}

Paper 3's transfer function is even in $\lambda$, so the sign of the slope is not observable
there. Splitting the $\cosh$ makes it observable, so we fix it first:
\[
  \lambda_q(n)\;=\;+\frac{d}{dm}\log r^{q}_A(m)\Big|_{m=n}
  \;=\;\tfrac14\log\frac{r^{q}_A(n+2)}{r^{q}_A(n-2)}+O(\cdot),
\]
positive below the biased centre. This is the convention under which $\lambda\approx s$;
paper 3's \Xlab{paper3}{def:slope} carried the opposite sign and has been corrected, with no
numerical consequence there for exactly the reason just given.

\subsection{The derivation}

Take $P_{q,s}$: element $a$ present with weight $q\,e^{-sa}$, absent with weight $1-q$, so
$p_a=q e^{-sa}/((1-q)+qe^{-sa})$ and $p_a=q-q(1-q)sa+O(s^2a^2)$. Two consequences, both
absent at $q=\tfrac12$:
\begin{itemize}
\item the per-element variance is $a^2p_a(1-p_a)=q(1-q)a^2(1+O(sa))$, so the layer weight of
      paper 3's \Xlab{paper3}{rem:mushift} becomes $e^{-\lambda^2q(1-q)s_d/2}$;
\item removing $I_d$ removes mean $q\sigma_d$, so the two strata no longer sit at $\pm\delta_d$
      about a common centre. They sit at
      \[
        \delta^{+}_d=d+q\sigma_d,\qquad -\delta^{-}_d,\quad \delta^{-}_d=d+(1-q)\sigma_d .
      \]
      \textbf{The bias splits the pair asymmetrically, in the ratio $q:(1-q)$.}
\end{itemize}
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phiq}
  \Phi^{(q)}(\lambda)\;=\;1+\sum_{d\ge1}
  \Bigl[(1-q)^{N_d}e^{\lambda\delta^{+}_d}+q^{N_d}e^{-\lambda\delta^{-}_d}\Bigr]
  e^{-\lambda^{2}q(1-q)s_d/2}.
\end{equation}
\STATUS{derived; three internal checks below are exact, the point prediction is verified}

Three checks are built in, and all three hold to machine zero ($0.00\cdot10^{0}$, two
profiles, $k=60$): $\Phi^{(q)}(0)=\Gamma^{(q)}$ of Definition~\ref{def:gq}; the
complementation symmetry $\Phi^{(q)}(\lambda)=\Phi^{(1-q)}(-\lambda)$, which holds because
$\delta^{+}(q)=\delta^{-}(1-q)$; and $\Phi^{(1/2)}$ is paper 3's $\Phi$ term by term.
\STATUS{experimentally confirmed; \texttt{e10\_r105}}

\subsection{Tilted rigidity}

\begin{proposition}[the biased landscape responds at first order]\label{prop:tilted}
$\displaystyle \Phi^{(q)\prime}(0)=\sum_{d\ge1}\Bigl[(1-q)^{N_d}\delta^{+}_d-q^{N_d}\delta^{-}_d\Bigr]$,
which vanishes identically at $q=\tfrac12$ and is non-zero otherwise. So rigidity at a biased
measure is not flatness: it is a \emph{tilt} of a definite slope, and the slope is a point
prediction with no free parameter.
\end{proposition}

\STATUS{derived and verified, not proved from a hypothesis-free statement: it is a consequence
of $\Phi^{(q)}$, itself derived by the partition-function argument of \S\ref{sec:phiq} and

\section{Random sequences, unconditionally}\label{sec:random}

Before the theorem, the floor it has to stand on. Let $A$ be drawn from the Cram\'er model on
the odd integers: each odd $m\in[3,N]$ is included independently with probability $2/\log m$,

instance to instance. That is deliberate: $kR_A(k)$ is the $k$-invariant quantity of paper~3's

\emph{(ii) The constant concentrates.} The spread across instances is under $5\%$ and falls

different object.

There is one place where the random model is \emph{not} like the primes, and it is the place
that matters for the minor arcs. Write
$h_d(q)=\max_{(r,q)=1}\bigl(\prod_{a\in B_d}|\cos(\pi ra/q)|\bigr)^{1/|B_d|}$.
Since $|\cos(\pi a/4)|=1/\sqrt2$ for \emph{every} odd $a$, the modulus-$4$ floor
$h_d(4)=1/\sqrt2$ is deterministic, not merely universal. Part~II~\cite{P2}'s vanishing lemma kills the
modulus-$2m$ peak ($m$ odd) as soon as $B_d$ contains an element $\equiv3\pmod 6$. A set of
primes contains exactly one such element, namely $3$, and $3$ leaves $B_d$ at $d\ge2$, so the
primes keep $h_d(6)=\sqrt3/2$; a random odd sequence contains about $|B_d|/3$ of them and
loses the peak at every $d$. Measured over $q\le120$ at $d=1,2,5$: every random instance has
$\max_{q\ge3}h_d(q)=1/\sqrt2$ attained at $q=4$, and the primes have $\sqrt3/2$ at $q=6$.

\noindent
So the random theorem should come out with the geometric rate $1/\sqrt2=0.7071$ per element
rather than the $\sqrt3/2=0.8660$ of Parts~I and~II~\cite{P1,P2}: \emph{the primes are the harder case, and
the modulus-$6$ ripple is a structural feature of the primes that the random model destroys.}

\subsection{The modulus-4 theorem}\label{sec:modfour}

The observation above is not an accident of the ensemble; it is a theorem about residues, and
it is the exact counterpart of Part~II's modulus-$6$ theorem for a different equidistribution
class. Part~II averages $|\cos|$ over the \emph{reduced} residues, which is the right class
for the primes. A random odd sequence is equidistributed over the \emph{odd} residues
instead, so the relevant quantity is a different one.

\begin{definition}\label{def:modd}
For $q\ge2$ let $R_q=\mathbb{Z}/q$ if $q$ is odd and $R_q=\{j:\ j\text{ odd}\}\subset
\mathbb{Z}/q$ if $q$ is even --- for $q$ even and $m$ odd, $m\bmod q$ is odd, so $R_q$ is the
set of residues an odd sequence can occupy. Put
\[
   M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)\;=\;\Bigl(\prod_{j\in R_q}\bigl|\cos(\pi j/q)\bigr|\Bigr)^{1/|R_q|}.
\]
For $r$ coprime to $q$ the map $j\mapsto jr$ permutes $R_q$, so $M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)$ does not
depend on the numerator of the rational $r/q$.
\end{definition}

Everything below is a corollary of the coset identity of \S\ref{sec:coset}, because
$\{j/q:j\in R_q\}$ \emph{is} a coset of $(1/v)\mathbb{Z}$.

\begin{corollary}[the identity in the form \S\ref{sec:rate} uses]\label{cor:cosetRq}
Let $q\ge2$, put $v=q$ for $q$ odd and $v=q/2$ for $q$ even, and let $\sigma=\tfrac12$ if
$q\equiv2\pmod 4$ and $\sigma=0$ otherwise. Then $|R_q|=v$ and, for every real $s$,
\[
   \prod_{j\in R_q}\Bigl|\cos\pi\Bigl(s+\frac jq\Bigr)\Bigr|
   \;=\;2^{\,1-v}\,\bigl|\cos\pi(vs+\sigma)\bigr| ,\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
   \frac1v\sum_{j\in R_q}X\Bigl(s+\frac jq\Bigr)\;\ge\;\Bigl(1-\frac1v\Bigr)\log2 .
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$\{j/q:j\in R_q\}$ is $\{k/v\}_{k<v}$ for $q$ odd and $\{\tfrac1{2v}+\tfrac kv\}_{k<v}$ for $q$
even. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} at $t=s$ and at $t=s+\tfrac1{2v}$ respectively. In the second
case the shift contributes $v\cdot\tfrac1{2v}=\tfrac12$ to the argument, so the total offset is
$\tau_v+\tfrac12$, which is an integer when $v$ is even and $\tfrac12$ when $v$ is odd; and $v$
odd with $q$ even is exactly $q\equiv2\pmod4$. The inequality is
Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[closed form]\label{lem:moddclosed}
$M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)=2^{1/q-1}$ for $q$ odd. For $q=2u$ even,
$M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)=2^{1/u-1}$ if $u$ is even, and $M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)=0$ if $u$ is odd.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $s=0$ in Corollary~\ref{cor:cosetRq}: the right-hand side is $2^{1-v}$ when $\sigma=0$ and
$0$ when $\sigma=\tfrac12$. Take $v$-th roots.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[a second proof of the odd case, with no roots of unity]\label{rem:doubleangle}
For $q$ odd the closed form also follows from the double-angle formula alone, and this is the
route we formalise. Let $P=\prod_{j=1}^{q-1}|\sin(\pi j/q)|$ and $C=\prod_{j=1}^{q-1}|\cos(\pi
j/q)|$. From $\sin2x=2\sin x\cos x$, $\prod_{j=1}^{q-1}|\sin(2\pi j/q)|=2^{q-1}PC$. Since $q$ is
odd, $j\mapsto 2j\bmod q$ is a bijection of $\{1,\dots,q-1\}$ and
$|\sin(2\pi j/q)|=|\sin(\pi\,(2j\bmod q)/q)|$, so the left side is $P$ again. As $P\ne0$,
$C=2^{1-q}$, and $\prod_{j\in R_q}|\cos(\pi j/q)|=C$ because the $j=0$ factor is $1$. Two proofs
of one load-bearing identity check the \emph{statement}; one proof checks only the argument.
This is the branch that is machine-checked, as \Lean{prod\_abs\_cos\_odd}; see
Remark~\ref{rem:leanmodfour}.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[the modulus-4 theorem]\label{thm:modfour}
$\displaystyle\max_{q\ge2}M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)=\frac1{\sqrt2}$, attained at $q=4$ and nowhere
else.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$v\mapsto2^{1/v-1}$ is strictly decreasing. For $q$ odd, $q\ge3$, Lemma~\ref{lem:moddclosed}
gives $M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)=2^{1/q-1}\le2^{-2/3}$. For $q\equiv2\pmod4$ it gives $0$. For
$q\equiv0\pmod4$, write $q=2u$ with $u$ even, $u\ge2$; then
$M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)=2^{1/u-1}\le2^{-1/2}$ with equality exactly when $u=2$, i.e.\ $q=4$. And
$2^{-2/3}<2^{-1/2}$.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:moddclosed} is verified against the product for all $q\le200$, worst discrepancy
$4.4\cdot10^{-16}$; Corollary~\ref{cor:cosetRq} is verified in its product form for $q\le120$ at
$400$ random shifts each, worst discrepancy $4.4\cdot10^{-16}$, and independently at $60$ decimal
digits, worst discrepancy $2.2\cdot10^{-61}$.
\STATUS{proved; and checked numerically, \texttt{mq4\_r107}, \texttt{stab\_r110}}

\emph{The maximisation is formally verified.} Taking the closed form of

a biconditional, \Lean{ModdCF\_lt\_of\_ne\_four} the strict bound off $q=4$ by a second route,
and \Lean{two\_rpow\_neg\_half} the normalisation $2^{-1/2}=1/\sqrt2$. This is the step where
an error would hide --- a case split over three residue classes and the comparison of

\emph{Of the closed form itself, the odd branch is now formalised and the even branch is not.}
\Lean{prod\_abs\_cos\_odd} proves $\prod_{j=1}^{q-1}|\cos(\pi j/q)|=(1/2)^{q-1}$ for every odd

\subsection{The rate, and what is still missing}\label{sec:rate}

\begin{theorem}[minor-arc rate for random odd sequences]\label{prop:rate}
Let $A$ be drawn from the Cram\'er model above and let
$F_A(\theta)=\prod_{a\in A}|\cos(\pi a\theta)|$. Then almost surely
\[
   \lim_{b\to\infty}\ \max_{\theta\ \mathrm{minor}}\ F_A(\theta)^{1/b}\;=\;\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]
Here \emph{minor} means $\|\theta\|\ge1/N$, with $\|\cdot\|$ the distance to the nearest integer;
Step~$2''$ shows that this radius is large enough, so nothing is left unowned.
\end{theorem}

\STATUS{proved, with every constant written out. The proof is the four steps below rather
than a \texttt{proof} environment, so it is worth saying here that it is one: no step is
deferred, no constant is fitted, and the only external input is the arithmetic half,
Theorem~\ref{thm:modfour}, which is proved in this paper. See Remark~\ref{rem:ratestatus} for
what an independent reader should check first.}

The arithmetic half is Theorem~\ref{thm:modfour}. Here is the rest, in the steps it
takes, with the constants named. Fix the Dirichlet parameter $Q=\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$
--- Step~2$'$ says why that value and not another --- and set
\[
   M=\log\frac{2Q}{\pi}+1,\qquad
   \boxed{\ \delta=\tfrac16\log 2=0.1155245\ldots\ }
\]
Write $X_m(\theta)=-\log|\cos(\pi m\theta)|\in[0,\infty]$, so that
$-\log F_A(\theta)=\sum_m\xi_mX_m(\theta)$ with $\xi_m$ the independent indicators of the
model. Only a \emph{lower} bound on this sum is wanted, which is what makes the argument easy.

\emph{Step 1 (truncate; the unbounded tail is free).} Put $X^{(M)}_m=\min(X_m,M)$. Then
$\sum\xi_mX_m\ge\sum\xi_mX^{(M)}_m$, and the summands now lie in $[0,M]$. At $\theta=\tfrac14$
truncation is inactive, since $X_m(\tfrac14)=\tfrac12\log2$ for every odd $m$.

\emph{Step 2 (the mean, and the constant $\delta$).} At $\theta=r/q$ the values $X_m$ over odd
$m$ run through $\{-\log|\cos(\pi j/q)|:j\in R_q\}$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:moddclosed} their
mean is $-\log M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)=(1-1/v)\log2$ and the \emph{gap} over the value $\tfrac12\log2$
at $q=4$ is
\[
   \delta(q)\;=\;\Bigl(\tfrac12-\tfrac1v\Bigr)\log 2 ,\qquad
   v=q\ (q\text{ odd}),\quad v=q/2\ (q\equiv0\bmod4),\quad \delta=\infty\ (q\equiv2\bmod4).
\]
This vanishes exactly at $v=2$, i.e.\ $q=4$, and increases with $v$; the smallest admissible
$v$ after $2$ is $v=3$. \textbf{So the binding competitor is $q=3$ and the gap is
$\delta=\tfrac16\log2$}, with $q=8$ next at $\tfrac14\log2$. Step~2$'$ will replace the entry
$\delta=\infty$ at $q\equiv2\pmod4$ by the finite value $(\tfrac12-\tfrac1u)\log2$: off the
rational point the vanishing residue no longer vanishes, and $q=6$ then \emph{ties} with $q=3$
at $\tfrac16\log2$. The constant is unchanged and two moduli bind it rather than one. The two
moduli with $v\le2$ --- $q=4$, where the floor is $\tfrac12\log2$ exactly and is meant to be, and
$q=2$, where $v=1$ and the floor degenerates to $0$ --- are handled in Step~$2''$, together with
$q=1$.
\STATUS{proved from Lemma~\ref{lem:moddclosed}; checked against the direct residue average for
all $q\le400$, worst discrepancy $1.0\cdot10^{-15}$, and the minimum located at $q=3$ over
$q\le2000$; \texttt{delta\_r109}}

Truncation does not eat this. For $q\not\equiv2\pmod4$ one has $|\cos(\pi j/q)|\ge\sin(\pi/2q)$,
so $\max_jX_m\le\log(2q/\pi)<M$ and truncation is \emph{inactive} at every $q\le Q$
--- measured, the largest value over all such $q\le1000$ is $6.4552$ against
$M-1=6.4562$, so the bound $\log(2Q/\pi)$ is sharp and the $+1$ in $M$ is there to keep the
argument off a knife edge. For $q=2u\equiv2\pmod4$ truncation \emph{is} active, at the single
odd residue $j=u$ where the cosine vanishes; putting $x=-1$ in $(x^u+1)/(x+1)$ gives
$\prod_{j\ne u}|\cos(\pi j/q)|=u\,2^{1-u}$, so the truncated mean is
$[(u-1)\log2-\log u+M]/u$, which exceeds $\tfrac12\log2+\delta$ by between $0.23$ and $1.25$
over $q\le198$ at $Q=200$.
\STATUS{experimentally confirmed, exact to $4.4\cdot10^{-16}$; \texttt{delta\_r109}}

\emph{Step 2$'$ (from the rationals to every $\theta$: there is no dent).} Write
$\theta=a/q+\beta$ with $q\le Q$ and $|\beta|\le1/(q(Q+1))$ (Dirichlet). The obstacle here looks
like a job for a quantitative equidistribution estimate --- Erd\H{o}s--Tur\'an or Koksma --- and
it is not: Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} settles it as an \emph{identity}. Since $j\mapsto ja$ permutes
$R_q$, the values $\{ja/q\}$ are the $\{j/q\}$, and for any common shift $s$
\[
   \frac1{|R_q|}\sum_{j\in R_q}X\Bigl(s+\frac jq\Bigr)
   \;=\;\underbrace{\Bigl(1-\frac1v\Bigr)\log2}_{-\log M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)}
   \;+\;\frac1v\,X(vs+\sigma)\;\ge\;-\log M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q),
   \tag{$\ast$}\label{eq:nodent}
\]
because $X\ge0$. \emph{Shifting the whole rational grid can only raise the mean}; the dent one
expects when $\theta$ moves off a rational does not exist, and no discrepancy theory is needed
to bound it. Equality in \eqref{eq:nodent} holds exactly on $vs+\sigma\in\mathbb Z$, so the
rational values are the true minima, not merely convenient sample points.
\STATUS{proved (Lemma~\ref{lem:coset}); the minimum over $s$ of the left side of
\eqref{eq:nodent} is checked to equal $-\log M_{\mathrm{odd}}(q)$ to $3\cdot10^{-16}$ for
$15$ moduli, and to survive truncation at $M$ for every $q\le1000$; \texttt{stab\_r110}}

First, truncation does not spoil \eqref{eq:nodent}. The loss $(X-M)^{+}$ is nonzero only within
$e^{-M}/\pi$ of a pole, and the coset points are $1/v\ge1/Q>1/(Qe)=2e^{-M}/\pi$ apart, so at most
one residue is affected; if it lies at distance $d$ from the pole then
$X\le\log\frac1{2d}$, while the excess term of \eqref{eq:nodent} is
$\tfrac1vX(vs+\sigma)\ge\tfrac1v\log\frac1{\pi vd}$ because $vs+\sigma$ sits at distance $vd$ from
a pole. The floor therefore survives as soon as $M\ge\log(\pi v/2)$, and with $M=\log(2Q/\pi)+1$
and $v\le Q$ that reads $1\ge\log(\pi^2/4)$ --- true, \textbf{with a margin of exactly
$1-\log(\pi^2/4)=0.0968$}. This is the third place the $+1$ in $M$ earns itself.
\STATUS{proved; and $\min_s\Phi^{(M)}_q(s)-(1-1/v)\log2$ is checked to be $\ge-5.6\cdot10^{-16}$
for every $q\le1000$; \texttt{stab\_r110}, \texttt{rate\_r112}}

\emph{Step $2'$ continued (the two error terms, in full).} What \eqref{eq:nodent} does not by
itself cover is that element $m$ of the class $j$ sits at $ja/q+m\beta$, so the shift varies
\emph{within} a class. Fix $q\notin\{1,2\}$ and group the odd $m\in[3,N]$ by residue class mod
$q$. In a class the $m$ form an arithmetic progression of common difference $2v$ (for $q$ odd the
odd numbers in a class mod $q$ step by $2q=2v$; for $q=2u$ they step by $q=2v$), so the phases
form an arithmetic progression of step $\eta=2v\beta$ sweeping an arc of length $L=N|\beta|$, and
the class starts at $x_j+m_j\beta$ where $m_j\le 2v$ is its first element. Three errors, and
nothing else:

\emph{(i) Progression versus integral.} $X^{(M)}$ is of bounded variation, at most $2M$ per
period, so for a progression of step $\eta$ over an arc of length $L$,
$\bigl|\sum_rX^{(M)}-\eta^{-1}\!\int\bigr|\le2M(L+1)$. There are $v$ classes and $\asymp N/2$
terms in all, so in the mean this costs at most $4qM/N+4M/(Q+1)$, using $v|\beta|\le1/(Q+1)$.

\emph{(ii) Jitter.} Replacing the arc of class $j$ by the common arc is a translation by
$\epsilon=m_j\beta$, and $m_j\le2v$ while the progression step is $\eta=2v\beta$, so
$|\epsilon|\le|\eta|$ \emph{exactly}. Translating by $\epsilon$ changes the integral by at most
$\epsilon V$, so the per-class error is at most $\epsilon V/\eta\le V\le2M(L+1)$ --- the same
shape as (i), and not a separate mechanism at all. Per element, $4qM/N+4M/(Q+1)$.

\emph{(iii) Unequal class counts.} They differ by at most $1$, costing $vM$ in the sum, i.e.\
$2qM/N$ in the mean.

After (i)--(iii) the integrand is the honest coset average, which is $\ge(1-1/v)\log2$ pointwise
by \eqref{eq:nodent}; integrating a pointwise inequality needs no further hypothesis, and in
particular \emph{no case distinction on the length of the sweep} --- which is what makes the
route robust in $Q$ where an arc-comparison design is not. Collecting,
\[
   \frac{\sum_m p_mX^{(M)}_m(\theta)}{\sum_m p_m}\;\ge\;\Bigl(1-\frac1v\Bigr)\log2
   \;-\;E,\qquad E\;\le\;\frac{10\,qM}{N}+\frac{8M}{Q+1}.
\]
\emph{Both terms are minimised together at $Q=\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$, where $E=O(M/\sqrt N)$} ---
which is why the Dirichlet parameter is $\sqrt N$ and not, as one first writes, $N$. At $Q=N$ the
argument fails twice over: the residue classes hold $O(1)$ elements each, and $|\beta|\le1/(qN)$
makes every sweep shorter than a single period.

\emph{The weights need no argument at all.} $p_m=2/\log m$ is \emph{decreasing}, so by Abel
summation a bound valid for every prefix $\{m\le N'\}$ passes to the $p$-weighted mean unchanged:
if $S(N')\ge c\,P(N')-\mathcal{E}(N')$ for all $N'$, then
$\sum p_mX^{(M)}_m\ge c\sum p_m-\sum_k(p_k-p_{k+1})\mathcal{E}(k)-p_{N}\mathcal{E}(N)$, and with
$\mathcal{E}(N')\le C M(\sqrt N+N'/\sqrt N)$ the subtracted term is
$O(M\sqrt N)+O(M/\sqrt N)\sum p_m$, i.e.\ $O(M\log N/\sqrt N)$ relative. There is no block-weight
condition to check.
\STATUS{proved; the shortfall at the binding modulus $q=3$ is measured as
$1.9\cdot10^{-3},5.8\cdot10^{-4},1.7\cdot10^{-4},4.8\cdot10^{-5}$ at
$N=2001,8001,32001,128001$, and $\mathrm{deficit}\cdot N/(qM)$ is

\emph{Step $2''$ (the three moduli with $v\le2$, and the major arc).} Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}
gives $\tfrac12\log2$ at $q=4$ ($v=2$) and nothing at all at $q\in\{1,2\}$ ($v=1$). The last two
are not prose; they are the following lemma, and $q=4$ needs no more than the identity because
$\tfrac12\log2$ is exactly the value the theorem is about.

\begin{lemma}[the degenerate moduli]\label{lem:vone}
Let $\phi(w)=-\log|\sin\pi w|$ and $L>0$. Then
$\frac1L\int_0^L\phi=\log2+\mathrm{Cl}_2(2\pi\{L\})/(2\pi L)$, and
\[
   \frac1L\int_0^L\phi\;>\;\tfrac12\log2\quad\text{for every }L>0,
   \qquad
   \frac1L\int_0^L X\;\ge\;\log2-\frac{\mathrm{Cl}_2(\pi/3)}{2\pi}=0.5316\quad\text{for }L\ge1.
\]
Consequently the mean over a $q=2$ sweep exceeds $\tfrac12\log2$, and so does the mean over a
$q=1$ sweep once $L=N\|\theta\|\ge1$ --- which is exactly the definition of \emph{minor}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The closed form is $\int_0^x\phi=x\log2+\mathrm{Cl}_2(2\pi x)/(2\pi)$ together with the
periodicity of $\phi$. For $L\le\tfrac12$: $\phi$ is convex on $(0,1)$, so the average over
$[0,L]$ is at least the midpoint value $-\log\sin(\pi L/2)\ge-\log\sin(\pi/4)=\tfrac12\log2$; at
the endpoint $L=\tfrac12$ the two-point midpoint bound $\tfrac12[\phi(L/4)+\phi(3L/4)]=0.51986$
shows the inequality is strict there. For $L\ge\tfrac12$: $1/(2\pi L)\le1/\pi$ and
$|\mathrm{Cl}_2|\le\mathrm{Cl}_2(\pi/3)$, so the average is at least
$\log2-\mathrm{Cl}_2(\pi/3)/\pi=0.3701>\tfrac12\log2$. For $q=1$ the same computation with $X$
in place of $\phi$ gives $\log2+\mathrm{Cl}_2(2\pi L+\pi)/(2\pi L)$, and $L\ge1$ makes the
correction at most $\mathrm{Cl}_2(\pi/3)/(2\pi)=0.1615$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
The true $q=2$ floor is $0.4945$ at $L=0.7908$ --- above $\tfrac12\log2+\delta$, not merely above
$\tfrac12\log2$ --- but that is measured, not proved, and the theorem does not need it. The
smallest $L$ at which the $q=1$ average reaches $\tfrac12\log2+\delta$ is $0.4528$, so the radius
$1/N$ in the definition of \emph{minor} is comfortable rather than tight.
\STATUS{proved; the Clausen closed form checked against direct quadrature to $4.5\cdot10^{-5}$,
and the convexity bound verified at $5000$ values of $L$; \texttt{rate\_r112}}

\emph{Step 3 (concentration).} The summands are independent and lie in $[0,M]$, and there are
$n=\lfloor(N-1)/2\rfloor$ of them, so Hoeffding gives
$\Pr[\sum\xi_mX^{(M)}_m<\mathbb{E}-t]\le\exp(-2t^2/(nM^2))$. Take $t=\varepsilon\,\mathbb{E}|A|$
and use $\mathbb{E}|A|\asymp2N/\log N$, $n\asymp N/2$, $Q=\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$ and hence
$M\asymp\tfrac12\log N$: the failure probability at one $\theta$ is
\[
   \exp\bigl(-c\,\varepsilon^2N/\log^4N\bigr),\qquad c=64 .
\]
Two readings of the same bound, and the theorem uses the second. With $\varepsilon=\tfrac12\delta$
this is the fixed-margin statement, $\exp(-cN/\log^4N)$ with $c=16\delta^2=0.2135$. But the
theorem only needs the mean to survive to within $o(1)$, so one may instead take
$\varepsilon=N^{-1/4}$, which still leaves the exponent $\asymp\sqrt N/\log^4N\to\infty$.
\emph{That is why $q\in\{1,2\}$ need only clear $\tfrac12\log2$ and not $\tfrac12\log2+\delta$},
and it is what makes Lemma~\ref{lem:vone} sufficient. No second moment of an exponential sum
appears: the randomness is in the \emph{sequence}, not in the phases.

\emph{Step 4 (net, then Borel--Cantelli).} Where $X_m<M$ we have $|\cos(\pi m\theta)|\ge e^{-M}$,
hence $|\partial_\theta X^{(M)}_m|=\pi m|\tan(\pi m\theta)|\le\pi Ne^{M}\le2eNQ\le2eN^{3/2}$, so
a net of spacing $\varrho/(2eN^{3/2}b)$ suffices and has $O(N^{5/2}/\varrho)$ points. Since
$N^{5/2}\exp(-c\varepsilon^2N/\log^4N)$ is summable in $N$ for $\varepsilon=N^{-1/4}$, the union
bound and Borel--Cantelli give the almost-sure statement.

\emph{Assembling.} Steps 1--4 give, almost surely and uniformly over minor $\theta$,
$b^{-1}\sum_{a\in A}X_a(\theta)\ge\tfrac12\log2-o(1)$, i.e.\
$\max_{\theta\ \mathrm{minor}}F_A(\theta)^{1/b}\le2^{-1/2}(1+o(1))$: at $q=4$ the floor is
$\tfrac12\log2$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:floor}, at every other $q\ge3$ it is $\tfrac12\log2+\delta$,
at $q\in\{1,2\}$ it is above $\tfrac12\log2$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:vone}, and the error of Step $2'$
is $O(M\log N/\sqrt N)$. The matching lower bound is free: $\theta=\tfrac14$ is minor and
$X_a(\tfrac14)=\tfrac12\log2$ for \emph{every} odd $a$, so $F_A(\tfrac14)^{1/b}=2^{-1/2}$
identically. Hence the limit is $2^{-1/2}$. \qed

\begin{remark}[what ``proved'' means here, and what it does not]\label{rem:ratestatus}
Theorem~\ref{prop:rate} carries no hypothesis and every constant in its proof is written down.
One qualification, stated here rather than left for a referee to raise. The three error terms of
Step~2$'$ are each reduced to a standard estimate --- a Riemann-sum comparison for a function of
bounded variation, and Abel summation against a monotone weight --- which we state and apply
in the form we need but do not prove; a referee would be within rights to ask for them in full,
and doing so would lengthen Step~2$'$ without changing any constant. \emph{No hypothesis is not
the same as written out in full}, a distinction Part~II now also makes explicitly
about its own main theorem, and the two should be read the same way.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[$\theta=\tfrac14$ is the limit of the maxima, not a maximum]\label{rem:notmax}
A first scan of $F_A(\theta)^{1/b}$ over a fine net asked whether anything exceeds $1/\sqrt2$,
and found that something does. That is not a counterexample and the reason is worth stating,
because it fixes the form of Theorem~\ref{prop:rate}: the maximum is \emph{approached} at
$\tfrac14$, and the statement is about the limit of $\max_\theta F_A^{1/b}$, not about where a
maximum sits at finite $b$. Moving off $\tfrac14$ by $\delta$
changes $\log F_A^{1/b}$ by $\delta\,b^{-1}\sum_a(\pm\pi a)+O(\delta^{2})$, and the signs
depend on $a\bmod 4$, so the first-order term is a random walk of size $b^{-1}\sqrt{S_2}$.
Optimising $\delta$ gives an excess of $O(1/b)$ in the exponent --- a \emph{bounded
multiplicative factor} on $F_A$, not a geometric one. Measured over

$9.0\cdot10^{-4}$, $1.8\cdot10^{-3}$, $1.1\cdot10^{-4}$, $1.2\cdot10^{-3}$, and
$b\log(\sqrt2\max_\theta F_A^{1/b})$ stays in $[0.05,0.89]$ --- $O(1)$, as derived. The
argmax sits at $\theta=0.24996,\dots,0.25001$.

\section{Where this sits, and the algorithmic reading}\label{sec:outlook}

\subsection{Iterating the coarse-graining}

Lemma~\ref{lem:coset} is used once, in one direction, and that is not the only thing it says.
Written as an operator, $(\mathcal{C}_vf)(t)=v^{-1}\sum_{k<v}f(t+k/v)$ acts on $X$ by
$\mathcal{C}_vX=(1-1/v)\log2+v^{-1}X(v\,\cdot+\tau_v)$, so $X$ spans a one-dimensional affine
invariant subspace of every $\mathcal{C}_v$ at once, and the constants compose:
$\mathcal{C}_{v'}\mathcal{C}_v=\mathcal{C}_{vv'}$ on this subspace, with released constants
adding as $(1-1/v)+v^{-1}(1-1/v')=1-1/(vv')$. Three questions we have not attempted, listed so
that the omission is deliberate rather than silent. \emph{(i)} Which energies other than $X$ have
this property --- is $-\log|\cos|$ characterised by being an exact fixed point of the family
$\{\mathcal{C}_v\}$ up to affine terms? \emph{(ii)} Is there a transfer-operator reading in which
$\Phi$ of paper~3 is the spectral object and $\mathcal{C}_v$ the renormalisation map? \emph{(iii)}
Papers 1--3's landscape language was a source of questions; here it produced an identity. Does the
same happen for the \emph{biased} energy of \S\ref{sec:phiq}, where the coset structure is
weighted by $q$?
\STATUS{conjectural directions; nothing in this paper depends on any of them}

\subsection{Related work}

Subset sums and number partitioning have a large landscape literature, and it is worth saying
precisely where the present series does and does not meet it. That literature is
overwhelmingly about \emph{random instances}: the phase transition and barrier-tree statistics
of the partitioning landscape, the local-REM description of the near-optimal spectrum, and
more recently overlap-gap obstructions for local algorithms. Its objects are the energy
spectrum and the dynamics; its randomness is in the instance; and its answers are laws for a
typical instance.

Papers 1--3 do something different in kind: they fix the sequence and compute an
\emph{arithmetic invariant of that set}, $\Gamma(A)=1+2\sum_d2^{-\#\{a\in A:\,a\le2d\}}$,
which the ratio
$\lm/\deg$ converges to for \emph{every} target. Nothing is averaged over instances, and the
answer is a number one reads off $A$. The present paper's \S\ref{sec:random} is the one place
where the two meet --- there the sequence is random --- and even there the question is the
ratio's limit rather than the spectrum's law.

One vocabulary warning, since the words collide. ``Biased Bernoulli measure'' is established
terminology in the theory of Bernoulli convolutions, where it denotes a self-similar measure
on $\mathbb{R}$; that is a different object from $P_q$ here, and we do not claim the term.
Within the subset-sum landscape literature we have not found the biased deformation
$\Gamma^{(q)}$ of \S\ref{sec:biased}, nor the odd-residue extremal quantity
$M_{\mathrm{odd}}$ of \S\ref{sec:modfour}, under any name.
\STATUS{literature pass, not a proof of novelty: absence of evidence over the searches made}

\subsection{The algorithmic reading}

This programme began with local search on subset-sum instances, and the invariant answers a
question one asks there. If a local-search procedure ends at a uniformly chosen strict local
minimum, the expected number of attempts before landing on a ground state is $\lm/\deg$, and
papers 1--3 predict that this number converges to $\Gamma(A)$ --- \emph{computable from $A$
alone, without solving a single instance, and independent of the target}. For the odd numbers
it is $3$; for the primes, $5.3492\ldots$ Two conditions are attached to that sentence and we
attach them here rather than leaving them in paper~3: the convergence is a
\emph{proof skeleton} there, not a theorem (paper~3, Theorem~\Xref{paper3}{thm:rigid}{3.1},
whose missing ingredient is
named in its own statement), and the uniformity of the terminal distribution is an assumption
about the search, not a fact about the landscape.

Theorem~\ref{thm:fair} is the part of this subsection that has a proof, and it is a statement
about \emph{sampling}, not about search dynamics: drawing configurations with a biased coin
instead of a fair one multiplies the ratio by $\Gamma^{(q)}/\Gamma\ge1$, strictly for
$q\ne\tfrac12$. So a natural one-parameter family of sampling rules cannot beat the fair coin
on this statistic --- which is a claim about the statistic, and becomes a claim about a
restart heuristic only under the uniformity assumption just named.
Section~\ref{sec:phiq} adds that the biased landscape also acquires a \emph{first-order}
sensitivity to the target that the fair one does not have.

Behind the uniformity assumption is the reason this is an outlook and not a theorem about
algorithms: a count of local minima is not a distribution over basins of attraction, and if
the basins are very unequal the ratio bounds the wrong quantity. Nor does anything here
address dynamics --- barrier heights, escape times, or the behaviour of any particular
heuristic. What the invariant supplies is a target-independent constant that any such analysis
has to be consistent with, together with a proved statement that one obvious way of trying to
improve that constant does not improve it.
