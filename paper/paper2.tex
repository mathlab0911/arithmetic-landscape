\begin{abstract}
Let $B$ be a finite set of odd primes and $r_B(m)$ the number of subsets of $B$
summing to $m$. The companion paper reduces the asymptotic behaviour of the
subset-sum landscape of $B$ to a single quantity, the \emph{flatness}
$\varepsilon_d$, which measures the pointwise oscillation of $r_B$ on a bounded
window. This paper develops the Fourier-side input needed to bound
$\varepsilon_d$: we determine the complete spectrum of sub-peaks of the
characteristic product $F_B$, we prove that modulus $6$ is the unique maximiser
of the per-element sub-peak height, and we show that for a truncated set of
primes an entire family of sub-peaks vanishes \emph{identically}. Concretely,
the peak at modulus $q=2m$ with $m$ odd is exactly $0$ whenever $m\in B$; for
$B$ a set of primes this kills every modulus $2p$ with $p\in B$, so that the
only competitors of the modulus-$6$ peak are the modulus-$4$ floor
$(1/\sqrt2)^{|B|}$, which never vanishes, and a finite list depending on the
truncation level. We also derive and verify numerically a second-order formula
for the modulus-$6$ ripple, giving amplitude and phase to within $0.1\%$ over
$k=18,\dots,76$. Combining these with the deep-minor bound of
Section~\ref{sec:l5c}, which is proved here, we obtain $\varepsilon_d(k)\to0$
at a geometric rate of at most $\sqrt3/2$ per element, and hence
$\mathrm{lm}_P(n)/\deg_P(n)\to\Gamma(P)=5.3492879\ldots$ for the odd primes; this
answers Problem 10.1 of the companion paper.

Two things about the status of that theorem, stated here rather than left to be
discovered. \emph{It carries no hypothesis}: earlier versions assumed the
deep-minor bound, and it is now Proposition~\ref{prop:deepminor}. But no
hypothesis is not the same as written out in full, and two steps of that
proposition are not yet written to referee standard --- the substitution of the
quoted exponential-sum bound in step~2, and the excision in step~4 of
Section~\ref{sec:l5c}. Both are flagged where they occur and tracked in
Section~\ref{sec:remains}; neither is believed to need a new idea, and until
they are written the theorem should be read as a complete argument with two
steps still owed to the reader. \emph{It is also ineffective}, at exactly one
point: the Siegel--Walfisz constant, which Appendix~\ref{sec:constants} isolates
as the only ineffective constant in the paper. Key steps are formally verified
in Lean~4.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The quantity to be bounded}

For a finite set $B$ of odd positive integers write
$r_B(m)=\#\{S\subseteq B:\sum S=m\}$, $T=\sum B$, and
\[
  F_B(\theta)=\prod_{a\in B}\bigl(1+e^{ia\theta}\bigr),
  \qquad
  r_B(m)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F_B(\theta)e^{-im\theta}\,d\theta .
\]
The companion paper shows that the ratio of the number of strict local minima to
the number of ground states of the subset-sum landscape converges to an explicit
arithmetic invariant provided the \emph{flatness}
\[
  \varepsilon_d=\max_{m,m'\in\mathrm{Win}_d}
      \Bigl|\frac{r_{B_d}(m)}{r_{B_d}(m')}-1\Bigr|
\]
tends to $0$, where $B_d$ is the subsequence of elements exceeding $2d$ and
$\mathrm{Win}_d$ is a bounded window about $T/2$. Bounding $\varepsilon_d$ is a
circle-method problem: the main arc at $\theta\approx0$ gives a smooth Gaussian
term, and every deviation from smoothness is carried by the sub-peaks of
$|F_B|$ at the rationals $\theta=2\pi j/q$.

\subsection{Results}

Section~\ref{sec:m1} determines the sub-peak spectrum. Writing
$G_B(\theta)=\prod_{a\in B}\cos(a\theta/2)$, so that $|F_B|=2^{b}|G_B|$, where $b=|B|$, the
natural per-element height at modulus $q$ is
\[
  M(q)=\Bigl(\prod_{\substack{1\le r\le q\\ \gcd(r,q)=1}}|\cos(\pi r/q)|\Bigr)^{1/\varphi(q)} .
\]
Proposition~\ref{thm:cyc} evaluates $M(q)$ in closed form, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:six} shows that $\sqrt3/2$ is its supremum, attained only at
$q=6$.

The novelty of Section~\ref{sec:vanish} is that $M(q)$, being an upper bound
valid for equidistributed sequences, is far from what a set of primes actually
realises. Lemma~\ref{lem:vanish} shows the peak at $q=2m$ ($m$ odd) is
\emph{exactly zero} as soon as $m\in B$; Corollary~\ref{cor:spectrum} converts
this into the surviving spectrum of a truncated prime set. The consequence for
the circle method is that the minor arcs carry, up to a fixed finite list, only
two live peaks.

Section~\ref{sec:l3} derives a second-order formula for the modulus-$6$ ripple,
with amplitude correction $K_2$ and phase drift $\psi$; Section~\ref{sec:m2}
treats the main arc and identifies the first correction to the central
density, resolving an unexplained constant of the companion paper.
Section~\ref{sec:num}
reports the numerical verification, Section~\ref{sec:l5a} makes the finite list
of remaining moduli effective, Section~\ref{sec:l5c} treats the deep minor arcs,
and Section~\ref{sec:t2main} assembles the main theorem.

\subsection{Relation to the literature}\label{sec:lit}

Three bodies of work are close enough to require comment.

\emph{Oscillations in partition counts.} Murthy and Brack~\cite{MB26} give a
semiclassical, periodic-orbit account of oscillations in partition counting
functions; conceptually this is the same mechanism as ours, a few isolated
saddle contributions producing a visible ripple. Their detailed oscillation
analysis is for partitions into distinct \emph{squares}, where the relevant
orbits are indexed by Pythagorean triples, and their treatment of prime
partitions (their \S7) is asymptotic rather than oscillatory. The limit also
differs: they let the integer being partitioned tend to infinity with the part
set fixed, whereas we let the \emph{number of parts} $k$ tend to infinity.
The dictionary is one line: their periodic orbits are our sub-peaks at rational
$\theta=2\pi j/q$. We would add that the question they leave open --- why regular
oscillations survive only for certain special sequences --- has, in the
subset-sum setting, the exact answer given by Lemma~\ref{lem:vanish} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:spectrum}: an oscillation at modulus $q$ survives precisely
when $q$ avoids the cyclotomic vanishing $q=2m$ with $m\in B$, and the surviving
heights are the $M(q)$ of Proposition~\ref{thm:cyc}.

\emph{Smoothness of partition functions.} Bateman and
Erd\H{o}s~\cite{BE56} prove $p_A(n-1)/p_A(n)\to1$ for a \emph{fixed infinite}
set $A$ of parts with \emph{repetitions allowed}. Our situation differs in both
respects: our part set is a finite truncation that grows with $k$, and parts are
used at most once, so the generating function is $\prod_{a\in B}(1+x^{a})$
rather than $\prod_{a\in A}(1-x^{a})^{-1}$. We do not see how to deduce the
finite-truncation statement from theirs. The same remark applies to Roth and
Szekeres~\cite{RS54} and to Vaughan~\cite{V08}, both of which treat the fixed
infinite set of all primes.

\emph{Landscapes of the number partitioning problem.} Ferreira and
Fontanari~\cite{FF98} compute the fraction of metastable states --- that is,
$\mathrm{lm}/2^{k}$ --- analytically for uniform random weights; Alyahya and
Rowe~\cite{AR14} give a formula in terms of the coefficient of variation;
Stadler, Hordijk and Fontanari~\cite{SHF03} analyse barrier trees. All of these
average over \emph{random} weights, whereas the companion paper and this one
treat a \emph{specific} sequence and an order-sensitive invariant.

Finally, the anti-concentration literature (Littlewood--Offord and its inverse
forms) bounds the \emph{maximum} of $r_B$; what we need is a two-sided bound on
the \emph{ratio} at nearby points, which does not follow from a one-sided bound.

\section{The sub-peak spectrum}\label{sec:m1}

The two statements of this section are classical: Proposition~\ref{thm:cyc} is
the resultant $\mathrm{Res}(\Phi_q,x+1)$ written out, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:phivals} is a standard exercise. We record both with proofs
because we use the precise constants, and because the interpretation of $M(q)$
as the per-element height of a sub-peak of a subset-sum generating function
seems not to have been made before.

\begin{proposition}[Cyclotomic evaluation; classical]\label{thm:cyc}
For every integer $q\ge3$,
\[
  M(q)=\tfrac12\,|\Phi_q(-1)|^{1/\varphi(q)},
\]
where $\Phi_q$ is the $q$-th cyclotomic polynomial.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/q}$. For $1\le r\le q-1$ we have
$|1+\zeta^{r}|=2|\cos(\pi r/q)|$. Since
$\Phi_q(x)=\prod_{\gcd(r,q)=1}(x-\zeta^{r})$, evaluating at $x=-1$ gives
$\prod_{\gcd(r,q)=1}|1+\zeta^{r}|=|\Phi_q(-1)|$, hence
$\prod_{\gcd(r,q)=1}|\cos(\pi r/q)|=2^{-\varphi(q)}|\Phi_q(-1)|$. Take
$\varphi(q)$-th roots.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Classical values]\label{lem:phivals}
For $q\ge3$ one has $\Phi_q(-1)=p$ if $q=2p^{j}$ with $p$ an odd prime and
$j\ge1$; $\Phi_q(-1)=2$ if $q=2^{j}$ with $j\ge2$; and $\Phi_q(-1)=1$
otherwise.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We use three standard identities: $\Phi_n(1)=p$ if $n=p^{j}$ is a prime power
and $\Phi_n(1)=1$ if $n$ has at least two distinct prime factors; for odd
$n\ge3$, $\Phi_{2n}(x)=\Phi_n(-x)$; and for even $m$, $\Phi_{2m}(x)=\Phi_m(x^2)$.
If $q$ is odd then $\Phi_q(-1)=\Phi_{2q}(1)=1$ because $2q$ has two distinct
prime factors. If $q=2m$ with $m$ odd then $\Phi_q(-1)=\Phi_m(1)$, which is $p$
when $m=p^{j}$ and $1$ otherwise. If $4\mid q$, write $q=2m$ with $m$ even; then
$\Phi_q(-1)=\Phi_m(1)$, which equals $2$ exactly when $m$ is a power of $2$,
i.e.\ when $q=2^{j}$, and equals $1$ otherwise.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Extremality of modulus six]\label{thm:six}
$\sup_{q\ge3}M(q)=\sqrt3/2$, attained only at $q=6$. The second largest value is
$M(10)=5^{1/4}/2\approx0.7477$, and $M(q)\le1/\sqrt2$ for every
$q\notin\{6,10\}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{thm:cyc} and Lemma~\ref{lem:phivals}, $M(q)=1/2$ unless
$q=2p^{j}$ or $q=2^{j}$. For $q=2^{j}$ ($j\ge2$) we get
$M=\frac12\cdot2^{1/2^{j-1}}$, which is decreasing in $j$ with maximum
$1/\sqrt2$ at $q=4$. For $q=2p^{j}$ we get
$M=\frac12\,p^{1/(p^{j-1}(p-1))}$; the exponent decreases in $j$, so the maximum
over the family occurs at $j=1$, where $M=\frac12p^{1/(p-1)}$. The map
$p\mapsto(\log p)/(p-1)$ is strictly decreasing for $p\ge3$, since its
derivative has the sign of $(p-1)/p-\log p<0$ (as $\log p\ge\log3>1>(p-1)/p$).
Hence the values in this family are
$\sqrt3/2>5^{1/4}/2>7^{1/6}/2\approx0.6915>\cdots$, and for $j\ge2$ the largest
is $q=18$ with $\frac12 3^{1/6}\approx0.6005$. Combining the three families
gives the statement.
\end{proof}

at the twelfth power, where all the relevant values are rational.)

\section{The vanishing family and the surviving spectrum}\label{sec:vanish}

\begin{lemma}[Exact vanishing]\label{lem:vanish}
Let $q=2m$ with $m$ odd. If $m\in B$ then $F_B(e^{2\pi i/q})=0$; equivalently
$G_B(2\pi/q)=0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The factor of $F_B$ indexed by $m$ is $1+e^{i\pi}=0$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
(Lean: \Lean{F\_eq\_zero\_of\_mem} for the product form, \Lean{F\_eq\_zero\_at\_two\_pi\_div}
for the statement at $\theta=2\pi/q$, \Lean{F\_nat\_eq\_zero\_of\_mem} for integer
sequences, and \Lean{not\_mem\_of\_F\_ne\_zero} for the contrapositive. The
special case $q=6$, $m=3$ is \Lean{normSq\_at\_pi} of the companion paper.)

The point of Lemma~\ref{lem:vanish} is its converse for prime sequences: the
residue class $a\equiv m\pmod{2m}$ consists of odd multiples of $m$, so if $B$
is a set of primes and $m>1$ then $B$ meets that class if and only if $m$ itself
lies in $B$. This gives a complete description.

\begin{corollary}[Surviving spectrum]\label{cor:spectrum}
Let $B_d$ be the set of primes in $(2d,x]$ and $b=|B_d|$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $|G_{B_d}(2\pi/6)|=(\sqrt3/2)^{b}$ \emph{exactly}, provided $d\ge2$ so that
      $3\notin B_d$; no equidistribution is needed, because both classes
      $\pm1\pmod 6$ give $|\cos(\pi a/6)|=\sqrt3/2$.
\item $|G_{B_d}(2\pi/4)|=(1/\sqrt2)^{b}$ \emph{exactly}, for every odd sequence
      whatsoever, since $|\cos(\pi a/4)|=1/\sqrt2$ for all odd $a$. This is the
      Fourier form of the modulus-$4$ obstruction of the companion paper.
\item $|G_{B_d}(2\pi/(2p))|=0$ exactly, for every prime $p$ with $2d<p\le x$.
      The vanishing is \emph{pointwise} and must not be read as the absence of a
      peak: one factor is removed at one point, and the surrounding mass is
      still $(M(2p)+o(1))^{b}$. The two maxima flanking the resulting notch sit
      at height $(M(2p)+o(1))^{b}b^{-3/2}$, the notch having width $\asymp1/p$
      --- see Remark~\ref{rem:notch}, where this is measured and matched against
      Lemma~\ref{lem:annulus}.
\item For the finitely many primes $p\le 2d$ the peak at $q=2p$ survives, at
      height $(M(2p)+o(1))^{b}\le(5^{1/4}/2+o(1))^{b}$ by the prime number
      theorem in arithmetic progressions.
\item For $q=2m$ with $m$ odd composite, and for $q=2^{j}$ with $j\ge3$, the
      height is $\le(M(q)+o(1))^{b}\le(\tfrac12 3^{1/6}+o(1))^{b}$.
\item For $q$ odd the height is $\le(\tfrac12+o(1))^{b}$, and at $\theta=\pi$ it
      is $0$ by parity.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\STATUS{mixed by item, and the mixture is the point. Items \textup{(i)--(iii)} and
\textup{(vi)} are \emph{exact identities}, proved in place, using no equidistribution
whatsoever. Items \textup{(iv)--(v)} do use equidistribution, and only for a finite list of
moduli, for which effective error terms are available --- these are the two items made
effective in Proposition~\ref{prop:l5a}. A reader who takes the whole corollary as
equidistribution-dependent will overstate what the paper assumes; one who takes it all as
exact will understate it.}

\begin{remark}
Items (i)--(iii) and (vi) are exact identities; only (iv)--(v) use
equidistribution, and only for a finite list of moduli, for which effective
error terms in the prime number theorem in arithmetic progressions are
available. The practical consequence is that the minor-arc analysis no longer
needs the uniform bound $M(q)$: within a factor $(0.75/0.866)^{b}$ of the
modulus-$6$ peak there are, for fixed $d$, only $q=4$ and the finite list
$\{2p:p\le 2d\}$.
\end{remark}

Figure~\ref{fig:peaks} shows the measured spectrum for the first $24$ primes

$q=10,14,22$, where the computed values are $6.0\cdot10^{-20}$,

\section{The modulus-six ripple to second order}\label{sec:l3}

Throughout this section $B$ is a set of $b$ odd primes with $3\notin B$. Put
\[
  S_2=\sum_{a\in B}a^{2},\quad S_4=\sum_{a\in B}a^{4},\quad
  \Delta=\sum_{a\in B}\tau_a a,\quad \Delta_3=\sum_{a\in B}\tau_a a^{3},
\]
where $\tau_a=+1$ if $a\equiv1\pmod6$ and $\tau_a=-1$ if $a\equiv5\pmod 6$, and
let $c_1,c_5$ be the corresponding class counts. Write $\Main(m)$ for the
Gaussian main-arc term and $\nu=T/2-m$.

\begin{lemma}[Local data at $\theta=\pi/3$]\label{lem:local}
Every $a\in B$ satisfies $\cos^{2}(a\pi/6)=3/4$, $\tan(a\pi/6)=\tau_a/\sqrt3$
and $\sec^{2}(a\pi/6)=4/3$. Consequently, with
$h(t)=\log|G_B(\pi/3+t)|$,
\[
  h'(0)=-\frac{\Delta}{2\sqrt3},\quad h''(0)=-\frac{S_2}{3}=:-V_6,\quad
  h'''(0)=-\frac{\Delta_3}{3\sqrt3},\quad h''''(0)=-\frac{S_4}{3}.
\]
Moreover $\frac{d}{d\theta}\arg(1+e^{ia\theta})=a/2$ exactly, so the phase of
$F_B$ is linear along the arc and all curvature lives in $|G_B|$.
\end{lemma}

\noindent
(Lean: \Lean{cos\_sq\_pi\_mul\_div\_six\_of\_one},
\Lean{cos\_sq\_pi\_mul\_div\_six\_of\_five}, \Lean{sec\_sq\_pi\_mul\_div\_six}.)

\begin{proposition}[Second-order ripple formula]\label{prop:ripple}
For $m$ in a bounded window about $T/2$,
\[
  \frac{r_B(m)}{\Main(m)}-1
  =2\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\Bigr)^{b+1}K_2
   \cos\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{6}\bigl(2m-(c_1-c_5)\bigr)+\psi(\nu)\Bigr)+E,
\]
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:k2}
  K_2=\exp\Bigl[\frac{\Delta^{2}}{8S_2}-\frac38\frac{S_4}{S_2^{2}}
      +\frac{\Delta\Delta_3}{4S_2^{2}}+\frac{5\Delta_3^{2}}{24S_2^{3}}
      -\frac{3S_4\Delta^{2}}{16S_2^{3}}\Bigr],
  \qquad
  \psi(\nu)=-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\,\nu\,\frac{\Delta+\Delta_3/S_2}{S_2}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\STATUS{derived, with the remainder \emph{measured} rather than bounded --- said here at the
display, because the two are easy to confuse. The equality is obtained by Laplace's method at
the saddle and the derivation is given below; the omitted cumulant contributions are named in
Remark~\ref{rem:error}; but $E$ is controlled numerically on the tested range
(Table~\ref{tab:k2}, Remark~\ref{rem:ripplerange}) and \emph{not} by a proved asymptotic
bound. This is therefore an exact derivation with a numerically controlled remainder, not a
theorem with a proved error term. Theorem~\ref{thm:t2main} inherits the distinction: its
conclusion $\varepsilon_d\to0$ does not use $K_2$ beyond the unconditional bound of
Remark~\ref{rem:whatneeded}, while its sharp constant $\sqrt3/2$ does.}

\begin{proof}[Derivation]
Laplace's method at the saddle $t^{*}=(L+i\nu)/V_6$ with $L=h'(0)$: the exponent
contributes $(L+i\nu)^{2}/(2V_6)+C_3(L+i\nu)^{3}/(6V_6^{3})+\cdots$, the
Gaussian-fluctuation factor $-\frac12\log(-h''(t^{*}))$ contributes
$C_3t^{*}/(2V_6)+C_4(t^{*})^{2}/(4V_6)+\cdots$, and the standard constant is
$5C_3^{2}/(24V_6^{3})+C_4/(8V_6^{2})$. Substituting
$C_3=-\Delta_3/(3\sqrt3)$, $C_4=-S_4/3$ and $V_6=S_2/3$ from
Lemma~\ref{lem:local}, and separating real from imaginary parts, gives $K_2$ and
$\psi$. The width factor $\sqrt{V_0/V_6}=\sqrt3/2$ accounts for the exponent
$b+1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The two branches]\label{rem:branches}
The term $\Delta^{2}/(8S_2)$ is positive and driven by the prime race between
the classes $\pm1\bmod6$; the term $-\tfrac38 S_4/S_2^{2}\asymp-9/(8b)$ is
negative and class-independent. Their competition produces the two branches seen
in the data. In the measured range $c_1-c_5\in\{-1,-3\}$, and the branch is
decided by that value: $c_1-c_5=-3$ forces $|\Delta|\ge135$ and $K_2>1$, while
$c_1-c_5=-1$ keeps $|\Delta|\le47$ and $K_2<1$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Error policy]\label{rem:error}
The omitted contributions are fifth- and sixth-cumulant terms and quartic
crosses in $\Delta$, of order
$O(|\Delta_5|/S_2^{5/2}+S_6/S_2^{3}+S_4\Delta^{4}/S_2^{4}+|\Delta_3|^{3}/S_2^{9/2})$
per unit of $K_2-1$. We state the remainder as an explicit numerical bound on
the verified range together with $o(1)$ asymptotics, rather than adding further
terms, which carry no structural information.
\end{remark}

\section{The main arc and the central density}\label{sec:m2}

The main arc contributes the smooth part of $r_B$. The leading term is the local
central limit theorem; what matters for the companion paper's absolute formula
is the \emph{first correction} to it.

\begin{proposition}[Central density with first correction]\label{prop:central}
Let $B$ be a set of $b$ odd integers, $V_0=S_2/4$, and let $m$ be at the centre
$T/2$. Then
\[
  r_B(m)=\frac{2^{b}}{\sqrt{2\pi V_0}}\Bigl(1-\frac{S_4}{4S_2^{2}}+O\bigl((S_4/S_2^{2})^{2}\bigr)\Bigr).
\]
\end{proposition}

\STATUS{proved. The derivation below is labelled \emph{Derivation} because it is a Taylor and
Laplace computation rather than a formal argument, but every term is written out and the
$O$-constant is explicit; the only step taken as standard is extending the main-arc integral to
$\mathbb{R}$. Said here because Proposition~\ref{prop:ripple} carries the same label and a
\emph{different} status --- there the remainder is measured, here it is bounded.}

\begin{proof}[Derivation]
Write $\log G_B(\theta)=\sum_{a\in B}\log\cos(a\theta/2)
=-\tfrac{V_0}{2}\theta^{2}-\tfrac{S_4}{192}\theta^{4}+O(S_6\theta^{6})$, using
$\log\cos u=-u^{2}/2-u^{4}/12+O(u^{6})$ and $u=a\theta/2$. Since $G_B$ is even
(Lean: \Lean{G\_even}), all odd-order terms vanish identically, so the first
correction is the quartic one. Integrating
$e^{-V_0\theta^{2}/2}(1-\tfrac{S_4}{192}\theta^{4})$ over the main arc and using
$\int\theta^{4}e^{-V_0\theta^{2}/2}=3\sqrt{2\pi}V_0^{-5/2}$ gives the stated
factor $1-\tfrac{S_4}{192}\cdot 3V_0^{-2}=1-\tfrac{S_4}{4S_2^{2}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The absolute formula of the companion paper]\label{rem:absolute}
The companion paper predicts the number of strict local minima by
$\mathrm{lm}\approx\Gamma\cdot 2^{b}/\sqrt{2\pi V_0}$, i.e.\ by replacing the
ground-state count by its Gaussian estimate. The measured ratio of the two sides
sits systematically below $1$ --- about $0.97$ --- with no explanation offered
there. Proposition~\ref{prop:central} identifies the deficit as
$S_4/(4S_2^{2})$, which at $k=20$ equals $0.0246$. Section~\ref{sec:m2num}
verifies this. We warn against one confusion: the value $0.972$ appearing here
(the ratio $\mathrm{lm}/\mathrm{pred}$ at $k=20$) is \emph{not} the $0.972$ in
the companion paper's list of $Q(k)$, which is $Q(18)$. Those are different
quantities and the numerical coincidence is accidental: $Q$ is formed with the
\emph{measured} ground-state count, so it carries no Edgeworth correction, and
it oscillates about $1$ rather than sitting below it.
\end{remark}

The comparison above was originally made over $k=18,\dots,40$. We have extended

doubles at no cost --- and then degrades. The degradation is a limit of the
measurement, not of the formula, and one can say so quantitatively. The ripple
being extracted has relative size $4(\sqrt3/2)^{b}$, which is
$1.5\cdot10^{-2}$ at $k=40$ but $2.6\cdot10^{-6}$ at $k=100$, whereas the smooth

signal therefore climbs from $3.5$ at $k=40$ to $7.6\cdot10^{3}$ at $k=100$.

within a factor of three while the ratio itself moves by two orders of
magnitude}. In other words the leakage of the smooth background into the
modulus-$6$ Fourier bin is a fixed $\approx5\cdot10^{-6}$ of that background,
and it becomes visible in the ratio only because the signal is shrinking
underneath it. Below $k\approx52$ the residual is instead dominated by the
accuracy of $K_2$ itself, at $3$--$6\cdot10^{-4}$. Reaching beyond $k\approx80$
would require subtracting the background to better than one part in $10^{5}$,
which is a question about the reference expansion and not about
Proposition~\ref{prop:ripple}.
\end{remark}

On branch B the individual values of $\text{measured}/K_2$ are $0.9991$
($k=32$), $0.9990$ ($k=34$) and $0.9997$ ($k=40$), against $0.9904$, $0.9923$
and $0.9952$ without the corrections. The largest residual over the whole range
is $0.0034$, attained at $k=18$. The omitted contributions are dominated by the
class-independent tail $O((S_4/S_2^{2})^{2})$, and $S_4/S_2^{2}$ decreases
monotonically over the range, from $0.1111$ at $k=18$ to $0.0507$ at $k=40$;
hence the residual is largest at the smallest $k$. That the $\Delta$-dependent
quantity $\Delta_3^{2}/S_2^{3}$ is in fact largest at $k=32$
($2.70\cdot10^{-3}$, against $1.04\cdot10^{-3}$ at $k=18$) without producing an

already absorbed into $K_2$.

\subsection{The phase drift}

Fitting the measured phase slope to
$\alpha\Delta/S_2+\beta(c_1-c_5)/S_2+\gamma/S_2$ over $k=18,\dots,40$, only
$\alpha$ is significant, at every window width tested: $t(\alpha)$ ranges from
$+4.26$ to $+5.16$, while $|t(\beta)|$ and $|t(\gamma)|$ never exceed $1.6$. The
one-parameter model $\text{measured}=c\cdot\psi'$ gives $c=0.884,0.888,0.904$
and $0.970$ at half-widths $24,48,96$ and $192$, with $R^{2}$ from $0.978$ to
$0.986$; restricting to $|\Delta|\ge17$ raises $R^{2}$ to $0.993$. We conclude
that $\psi$ as stated is correct, with the deficit in $c$ a finite-window
effect.

\begin{remark}[Extraction]\label{rem:extraction}
The amplitude is extracted from a narrow window of fixed half-width $24$, i.e.\
eight full periods of the modulus-$6$ ripple, which over $k=18,\dots,40$ is
between $0.08$ and $0.30$ standard deviations; both requirements --- enough
periods, and narrower than $\sigma$ --- hold throughout. Wide windows corrupt
the Gaussian reference. As a check, at $k=18$ the ratio
$\text{measured}/K_2$ is $0.9988$, $0.9974$, $1.0034$ and $1.0065$ at
half-widths $12,16,24$ and $32$, so the window choice moves the residual by
less than $1\%$. The phase slope requires \emph{wide} windows,
half-width $96$ to $192$; at half-width $384$ the normalisation breaks down in
the Gaussian tails. For $|\Delta|\le3$ the true slope is at the level of
extraction noise in narrow windows.
\end{remark}

\section{A free Gaussian envelope at every peak}\label{sec:l5b}

The following is elementary but useful: every sub-peak carries an envelope as
sharp as the main arc, with no arithmetic input at all.

\begin{proposition}[Curvature bound]\label{prop:curv}
Let $B$ be any finite set of positive reals and $V_0=\tfrac14\sum_{a\in B}a^{2}$.
On any interval where $G_B$ has no zero,
\[
  \frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}\log|G_B(\theta)|
  =-\sum_{a\in B}\frac{a^{2}}{4}\sec^{2}\!\Bigl(\frac{a\theta}{2}\Bigr)\;\le\;-V_0 ,
\]
because $\sec^{2}\ge1$. Consequently $\log|G_B|$ is concave there with curvature
at least $V_0$, and if $\theta^{*}$ is an interior local maximum then
\[
  |G_B(\theta)|\;\le\;|G_B(\theta^{*})|\,e^{-V_0(\theta-\theta^{*})^{2}/2}
\]
throughout that interval.
\end{proposition}

\noindent
(Lean: \Lean{one\_le\_one\_div\_cos\_sq}, \Lean{term\_curvature\_ge} and
\Lean{curvature\_ge\_V0} formalise the inequality
$V_0\le\sum(a^{2}/4)\sec^{2}(a\theta/2)$, and
\Lean{deriv\_le\_of\_second\_deriv\_nonpos} together with
\Lean{le\_of\_critical\_of\_second\_deriv\_nonpos} formalise the analytic
step: if $g''\le0$ and $g'(t)=0$ then $t$ is a maximum of $g$. Substituting
$g=f+c(\cdot-t)^{2}/2$ is then mechanical.)

Two caveats matter in practice, and both are visible numerically.

\begin{remark}[The centre is not $2\pi/q$]\label{rem:centre}
The envelope must be anchored at the true local maximum, which is displaced from
$2\pi/q$ by $-h'(0)/h''(0)$ with $h=\log|G_B|$; by Lemma~\ref{lem:local} this is
$\asymp\Delta/S_2$ at $q=6$. For the first $24$ odd primes truncated at $d=2$
the measured displacements are $+1.316\cdot10^{-5}$ ($q=6$),
$+3.795\cdot10^{-4}$ ($q=4$) and $-2.097\cdot10^{-3}$ ($q=8$), against predicted
$+1.316\cdot10^{-5}$, $+3.800\cdot10^{-4}$ and $-2.021\cdot10^{-3}$. Anchoring
at $2\pi/q$ instead makes the envelope fail, by $7.6\cdot10^{-6}$ at $q=6$ and
$4.7\cdot10^{-3}$ at $q=4$ in the same data.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Validity radius]\label{rem:radius}
The bound holds only inside the zero-free interval, whose half-width is
$\approx\pi/(2\max B)$. Measured in units of the envelope scale
$V_0^{-1/2}=2/\sqrt{S_2}$, this is
\[
  \frac{\pi}{2\max B}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{S_2}}{2}\;\approx\;\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{k/3}
  \;\approx\;0.45\sqrt{k},
\]
so the envelope covers a growing number of standard deviations: $1.72$ at
$k=16$, $2.08$ at $k=24$, $2.71$ at $k=40$ and $2.92$ at $k=48$, against the
prediction $1.80,2.21,2.85,3.12$.
\end{remark}

\section{Effective constants for the surviving peaks}\label{sec:l5a}

Items (iv) and (v) of Corollary~\ref{cor:spectrum} are the only ones that use
equidistribution, and only for a finite list of moduli. We make them effective.

Write $n_r=\#\{p\in B_d:p\equiv r\pmod q\}$ for $\gcd(r,q)=1$, and
$e_r=n_r-b/\varphi(q)$. Since
$\log|G_{B_d}(2\pi j/q)|=\sum_r n_r\log|\cos(\pi rj/q)|$ and
$\sum_r e_r=O(1)$,
\[
  |G_{B_d}(2\pi j/q)|\;\le\;M(q)^{b}\exp\Bigl(L_q\sum_r|e_r|\Bigr),
  \qquad
  L_q=\max_{\gcd(r,q)=1}\bigl|\log|\cos(\pi r/q)|\bigr| .
\]
Bennett, Martin, O'Bryant and Rechnitzer~\cite{BMOR18} supply two explicit
inputs which between them cover all $x$: their Theorem~1.3 gives
$|\pi(x;q,a)-\mathrm{Li}(x)/\varphi(q)|<c_\pi(q)\,x/(\log x)^{2}$ for
$x\ge x_\pi(q)$, with $c_\pi(q)\le1/840$ and $x_\pi(q)\le8\cdot10^{9}$ for
$3\le q\le10^{4}$; and their Theorem~1.9 gives
$\max_{y\le x}|\pi(y;q,a)-\mathrm{Li}(y)/\varphi(q)|\le2.734\sqrt{x}/\log x$ for
$x\le x_2(q)$, where $x_2(q)=10^{13}$ for $5<q\le100$. The ranges overlap, so
there is no gap.

\begin{proposition}[Effective form of Corollary~\ref{cor:spectrum}(iv)--(v)]
\label{prop:l5a}
\STATUS{proved, conditional on nothing, but quoting an external input: the thresholds in the
table are \emph{computed} from the published constants of~\cite{BMOR18} and are not measured,
so they inherit that paper's correctness and nothing of ours. Two things a reader should not
infer: the table is not a numerical fit, and the range $44<k<k_0(q,\eta)$ where neither direct
computation nor this proposition applies is not a gap in any asymptotic statement --- see the
three steps in the remark below.}
For each $\eta>0$ and each modulus $q$ in the finite list there is an explicit
$k_0(q,\eta)$ such that $|G_{B_d}(2\pi j/q)|\le(M(q)+\eta)^{b}$ for all
$k\ge k_0(q,\eta)$. With the constants of~\cite{BMOR18} one may take
\[
  \begin{array}{c|cccccc}
    q & 4 & 6 & 8 & 10 & 14 & 18\\\hline
    k_0(q,0.10) & 108 & 34 & 1694 & 856 & 3547 & 4411\\
    k_0(q,0.05) & 343 & 104 & 5596 & 2839 & 11914 & 14723\\
    k_0(q,0.01) & 5984 & 1737 & 103960 & 52658 & 226200 & 278350
  \end{array}
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
The $\sqrt{x}$-shaped bound of~\cite[Thm.~1.9]{BMOR18} is what makes these
thresholds small. Using only~\cite[Thm.~1.3]{BMOR18} one is forced to
$x\ge x_\pi(q)\approx10^{9}$, i.e.\ $k_0\approx4\cdot10^{8}$; the two theorems
must be combined. It is worth recording that using only the first of the two
costs a factor of $10^{4}$ in the threshold.

Regarding the range of validity we are explicit in three steps.
\emph{(a)} For $k\le44$ the peak heights are certified by direct computation:
$|G_{B_d}(2\pi j/q)|$ is a product of $b$ cosines and costs $O(k)$ arithmetic
operations, so it is simply evaluated (Section~\ref{sec:num}).
\emph{(b)} For $k\ge k_0(q,\eta)$ the bound is a theorem, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:l5a}.
\emph{(c)} The gap between the two is not needed for any asymptotic statement,
and in any case it can be closed by computation: for each of the finitely many
$q$ involved, evaluating $|G_{B_d}(2\pi j/q)|$ for all $k$ up to any desired
bound is an $O(k)$ computation per point.
Note also that $q=4$ and $q=6$ appear in the table only for comparison: for those
two moduli Corollary~\ref{cor:spectrum}(i)--(ii) are exact identities and no
equidistribution input is needed at all.
\end{remark}

\section{The deep minor arcs}\label{sec:l5c}

Most of this section is now proved: the dissection (Lemma~\ref{lem:cover}), the
counting of bad points (Lemma~\ref{lem:fejer}), the twisted sums
(Lemma~\ref{lem:twist}), the annulus profile with its closed form and its
consequence (Lemmas~\ref{lem:annulus}, \ref{lem:closed}, \ref{lem:supnorm}).
What is not written out to referee standard is the substitution of the quoted
exponential-sum bound in step~2 and the excision of step~4; we flag those
explicitly where they occur, and Section~\ref{sec:remains} keeps the ledger.

\subsection{The product is a superposition of prime exponential sums}

Expanding $\log|2\cos\pi u|=\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^{n+1}\cos(2\pi nu)/n$ termwise,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fourier}
  \log|G_B(\theta)|
  =-b\log2+\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\,
     \mathrm{Re}\!\!\sum_{a\in B}e\!\Bigl(\frac{na\theta}{2\pi}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
The inner sum is a classical exponential sum over primes. Thus the object we
must bound on the deep minor arcs is not exotic: it is a weighted superposition
of the sums $\sum_{p\le x}e(\alpha p)$ that Vinogradov's method was built for,
with $\alpha=n\theta/2\pi$. The only delicacy is that the coefficients $1/n$
decay slowly, and that the series diverges at the zeros of $\cos$, so a
truncation is needed near those points.

\subsection{How the circle is divided, and why nothing falls between}

Before the argument we fix the dissection, because the choice of the truncation
parameter $N_0$ decides whether it is exhaustive. Let $A\ge2$ be the exponent in
the Siegel--Walfisz theorem, put $A'=A+1$, and set
\[
  \delta_0=\frac{1}{\log b},\qquad N_0=(\log N)^{A'},\qquad
  Q_2=(\log N)^{A+A'} .
\]

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lllc@{}}
\toprule
Tier & range of $q$ & tool & located in\\
\midrule
exact peaks & $q\in\{4,6\}\cup\{2p:p\in B\}$ & cyclotomic identities & \S\ref{sec:vanish}\\
$T_2$ & remaining $q\le Q_2$ & PNT in AP $+$ envelope & \S\ref{sec:l5a}\\
$T_3$ & deep minor, see (ii) below & Vaughan $+$ Selberg $+$ Abel & this section\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
In tier $T_2$ the equidistribution of $B$ in the residue classes mod $q$ is what
produces the constant $M(q)$. Asymptotically this is the Siegel--Walfisz
theorem, which is what fixes $A$; effectively, for the $q$ that occur at any
accessible $k$, it is the explicit bounds of~\cite{BMOR18} used in
Proposition~\ref{prop:l5a}. Only the ineffectivity of the Siegel zero prevents
the two from being the same statement, and that is the sole reason
Section~\ref{sec:l5a} carries a threshold rather than a constant.

\begin{lemma}[Covering completeness]\label{lem:cover}
Every $\theta$ satisfies at least one of
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $|\theta-2\pi j/q|\le1/(q\tau)$ for some $q\le Q_2$ and $(j,q)=1$;
\item[(ii)] for every $n\le N_0$ the reduced denominator $q_n$ of $n\theta/2\pi$
      exceeds $(\log N)^{A}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose (ii) fails: some $n\le N_0$ has $q_n\le(\log N)^{A}$, so
$|n\theta/2\pi-j'/q_n|\le1/(q_n\tau)$ for some $j'$. Dividing by $n$ exhibits
$\theta/2\pi$ within $1/(nq_n\tau)$ of $j'/(nq_n)$, and
$nq_n\le N_0(\log N)^{A}=Q_2$. Reducing the fraction only decreases the
denominator, which gives (i).
\end{proof}

\noindent
The last line is the reason $Q_2$ has the value it does. A $\theta$ escapes
tier~$T_2$ only if \emph{every} harmonic $n\theta$ with $n\le N_0$ has
denominator above $(\log N)^{A}$; a single failure drags $\theta$ itself into a
major arc of denominator at most $N_0\cdot(\log N)^{A}$. So $Q_2$ is not a free
parameter once $N_0$ and $A$ are chosen: it is exactly the product
$(\log N)^{A'}\cdot(\log N)^{A}$, and the lower end of \eqref{eq:qrange} is the
same statement read the other way.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:whyN0}
With the earlier choice $N_0=b^{1/4}$ the same computation gives
$nq_n\le b^{1/4}(\log N)^{A}$, and the band
$(\log N)^{A}<q\le b^{1/4}(\log N)^{A}$ then belongs to no tier: too large for
the explicit bounds, too small to be deep minor. Replacing $b^{1/4}$ by a fixed
power of $\log N$ closes it, and costs nothing --- see
Remark~\ref{rem:budget}, where the change in fact improves every measured
quantity that matters.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The deep-minor bound}

We now write the argument out. Throughout, $\theta$ is of type~(ii) of
Lemma~\ref{lem:cover}; the $\theta$ of type~(i) are covered by
Corollary~\ref{cor:spectrum} and Proposition~\ref{prop:l5a}. Write
\[
  \alpha_n=\frac{n\theta}{2\pi},\qquad
  u_p=\frac{p\theta}{2\pi},\qquad
  v_p=u_p-\tfrac12 ,
\]
and split $B=B_{\mathrm{good}}\cup B_{\mathrm{bad}}$, where $p$ is \emph{bad}
when $\|v_p\|<\delta_0$.

\begin{proposition}[Deep minor arcs]\label{prop:deepminor}
Let $\theta$ be of type~\textup{(ii)} and let $A>8$. Then
\[
  \frac1b\log|G_B(\theta)|\;\le\;-\log2\;+\;R(\theta),
  \qquad
  R(\theta)\;\ll\;\delta_0\log\log N+\frac{1}{N_0\delta_0}
    +(\log N)^{4-A/2}(\log\log N)^{2},
\]
uniformly in $\theta$. With $A=11$, $A'=12$ and $\delta_0=1/\log b$ this reads
$R(\theta)\ll\log\log N/\log N$. In particular
$|G_B(\theta)|\le\bigl(\tfrac12+o(1)\bigr)^{b}$ on the deep minor arcs.
\end{proposition}

\noindent
Before the proof we record the exponent budget, so that the reader can see which
step is the slow one. Every entry is a bound on the named quantity \emph{divided
by} $b$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
step & quantity & bound $/b$ & at $A=11$, $\delta_0=1/\log b$\\
\midrule
2 & $\max_{n\le N_0}|S_B(\alpha_n)|$ & $(\log N)^{4-A/2}$ & $(\log N)^{-3/2}$\\
2 & $H(\theta)=\sum_{n\le N_0}n^{-1}|S_B(\alpha_n)|$
  & $(\log N)^{4-A/2}\log\log N$ & $(\log N)^{-3/2}\log\log N$\\
3 & $|B_{\mathrm{bad}}|$ & $\delta_0+N_0^{-1}+b^{-1}H$ & $(\log N)^{-1}$\\
4a & $\sum_{p\in B_{\mathrm{bad}}}\log|2\cos\pi u_p|$ & $(\log2)\,b^{-1}|B_{\mathrm{bad}}|$
  & $(\log N)^{-1}$\\
4b & tails past $N_0$, good $p$ & $(2N_0\delta_0)^{-1}+N_0^{-1}$ & $(\log N)^{1-A'}$\\
4c & $B_{\mathrm{bad}}$ inside the head & $(1+\log N_0)\,b^{-1}|B_{\mathrm{bad}}|$
  & $\log\log N/\log N$\ \ \textbf{(slowest)}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: harmonics stay minor.} This is condition~(ii) verbatim: for every
$n\le N_0$ the denominator $q_n$ produced by Dirichlet's theorem with parameter
$\tau=N(\log N)^{-A}$ satisfies $q_n>(\log N)^{A}$. This is the only place where
the value of $N_0$ enters the dissection, and it is why $N_0$ must be a power of
$\log N$ rather than of $b$ (Remark~\ref{rem:whyN0}).

\emph{Step 2: the head of \eqref{eq:fourier}.} Dirichlet's theorem with
parameter $\tau$ gives, for each $n$, a fraction $a_n/q_n$ with $q_n\le\tau$,
$(a_n,q_n)=1$ and $|\alpha_n-a_n/q_n|\le1/(q_n\tau)$. Since $q_n\le\tau$ we have
$|q_n\alpha_n-a_n|\le1/\tau\le1/q_n$, so the hypothesis of Lemma~3.1
of~\cite{Ku05} holds; and $q_n\le\tau\le N$. Combining with step~1,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qrange}
  (\log N)^{A}\;<\;q_n\;\le\;N(\log N)^{-A}\qquad(n\le N_0),
\end{equation}
which is exactly the range on which Vaughan's identity is effective. No other
range occurs: the tiers below $(\log N)^{A}$ are not lost but are handled in
\S\ref{sec:vanish} and \S\ref{sec:l5a}, where the cyclotomic constants $M(q)$
give the bound directly and no exponential sum is needed at all. On this range
Lemma~3.1 of~\cite{Ku05} --- attributed there to Vaughan, as the sharpest form
of Vinogradov's 1937 estimate --- gives
\[
  S_B(\alpha_n)=\sum_{p\in B}e(p\alpha_n)
  \;\ll\;(\log N)^{3}\bigl(Nq_n^{-1/2}+N^{4/5}+(Nq_n)^{1/2}\bigr).
\]
Insert the two ends of \eqref{eq:qrange}: the first term is
$\ll N(\log N)^{-A/2}$ by the lower end, and the third is
$\le(N\cdot N(\log N)^{-A})^{1/2}=N(\log N)^{-A/2}$ by the upper end --- the two
ends are used in opposite directions, which is why both are needed. Hence,
uniformly in $n\le N_0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sbbound}
  |S_B(\alpha_n)|\;\ll\;N(\log N)^{3-A/2}+N^{4/5}(\log N)^{3}.
\end{equation}
Since $b=\pi(N)=(1+o(1))N/\log N$, dividing by $b$ turns \eqref{eq:sbbound} into
$(\log N)^{4-A/2}+N^{-1/5}(\log N)^{4}\ll(\log N)^{4-A/2}$, the second term
being negligible. Summing the head with the weights $1/n$,
\[
  H(\theta):=\sum_{n\le N_0}\frac{|S_B(\alpha_n)|}{n}
  \;\le\;(1+\log N_0)\max_{n\le N_0}|S_B(\alpha_n)|,
  \qquad \log N_0=A'\log\log N,
\]
so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:head}
  \frac{H(\theta)}{b}\;\ll\;(\log N)^{4-A/2}\log\log N .
\end{equation}
This is $o(1)$ precisely when $A>8$; $A$ is ours to choose and Siegel--Walfisz
holds for every fixed $A$, so we fix $A=11$ once and for all, giving
$H(\theta)/b\ll(\log N)^{-3/2}\log\log N$. A log-free version valid on a wider
range of $q$ is available in~\cite{Sr25}.

\emph{Step 3: the bad points are few.} Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:fejer} --- the
one-interval form of the Erd\H{o}s--Tur\'an inequality, proved in the appendix
from the Fej\'er kernel so that no external reference is incurred --- to the
points $u_p$, the interval $\|x-\tfrac12\|<\delta_0$ and the degree $N_0$:
\[
  |B_{\mathrm{bad}}|\;\le\;4b\Bigl(\delta_0+\frac{1}{N_0}\Bigr)
    +4\sum_{n\le N_0}c_n|S_B(\alpha_n)|,
  \qquad c_n=\min\Bigl(2\delta_0+\tfrac{2}{N_0},\tfrac1{\pi n}\Bigr).
\]
Bounding $c_n\le1/(\pi n)$ and using \eqref{eq:head},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bad}
  \frac{|B_{\mathrm{bad}}|}{b}\;\le\;4\delta_0+\frac4{N_0}
    +\frac4\pi\cdot\frac{H(\theta)}{b}
  \;\ll\;\delta_0+\frac1{N_0}+(\log N)^{4-A/2}\log\log N .
\end{equation}
We stress that this removes a dependency rather than adding one: no external
discrepancy theorem for $\{p\theta\}$ is required. The literature on $\alpha p$
modulo one --- Vinogradov, Vaughan, Harman, Matom\"aki --- proves \emph{small-value}
statements, that some prime $p\le N$ makes $\|\alpha p-\beta\|$ small; that is a
statement about the extreme of the sequence and does not by itself bound its
discrepancy. The route above uses only the exponential sums of step~2.

\emph{Step 4: excision.} Since
$\log|G_B(\theta)|=-b\log2+\sum_{p\in B}\log|2\cos\pi u_p|$, it suffices to bound
the last sum above by $bR(\theta)$. Split it over $B_{\mathrm{bad}}$ and
$B_{\mathrm{good}}$ and, on $B_{\mathrm{good}}$, split \eqref{eq:fourier} at
$N_0$:
\[
  \sum_{p\in B}\log|2\cos\pi u_p|
  \;\le\;\underbrace{|B_{\mathrm{bad}}|\log2}_{\text{(4a)}}
   +\underbrace{\Bigl|\sum_{n\le N_0}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}
       \mathrm{Re}\!\!\sum_{p\in B_{\mathrm{good}}}\!\!e(p\alpha_n)\Bigr|}_{\text{(4c)}}
   +\underbrace{\sum_{p\in B_{\mathrm{good}}}|T_p|}_{\text{(4b)}} ,
\]
where $T_p$ is the tail of \eqref{eq:fourier} past $N_0$ at the point $u_p$.

\emph{(4a)} $\log|2\cos\pi u|\le\log2$ for every $u$, so this term is at most
$|B_{\mathrm{bad}}|\log2$, which is $\ll b\,\bigl(\delta_0+N_0^{-1}
+(\log N)^{4-A/2}\log\log N\bigr)$ by \eqref{eq:bad}.

\emph{(4b)} Put $v=v_p$, so that $\log|2\cos\pi u_p|=\log|2\sin\pi v|
=-\sum_{n\ge1}\cos(2\pi nv)/n$ and $\|v\|\ge\delta_0$. The Dirichlet kernel
$D_M(v)=\sum_{n\le M}\cos2\pi nv=\frac{\sin((2M+1)\pi v)}{2\sin\pi v}-\frac12$
satisfies, using $|\sin\pi v|\ge2\|v\|$,
\[
  |D_M(v)|\;\le\;\frac{1}{4\|v\|}+\frac12\qquad\text{for every }M\ge1 .
\]
Summation by parts against $1/n$ on $(N_0,M]$ gives
$\bigl|\sum_{N_0<n\le M}\cos(2\pi nv)/n\bigr|\le
|D_M|/M+|D_{N_0}|/(N_0+1)+\sup_n|D_n|\sum_{n>N_0}\bigl(\tfrac1n-\tfrac1{n+1}\bigr)$,
and letting $M\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tailbound}
  |T_p|\;\le\;\frac{2}{N_0+1}\Bigl(\frac1{4\|v_p\|}+\frac12\Bigr)
  \;\le\;\frac{1}{2N_0\delta_0}+\frac1{N_0}.
\end{equation}
Summing over at most $b$ good points, (4b) is at most
$b\bigl((2N_0\delta_0)^{-1}+N_0^{-1}\bigr)$.

\emph{(4c)} Write $\sum_{p\in B_{\mathrm{good}}}e(p\alpha_n)
=S_B(\alpha_n)-\sum_{p\in B_{\mathrm{bad}}}e(p\alpha_n)$ and bound the second
sum trivially by $|B_{\mathrm{bad}}|$. Then
\[
  \text{(4c)}\;\le\;H(\theta)+(1+\log N_0)\,|B_{\mathrm{bad}}| .
\]

Collecting (4a)--(4c), dividing by $b$, using \eqref{eq:bad} and $\delta_0\le1$
(so that $N_0^{-1}\le(N_0\delta_0)^{-1}$), and absorbing the factor
$1+\log N_0\asymp A'\log\log N$ into the bracket of \eqref{eq:bad}, gives
\[
  R(\theta)\;\ll\;\delta_0\log\log N+\frac{1}{N_0\delta_0}
    +(\log N)^{4-A/2}(\log\log N)^{2},
\]
which is the stated bound. With $A=11$, $A'=12$ and $\delta_0=1/\log b$ the
three terms are $\asymp\log\log N/\log N$, $\asymp(\log N)^{1-A'}$ and
$\asymp(\log N)^{-3/2}(\log\log N)^{2}$; the first dominates.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[$\delta_0$ is free, and $1/\log b$ is not the best choice]
\label{rem:delta0}
The proof uses only $N_0\delta_0\to\infty$ and $\delta_0\log\log N\to0$. Within
that range the two $\delta_0$-terms of Proposition~\ref{prop:deepminor} balance
at $\delta_0\asymp(N_0\log\log N)^{-1/2}$; the simpler choice
$\delta_0=N_0^{-1/2}=(\log N)^{-A'/2}$ already sends both of them below the
exponential-sum term, improving the rate from
$\log\log N/\log N$ to $(\log N)^{4-A/2}(\log\log N)^{2}$, i.e.\ to
$(\log N)^{-3/2}(\log\log N)^{2}$ at $A=11$. We keep $\delta_0=1/\log b$ because
it is the value at which the budget of Remark~\ref{rem:budget} was measured, and
because the conclusion is the same; a reader who wants the better rate may
substitute throughout.

The rate is in any case at the mercy of $A$: the exponential-sum term is
$(\log N)^{4-A/2}$ up to log-logs, so any fixed $J$ is reached by taking
$A\ge2J+8$. That is free asymptotically but not for free in
Proposition~\ref{prop:l5a}, whose explicit constants are calibrated to
$q\le Q_2=(\log N)^{A+A'}$; raising $A$ raises that threshold. Since this
section is in any case ineffective through $C_1$
(Appendix~\ref{sec:constants}), we do not optimise.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[What the proved rate is, and what is measured]
Proposition~\ref{prop:deepminor} gives $R(\theta)\ll\log\log N/\log N$, and the
implied constant, while absolute, is generous: the Erd\H{o}s--Tur\'an factor $4$
of Lemma~\ref{lem:fejer}, the trivial bound $|B_{\mathrm{bad}}|$ used in (4c),
and the crude $c_n\le1/(\pi n)$ each cost a factor. The proof needs only $o(1)$
and gets it. What the quantity actually does is recorded separately in
Remark~\ref{rem:budget}: the measured
$-b^{-1}\log|G_B(\theta)|-\log2$ was $0.123$ at $b=99$ and $0.024$ at $b=1999$,
against the $\log\sqrt3=0.549$ that the conclusion needs.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[What this settles about the technique needed]
Only a one-sided bound together with an exceptional set of size $o(b)$ is
required. The precise asymptotic machinery of~\cite{DRZZ25} --- their strange
and pseudo-differentiable functions --- is therefore not needed here, and we
cite them for context only. The question was nevertheless the right one: the
non-smoothness of $\log|2\cos|$ is exactly where the elementary excision of
step~4 does its work.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[An alternative route]
One can instead avoid~\eqref{eq:fourier} altogether and argue through the full
discrepancy $D_B(\theta)$ of $\{p\theta/2\pi\}_{p\in B}$: bound it by
Erd\H{o}s--Tur\'an from the same exponential sums, then apply Koksma's
inequality to the truncated function $\max(\log|\cos\pi x|,\log\delta)$. The two
routes give the same conclusion. We prefer the one above for two reasons:
\eqref{eq:fourier} is an exact identity, and step~3 needs the Erd\H{o}s--Tur\'an
machinery only for a single interval, where it is elementary, rather than
uniformly over all intervals.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Where the effective constants are, and are not]
Effectivity in this paper is confined to three places: the peak heights at
$q=4,6$, which are exact identities (Corollary~\ref{cor:spectrum}(i)--(ii)); the
remaining finite list of moduli, effective with $k_0\le1.5\cdot10^{4}$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:l5a}); and the Gaussian envelopes, which need no
arithmetic input at all (Proposition~\ref{prop:curv}). This section is
asymptotic; see Remark~\ref{rem:helfgott}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[On explicit constants]\label{rem:helfgott}
One might hope to make step~2, and hence step~3, effective using Helfgott's
explicit minor-arc
bounds~\cite{Hel12}. His Main Theorem is stated for $x\ge x_0=2.16\cdot10^{20}$,
which corresponds to $k=\pi(x_0)\approx4.7\cdot10^{18}$. That is far outside any
range we could combine with the direct computations of Section~\ref{sec:num}, so
we do not claim an effective version of this section. Effectivity in this paper
is confined to Proposition~\ref{prop:l5a} (thresholds $\approx10^{4}$) and to
Proposition~\ref{prop:curv}, which needs no arithmetic input at all.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Numerical check of the budget]\label{rem:budget}
The parameters are asymptotic and are meaningless at $b=23$, so we tested the
steps above at $b$ up to $1999$, taking the worst of six sampled deep-minor
$\theta$ at each size. Each evaluation is $O(bN_0)$. One substitution is forced:
the argument takes $N_0=(\log N)^{A'}$ with $A'=12$, which at $b=1999$ is about
$2\cdot10^{10}$ and cannot be computed; the table therefore uses
$N_0=(\log b)^{3}$, which is the same qualitative object --- a fixed power of a
logarithm --- and is the smallest such choice for which the tail is already
meaningful. Increasing the exponent only improves the tail and worsens the head,
both monotonically, so the comparison with $b^{1/4}$ below is not sensitive to
it. Writing $\mathrm{tail}$ for the step-4 bound $1/(N_0\delta_0)$:
\[
  \begin{array}{l|cccc}
    b & 99 & 299 & 999 & 1999\\\hline
    |B_{\mathrm{bad}}|/b\ \text{(measured)} & 0.485 & 0.405 & 0.324 & 0.282\\
    \text{step-3 bound}/b,\ N_0=(\log N)^{3} & 1.079 & 0.910 & 0.614 & 0.459\\
    b^{-1}\sum_{n\le N_0}|S_B(n\theta)|/n & 0.697 & 0.640 & 0.415 & 0.292\\\hline
    \mathrm{tail},\ N_0=b^{1/4}\ \text{(old)} & 1.532 & 1.425 & 1.381 & 1.267\\
    \mathrm{tail},\ N_0=(\log N)^{3}\ \text{(new)} & 0.047 & 0.031 & 0.021 & 0.017
  \end{array}
\]
The bound of step~3 holds in every case. The change of $N_0$ is the decisive
one: with $N_0=b^{1/4}$ the tail bound never drops below $1$ in any accessible
range --- it would need $b\approx10^{6}$ --- so that step was vacuous in
practice, whereas with $N_0=(\log N)^{3}$ it is already below $0.05$ at $b=99$.
The price is a larger head, since more harmonics are summed; the head is the
slower of the two now, and the step-3 bound only becomes nontrivial (that is,
smaller than $1$) from $b\approx300$.

None of this is close to binding. The conclusion needs only
$\tfrac12e^{o(1)}<\sqrt3/2$, i.e.\ the $o(1)$ to stay below
$\log\sqrt3=0.549$, and the quantity it controls,
$-b^{-1}\log|G_B(\theta)|-\log2$, was measured at $0.123$ for $b=99$ and $0.024$
for $b=1999$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[What the peaks actually look like]\label{rem:peakscan}
As a check on the dissection we located every local maximum of $|G_B|$ on
$[0,\pi]$ by direct evaluation on a grid of $6\cdot10^{6}$ points, for
$k=40,60,100,140$. Every one of the twelve largest lies within the envelope
radius of a rational $2\pi j/q$ with $q\le26$, and the ordering by height
follows $M(q)$ as predicted, with one instructive exception: at $q=10$, where
$5\in B$ forces $G_B(2\pi/10)=0$ exactly, the vanishing cuts a narrow notch and
the flanking maxima sit at $b^{-1}\log|G_B|=-0.355$ at $b=99$, rising towards
$\log M(10)=-0.291$ like $-\tfrac32(\log b)/b$, which is the expected width of
the notch. Excluding the central peak and a disc of the envelope radius about
each rational with $q\le60$, the largest remaining value of
$b^{-1}\log|G_B|$ is $-0.305$ at $b=99$ and $-0.305$ at $b=139$, against
$\log(\sqrt3/2)=-0.144$: a margin of $0.16$ per element, stable in $k$.
\end{remark}

\section{The main theorem}\label{sec:t2main}

Nothing new is proved in this section; it is the inventory that assembles the
previous ones.

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic flatness]\label{thm:t2main}
Fix $d\ge2$ and let
$n=\lfloor T/2\rfloor$. With
$B=B_d(k)$, $b=|B|$, as $k\to\infty$,
\[
  \varepsilon_d(k)=4\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\Bigr)^{b+1}K_2(k)\,\kappa_d(k)\,
  \bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)+O\!\Bigl(\frac{w_d^{2}}{S_2}\Bigr),
\]
where $K_2$ is the amplitude correction of Proposition~\ref{prop:ripple},
$\kappa_d\in[0,1]$ is the explicit trigonometric window factor --- half the
range of $\cos((\pi/6)(2m-(c_1-c_5))+\psi)$ over $m\in\mathrm{Win}_d$ --- and
$w_d$ is the window width. In particular $\varepsilon_d(k)\to0$ at the
geometric rate $\sqrt3/2$ per element.

\STATUS{no hypothesis, but not fully written out; ineffective at one constant; one factor of
the display calibrated numerically --- itemised next, in the statement rather than only in the
surrounding text}
\textbf{(i)} The
theorem carries no hypothesis, but its proof is not yet complete as written: two steps of
Proposition~\ref{prop:deepminor} --- the substitution of the quoted exponential-sum bound in
step~2 and the excision in step~4 of Section~\ref{sec:l5c} --- are not written to referee
standard. \textbf{(ii)} It is ineffective at one point, the Siegel--Walfisz constant $C_1$;
the effective substitute is the weaker rate $e^{1/8}\sqrt3/2=0.98134\ldots$ per element, which
still gives $\varepsilon_d\to0$. \textbf{(iii)} Of the factors in the display, the peak height
$(\sqrt3/2)^{b+1}$ and the window factor $\kappa_d\in[0,1]$ are proved, while the amplitude
correction $K_2$ is derived by Laplace's method and \emph{calibrated numerically}
(Proposition~\ref{prop:ripple}, to $0.1\%$ for $k\le76$); the conclusion
$\varepsilon_d\to0$ does not depend on $K_2$ beyond the unconditional bound just quoted, but
the sharp constant $\sqrt3/2$ does.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
Earlier versions of this paper carried the deep-minor bound as a hypothesis,
on the ground that Section~\ref{sec:l5c} gave a programme rather than a proof.
It is now Proposition~\ref{prop:deepminor}, and the theorem above is
unconditional. What is quoted from outside is a single exponential-sum
estimate, Lemma~3.1 of~\cite{Ku05}; everything the present paper adds to it is
written out, with the exponent budget displayed so that the substitution can be
checked line by line.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[What the conclusion needs, and what it does not]\label{rem:whatneeded}
The displayed formula and the conclusion $\varepsilon_d\to0$ have different
statuses, and it is worth separating them, because only the first involves
anything that is merely calibrated numerically.

The \emph{conclusion} $\varepsilon_d(k)\to0$ needs: the
exact peak heights of Corollary~\ref{cor:spectrum}, which are identities; the
envelope of Proposition~\ref{prop:curv}, which is proved; the annulus bound of
Lemma~\ref{lem:supnorm} with Proposition~\ref{prop:supnorm}, which is proved;
$\kappa_d\in[0,1]$, which is immediate from the definition of $\kappa_d$ as half
the range of a cosine; and an upper bound on $K_2$. We give that bound
unconditionally, and we are careful to say what is and is not proved about it.

\emph{(i) An unconditional, effective bound, and it already suffices.} With
$\Delta=\sum_{a\in B}\tau_a a$ and $\tau_a=\pm1$, Cauchy--Schwarz gives
$\Delta^{2}\le\bigl(\sum_a\tau_a^{2}\bigr)S_2=b\,S_2$, hence
\[
  \frac{\Delta^{2}}{8S_2}\;\le\;\frac b8 .
\]
The four remaining terms in the exponent \eqref{eq:k2} are $O(1)$ by the trivial
moment bounds $S_4\le N^{2}S_2$, $S_6\le N^{4}S_2$ and
$|\Delta_3|\le(bS_6)^{1/2}$, together with $S_2\gg N^{3}/\log N$ and
$b\ll N/\log N$: they are at most $O(\log N/N)$, $bN^{2}/(4S_2)=O(1)$,
$O(\log N/N)$ and $3bN^{2}/(16S_2)=O(1)$ respectively. Therefore
\[
  K_2\;\le\;\exp\bigl(b/8+O(1)\bigr),
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad
  \Bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\Bigr)^{b}K_2\;\ll\;\bigl(e^{1/8}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\bigr)^{b}
  =(0.98134\ldots)^{b},
\]
and $e^{1/8}\sqrt3/2<1$ with a margin of $0.0187$ per element. \emph{The
conclusion $\varepsilon_d\to0$ therefore survives with no arithmetic input
whatsoever}; only the rate degrades, from $\sqrt3/2$ to $0.9813$.

\emph{(ii) The rate $\sqrt3/2$ is recovered asymptotically, but not effectively.}
The Siegel--Walfisz estimate already used in Lemma~\ref{lem:twist} gives
$\Delta\ll N^{2}e^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}$ and hence $\Delta^{2}/(8S_2)=o(b)$, so
$K_2^{1/b}\to1$ and the rate returns to $\sqrt3/2$. As with every other appeal
to Siegel--Walfisz in this paper the threshold is ineffective: the ratio of the
resulting bound to $b/8$ is $\asymp(\log N)^{2}e^{-2c\sqrt{\log N}}$, which
tends to $0$ but at a point that depends on the admissible $c$, and $c$ is not
pinned down here. This is why we take (i), not (ii), as the load-bearing
statement.

\emph{(iii) The numerically calibrated part is the value, not the bound.} What
Proposition~\ref{prop:ripple} supplies --- and all it supplies --- is the
\emph{value} $K_2\approx1$, i.e.\ the second-order amplitude, verified to
$0.1\%$ rather than proved (Remark~\ref{rem:ripplerange}). A reader who
distrusts it may delete $K_2$ from the display, replace it by the bound of (i),
and read the statement as $\varepsilon_d(k)\ll(0.9813)^{b}$; nothing else
changes. \emph{No part of the conclusion rests on
Proposition~\ref{prop:ripple}.}

One further point, since it is a natural worry about any argument that passes
through Fourier data. There is no step here from ``global integrals'' to
``pointwise ratios'': $r_B(m)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}F_B(\theta)e^{-im\theta}
\,d\theta$ is an exact identity for each individual $m$, and every bound in
Sections~\ref{sec:l5b}--\ref{sec:l5c} is a bound on the integrand $|G_B(\theta)|$
at each $\theta$, integrated afterwards. The place where care is genuinely
needed is the $o(1)$ inventory of item~(6) in the outline below, which is why we
list it there rather than hide it.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:goal}
Combining Theorem~\ref{thm:t2main} with the sandwich theorem of the companion
paper and the identity $W_D=\Gamma+(2D+1)2^{-k}$,
\[
  \frac{\mathrm{lm}_{P}(n)}{\deg_{P}(n)}\longrightarrow\Gamma(P)=5.3492879\ldots
\]
for the odd primes.
\end{corollary}

\STATUS{inherits the status of Theorem~\ref{thm:t2main} and adds nothing to it: the bookkeeping
below is an outline because each of its four steps is proved elsewhere and only the combination
is assembled here, \emph{not} because a step is missing. This corollary is the sentence a
reader is most likely to quote, so it should not be quotable without the theorem's caveats
travelling with it.}

\begin{proof}[Outline]
The bookkeeping is as follows.
\emph{(1)} By Proposition~\ref{prop:central}, $r_B(m)=\Main(m)(1+E_0(m))$ with
the correction $1-S_4/(4S_2^{2})$; over the bounded window the smooth ratio
varies by $O(w_d^{2}/S_2)$.
\emph{(2)} By Proposition~\ref{prop:ripple}, the two exact peaks contribute
$2(\sqrt3/2)^{b+1}K_2\cos(\cdot+\psi)$ and $2(1/\sqrt2)^{b+1}K_4\cos(\cdot)$;
the second is smaller by a factor $(0.8165)^{b}$ and is absorbed into $1+o(1)$.
\emph{(3)} By Lemma~\ref{lem:vanish} every peak at $q=2p$ with $p\in B$
vanishes, and by Corollary~\ref{cor:spectrum} and Proposition~\ref{prop:l5a} the
surviving finite list has heights at most $(m_d+\eta)^{b}$ with
$m_d\le5^{1/4}/2$.
\emph{(4)} By Proposition~\ref{prop:curv} each peak carries a Gaussian envelope
of curvature $V_0$ centred at its true maximum, with reach $\Theta(\sqrt k)$
standard deviations, so the arcs tile.
\emph{(5)} Off all envelopes, Section~\ref{sec:l5c} gives
$|G_B|\le(1/2+o(1))^{b}$.
\emph{(6)} Integrating over each region and dividing by $\Main$ gives the
inventory
\[
  \begin{array}{l|l|l}
    \text{region} & \text{bound, relative to }\Main & \text{measured, }k=32\\\hline
    \text{central} & 1 & 0.985\\
    q=6 & C(\sqrt3/2)^{b} & 1.03\cdot10^{-2}\\
    q=4 & C(1/\sqrt2)^{b} & 1.48\cdot10^{-5}\\
    \text{other }q\le Q_2 & CQ_2\,(m_d+o(1))^{b} & 4.06\cdot10^{-7}\\
    q=2p,\ p\in B & CQ_2\,(M(2p)+o(1))^{b}b^{-3/2} & \text{---}\\
    \text{deep minor} & CQ_2\,(m_d+o(1))^{b}+C(1/2+o(1))^{b} & 3.66\cdot10^{-6}
  \end{array}
\]
with $m_d\le M(18)=0.6005$ for $d=2$. The row for $q=2p$ is there because the
vanishing of Corollary~\ref{cor:spectrum}(iii) is pointwise: it contributes no
mass at the rational itself but the notch is narrow and the flanking peaks
are not removed (Remark~\ref{rem:notch}). The last row deserves a word. It is
tempting to bound the deep-minor region by its pointwise bound
$(1/2+o(1))^{b}$ times the measure of the region, but that is wrong by several
orders of magnitude --- at $k=32$ it would give $1.2\cdot10^{-7}$ against a
measured $3.7\cdot10^{-6}$ --- because the integral there is dominated not by
the deepest points but by the many shallow peaks the region also contains, each
of which carries a width. Counting those peaks with their widths, at height
$m_d^{b}$, reproduces the measurement to within a bounded factor. The
conclusion is unaffected: what matters is that every row after the second is
$o\bigl((\sqrt3/2)^{b}\bigr)$, and the measured ratios of rows $3$--$6$ to row
$2$ fall from $1.3\%$ at $k=24$ to $0.04\%$ at $k=32$. Taking the maximum and
minimum over the bounded window then gives the displayed formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[which of these the main theorem waits on]\label{rem:whichwait}
Worth separating, because a reader who meets three open problems in a
circle-method paper will reasonably assume the main theorem is one of their
hostages. Theorem~\ref{thm:t2main} rests on
Proposition~\ref{prop:deepminor}, whose steps 2 and 4 are the ones not yet
written to referee standard; \emph{that} is the debt, and
Problem~\ref{prob:minor} is where it is recorded. Problems~\ref{prob:main}
and~\ref{prob:assembly} are a different matter: they ask for an
\emph{effective} main-arc estimate and the assembly that would follow from it,
which would sharpen the result and remove the Siegel--Walfisz constant, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:t2main} does not wait on either.
\end{remark}

\begin{problem}[Main arc]\label{prob:main}
Make the local central limit theorem for $r_B$ on the main arc effective, with
an error term of the shape $C/S_2$ arising from the curvature sum of the smooth
part. Numerically this term accounts for the whole of the previously
unexplained $25$--$29\%$ gap in the ripple prediction, and its size is
$\asymp1/S_2$ rather than exponential in $b$.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Minor arcs]\label{prob:minor}
Carry out the programme of Section~\ref{sec:l5c}. The exponential sums are the
classical ones by~\eqref{eq:fourier}; the non-classical part is the excision
near the zeros of $\cos$. Dong, Robles, Zaharescu and Zeindler~\cite{DRZZ25}
handle non-principal major arcs for prime partitions using what they call
strange and pseudo-differentiable functions, and their treatment of non-smooth
behaviour looks like the right tool for that excision, although their limit
($n\to\infty$ with the part set fixed) is not ours.
\end{problem}

\begin{lemma}[The annulus, resolved]\label{lem:supnorm}
For every $q$ and every $x>0$,
\[
  \log M(q)+\Phi_q(x)\;\le\;\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\log\|\Phi_q\|_{\infty}-\log2,
  \qquad \|\Phi_q\|_{\infty}:=\max_{|z|=1}|\Phi_q(z)| .
\]
Consequently $\log M(q)+\Phi_q(x)\le\log(\sqrt3/2)$ for all $x>0$ as soon as
$\|\Phi_q\|_{\infty}\le3^{\varphi(q)/2}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:closed}, $\log M(q)+\Phi_q(x)=-\log2+\frac1x\int_0^{x}h_q$
where $h_q(v)=\varphi(q)^{-1}\log|\Phi_q(-e^{-iv})|$. Bound the integrand by its
supremum, $h_q(v)\le\varphi(q)^{-1}\log\|\Phi_q\|_{\infty}$, and average.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What this replaces]\label{rem:trivial}
This is worth stating plainly, because it is the third and last form the
question took. We first asked for the reach of the envelope, then for the sign
of $\Phi_q$ on a compact range of $x$, and expected to settle the latter by
certified numerical integration. Once Lemma~\ref{lem:closed} had put $\Phi_q$
in the form of a running average, no integration was needed: a running average
never exceeds the supremum of what is averaged, so \emph{no range of $x$ has to
be examined at all}. The tight case $q=6$ is immediate from $|1+2\cos v|\le3$.

The same identity explains Remark~\ref{rem:phipos} exactly: $\Phi_q\le0$ holds
precisely when $\|\Phi_q\|_{\infty}=|\Phi_q(-1)|$, that is, when $|\Phi_q|$
attains its maximum on the circle at $z=-1$. For $q=4,6,8,10,18$ it does; for
$q=12,15,20,21,24$ it does not, and those are exactly the counterexamples of
Remark~\ref{rem:phipos}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Large $q$ needs nothing new]\label{rem:flat}
For $n\le N_0$, H\"older's formula
$c_q(n)=\mu(q/g)\varphi(q)/\varphi(q/g)$ with $g=\gcd(n,q)\le N_0$ gives
$|c_q(n)|/\varphi(q)\le1/\varphi(q/g)$, and $\varphi(m)\gg m/\log\log m$ then
yields $|c_q(n)|/\varphi(q)\ll N_0\log\log q/q$. (One may not write
$\varphi(q/g)\ge\varphi(q/N_0)$: $\varphi$ is not monotone.) Hence
$\Phi_q\to0$ uniformly as $q\to\infty$, and
$\log M(q)+\Phi_q\le\log(\sqrt3/2)+o(1)$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:six}. So only a
finite list of $q$ would ever be at issue even without
Proposition~\ref{prop:supnorm}.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[$\ell^{1}$ reduction]\label{lem:l1}
$\|\Phi_q\|_{\infty}\le\|\Phi_q\|_{1}:=\sum_i|c_i|$, where $\Phi_q=\sum_ic_ix^i$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$|\Phi_q(z)|=|\sum_ic_iz^{i}|\le\sum_i|c_i||z|^{i}=\sum_i|c_i|$ for $|z|=1$.
(Formalised: \texttt{norm\_poly\_le\_l1\_on\_circle}.)
\end{proof}

\noindent
Since $\varphi(q)$ is even for $q\ge3$, the quantity $3^{\varphi(q)/2}$ is an
integer, so the criterion of Lemma~\ref{lem:supnorm} becomes a comparison of two
integers:
\[
  \sum_i|c_i|\;\le\;3^{\varphi(q)/2}.
\]
We have verified this by exact integer arithmetic for $3\le q\le1200$. It holds
throughout, with equality \emph{only} at $q=3$ and $q=6$. Writing
$\lambda(q)=\varphi(q)^{-1}\log\|\Phi_q\|_1$ for the quantity that must stay
below $\log\sqrt3=0.5493$:
\[
  \begin{array}{l|ccccccc}
    Q & 3 & 7 & 13 & 61 & 241 & 481 & 961\\\hline
    \sup_{q\ge Q}\lambda(q) & 0.5493 & 0.4024 & 0.3243 & 0.1354 & 0.0526
      & 0.0337 & 0.0182
  \end{array}
\]
So $Q_4=7$ suffices: past the two extremal moduli $q=3,6$ the margin is already
$0.147$ per element, and it grows. The $\ell^{1}$ norm itself barely moves ---
it is $7$ at $q=240$, against $3^{32}$ on the right.

Two structural remarks cut the problem down further. First,
$\Phi_n(x)=\Phi_{\mathrm{rad}(n)}(x^{n/\mathrm{rad}(n)})$, so
$\|\Phi_n\|_1=\|\Phi_{\mathrm{rad}(n)}\|_1$ exactly, while
$\varphi(n)\ge\varphi(\mathrm{rad}(n))$; hence
$\lambda(n)\le\lambda(\mathrm{rad}(n))$ and \emph{only squarefree $q$ need be
considered}. (Verified for $3\le n\le300$: no exceptions.) Second, the
extremal moduli are exactly the ones with the smallest $\varphi$: $q=3,6$ give
$\|\Phi_q\|_1=3=3^{\varphi(q)/2}$, and every larger squarefree modulus has
enough room in $\varphi(q)$ to absorb its $\ell^{1}$ norm.

\begin{lemma}[$\ell^{1}$ from the M\"obius product]\label{lem:l1geom}
For squarefree $q$ with $\omega(q)=\omega$ prime factors,
$\|\Phi_q\|_1\le\bigl(2(\varphi(q)+1)\bigr)^{2^{\omega-1}}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $\Phi_q=\prod_{d\mid q}(x^{d}-1)^{\mu(q/d)}$. Exactly $2^{\omega-1}$
divisors have $\mu(q/d)=+1$ and $2^{\omega-1}$ have $\mu(q/d)=-1$. Each factor
of the first kind has $\|x^{d}-1\|_1=2$. For the second kind expand
$(x^{d}-1)^{-1}=-(1+x^{d}+x^{2d}+\cdots)$ and truncate above degree
$\varphi(q)=\deg\Phi_q$: the truncation has at most $\varphi(q)+1$ terms, all of
modulus~$1$, so its $\ell^{1}$ norm is at most $\varphi(q)+1$. Since
$\Phi_q$ is a polynomial of degree $\varphi(q)$, its coefficients agree with
those of the product of the truncations, and $\|\cdot\|_1$ is submultiplicative.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Comparison, as an integer inequality]\label{lem:l1small}
For $q$ odd, squarefree and $q\notin\{3,5,15\}$,
\[
  \bigl(2\varphi(q)+2\bigr)^{2^{\omega(q)}}\;\le\;3^{\varphi(q)},
\]
which is exactly $\bigl(2(\varphi+1)\bigr)^{2^{\omega-1}}\le3^{\varphi/2}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Both sides are integers, so the statement is a comparison of integers. Set
$D(\varphi)=\tfrac12\varphi\log3-2^{\omega-1}\log(2\varphi+2)$ at fixed
$\omega$. Then $D'(\varphi)=\tfrac12\log3-2^{\omega}/(2\varphi+2)$, so $D$ is
increasing once $\varphi+1>2^{\omega}/\log3$. For each $\omega$ the smallest
admissible $\varphi$ is that of the odd primorial $3\cdot5\cdots p_{\omega+1}$,
namely $2,8,48,480,5760,\dots$, and these already exceed the thresholds
$1.82,3.64,7.28,14.6,29.1,\dots$; so $D$ is increasing on the whole admissible
range of $\varphi$ for every $\omega$, and it suffices to inspect the smallest
few values of $\varphi$ at each $\omega$. For $\omega\ge3$ the minimum already
passes: at $\varphi=48$ one has $98^{8}=8.5\cdot10^{15}\le3^{48}=8.0\cdot10^{22}$.
For $\omega=2$ the values $\varphi=8$ ($q=15$) fails, $104976>6561$, and
$\varphi=12$ ($q=21$) passes, $26^{4}=456976\le3^{12}=531441$. For $\omega=1$,
$\varphi=2$ ($q=3$) fails, $36>9$; $\varphi=4$ ($q=5$) fails, $100>81$;
$\varphi=6$ ($q=7$) passes, $196\le729$. Monotonicity closes each case upward.

It remains to pass from the finitely many $\omega$ inspected to all of them.
Write $\varphi_\omega$ for the value at the odd primorial and
$S(\omega)=\varphi_\omega\log3-2^{\omega}\log(2\varphi_\omega+2)$. With
$p=p_{\omega+2}$ the next prime, $\varphi_{\omega+1}=(p-1)\varphi_\omega$ and
$2\varphi_{\omega+1}+2\le(p-1)(2\varphi_\omega+2)$, so
\[
  S(\omega+1)\;\ge\;(p-1)\varphi_\omega\log3-2^{\omega+1}
     \bigl[\log(2\varphi_\omega+2)+\log(p-1)\bigr]
  \;\ge\;(p-3)\bigl[\varphi_\omega\log3-2^{\omega+1}\bigr],
\]
using $S(\omega)\ge0$ for the middle step and $\log(p-1)\le p-3$ (valid for
every prime $p\ge5$) for the last. So the induction runs on the auxiliary
invariant $\varphi_\omega\log3\ge2^{\omega+1}$ --- which does double duty, since
$2^{\omega+1}>2^{\omega}$ makes it imply the monotonicity condition
$\varphi>2^{\omega}/\log3$ used above --- and which holds at $\omega=3$
($52.7\ge16$) and is preserved because $\varphi$ is multiplied by
$p-1\ge10$ at each step while $2^{\omega+1}$ only doubles: the ratio grows by a
factor at least $5$ per step, from $3.3$ at $\omega=3$ to $5.9\cdot10^{9}$ at
$\omega=12$. Hence $S(\omega)\ge0$ for all $\omega\ge3$; numerically the ratio
of the two sides falls from $0.696$ at $\omega=3$ to $2.7\cdot10^{-9}$ at
$\omega=12$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[The sup-norm bound]\label{prop:supnorm}
$\|\Phi_q\|_\infty\le\|\Phi_q\|_1\le3^{\varphi(q)/2}$ for every $q\ge3$, with
equality only at $q=3$ and $q=6$. Hence $\lambda(q)\le\log\sqrt3$ throughout.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Powers of two.} For $q=2^{j}$ with $j\ge2$, $\Phi_q(x)=x^{2^{j-1}}+1$, so
$\|\Phi_q\|_1=2\le3=3^{\varphi(q)/2}$ at $j=2$ and more comfortably beyond.

\emph{Reduction to odd squarefree $q$.} By the paragraph above,
$\lambda(n)\le\lambda(\mathrm{rad}\,n)$, so $q$ may be taken squarefree. If
$q=2m$ with $m$ odd then $\Phi_{2m}(x)=\Phi_m(-x)$, which changes only the signs
of the coefficients: $\|\Phi_{2m}\|_1=\|\Phi_m\|_1$ and
$\varphi(2m)=\varphi(m)$, so $\lambda(2m)=\lambda(m)$ exactly.

\emph{The three exceptions.} For $q=3,5$ the polynomial is
$1+x+\cdots+x^{q-1}$, so $\|\Phi_q\|_1=q$: this gives $3\le3^{1}$ with
\emph{equality}, and $5\le3^{2}=9$. For $q=15$, $\|\Phi_{15}\|_1=7\le3^{4}=81$.

\emph{Everything else.} Lemma~\ref{lem:l1geom} followed by
Lemma~\ref{lem:l1small}.

\emph{Equality.} By the reduction, $q=6$ inherits the equality at $q=3$; and
the exact integer comparisons above show these are the only two.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The two equality cases are one polynomial]\label{rem:onepoly}
It is worth pausing on this, because it closes the circle the paper opened.
The only equalities are $q=3$ and $q=6$, and by the reduction above they are the
\emph{same} polynomial seen from two sides: $\Phi_6(x)=\Phi_3(-x)$, that is,
$x^2-x+1$ and $x^2+x+1$. The first section of the paper derived the peak height
$M(q)=\tfrac12|\Phi_q(-1)|^{1/\varphi(q)}$, whose supremum $\sqrt3/2$ is
attained exactly at $q=6$ because $\Phi_6(-1)=3$; the last section needs
$\|\Phi_q\|_\infty\le3^{\varphi(q)/2}$, whose only equalities are at
$\Phi_3$ and $\Phi_6$ because $\|\Phi_3\|_1=3$. The value of the polynomial at
$-1$ and its maximum modulus on the circle coincide there, and it is the same
$3$ both times.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[On the literature]\label{rem:bzdega}
This deserves a word, because it turns out we had reinvented a named object.
The sup norm $\max_{|z|=1}|\Phi_n(z)|$ has been studied under the name $L_n$
alongside the height $A_n=\max_m|a_n(m)|$, the length $S_n=\sum_m|a_n(m)|$ and
$Q_n=\sum_ma_n(m)^2$; see Sanna's survey~\cite{San22} for the field and
Bzd\k{e}ga~\cite{Bz16,Bz12} for the sharpest bounds. Three remarks are worth
recording. First, our $\ell^{1}$ reduction (Lemma~\ref{lem:l1}) is the step
$L_n\le S_n$ of the chain $L_n/n\le S_n/n\le\sqrt{Q_n/n}\le A_n$ quoted there,
and the sharp order in that literature is $M_n=\prod_{j\le k-2}p_j^{2^{k-j-1}-1}$,
against which our Lemma~\ref{lem:l1geom} is very crude --- but crude is enough
here, and it is effective.

Second, a caution we record because we walked into it. The sharpest constants
there are stated for $\mathcal{B}_k=\limsup_{p_1\to\infty}A_n/M_n$, a limit as
the \emph{smallest} prime factor grows, with an error $\epsilon_k\to0$ carrying
no explicit rate. Our worst case is the odd primorial $3\cdot5\cdot7\cdots$,
whose smallest prime factor is fixed at $3$ --- precisely outside that limit ---
so those constants cannot be used as a pointwise bound here. This is why
Proposition~\ref{prop:supnorm} is proved from scratch above.
Third, the extremal cases match: the only equality in
Proposition~\ref{prop:supnorm} is at $\varphi=2$, where
$\Phi_3(x)=x^2+x+1$ and $\Phi_6(x)=x^2-x+1$ both have $L=3=3^{\varphi/2}$, and
these are precisely the two moduli that our peak analysis singles out --- $q=6$
because $M(6)=\sqrt3/2$ is the supremum of the peak heights, and $q=3$ because
it is the odd companion of $6$.
\end{remark}


\begin{lemma}[Twisted sums, uniformly]\label{lem:twist}
Let $q\le(\log N)^{A}$, let $r$ be coprime to $q$, let $n\le N_0$ and let
$|t|\le1/(q\tau)$. Then
\[
  \sum_{\substack{p\in B\\ p\equiv r\ (q)}}e(pnt)
  \;=\;\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{p\in B}e(pnt)
   \;+\;O\!\bigl(N e^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}(\log N)^{A+A'}\bigr),
\]
uniformly in $t$, $n$ and $r$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $f(u)=e(unt)$, $F(u)=\#\{p\in B:p\le u,\ p\equiv r\ (q)\}$ and
$G(u)=\#\{p\in B:p\le u\}$. Abel summation gives
$\sum_{p\equiv r}e(pnt)=f(N)F(N)-\int_{2d}^{N}F(u)f'(u)\,du$ and the same
identity with $G/\varphi(q)$ in place of $F$. Subtracting and writing
$E(u)=F(u)-G(u)/\varphi(q)$,
\[
  \Bigl|\sum_{p\equiv r}e(pnt)-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{p}e(pnt)\Bigr|
  \;\le\;|E(N)|+\int_{2d}^{N}|E(u)|\,|f'(u)|\,du
  \;\le\;E^{*}\bigl(1+2\pi N|nt|\bigr),
\]
since $|f'(u)|=2\pi|nt|$. Two inputs finish it. First, $E^{*}=\sup_{u\le
N}|E(u)|\ll Ne^{-c\sqrt{\log u}}$ uniformly for $q\le(\log N)^{A}$, by the
Siegel--Walfisz theorem applied to $\pi(u;q,r)$ and the prime number theorem
applied to $\pi(u)$: both have main term $\mathrm{li}(u)/\varphi(q)$ and
$\mathrm{li}(u)$ respectively, and these cancel in $E$. Second,
$|t|\le1/(q\tau)=(\log N)^{A}/(qN)$ and $n\le N_0=(\log N)^{A'}$ give
$N|nt|\le(\log N)^{A+A'}/q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
It is worth keeping the Siegel--Walfisz saving in the form
$e^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}$ rather than converting it to $(\log N)^{-C}$ for some
fixed $C$. The assembly below multiplies the error by $\varphi(q)\le(\log
N)^{A}$, sums it against $\sum_{n\le N_0}1/n$, and compares it with
$b/N_0\gg N(\log N)^{-1-A'}$; a fixed power of $\log N$ would have to be chosen
larger than $2(A+A')+1$, which with $A=11$, $A'=12$ means $C>47$. Carrying
$e^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}$, which beats every power of $\log N$, removes the
bookkeeping entirely.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Annulus profile]\label{lem:annulus}
Let $q\le(\log N)^{A}$, let $\theta=2\pi j/q+t$ with $(j,q)=1$, and let
$\Phi_q(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\,\frac{c_q(n)}{\varphi(q)}
\bigl(\mathrm{Re}\,\widetilde I(nx)-1\bigr)$, where $c_q$ is the Ramanujan sum and
$\widetilde I$ is the window of Appendix~\ref{sec:window}. Then
\[
  \tfrac1b\log|G_B(\theta)|\;=\;\log M(q)+\Phi_q(N|t|)+o(1),
\]
an \emph{equality}, where $\Phi_q(x)\to0$ as $x\to0$ and
$\Phi_q(x)\to-\log2-\log M(q)$ as $x\to\infty$, so the profile interpolates
between the peak height and the deep-minor bound of Section~\ref{sec:l5c}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
In~\eqref{eq:fourier} write $\theta=2\pi j/q+t$ and split the inner sum by
residue class, $S_B(n\theta)=\sum_{r\bmod q}e(nrj/q)\sum_{p\equiv
r}e(pnt)$. The classes with $(r,q)>1$ contain at most $\omega(q)$ primes in
total, so they contribute $O(\omega(q))=O(\log\log N)$. To the remaining
$\varphi(q)$ classes apply Lemma~\ref{lem:twist}; the errors add to
$\varphi(q)\cdot O(Ne^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}(\log N)^{A+A'})
\ll Ne^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}(\log N)^{2A+A'}=o(b/N_0)$, and the main terms
assemble as
\[
  S_B(n\theta)=\frac{c_q(nj)}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{p\in B}e(pnt)+o(b/N_0)
  =\frac{c_q(n)}{\varphi(q)}\,b\,\widetilde I(Nnt)+o(b/N_0),
\]
using $(j,q)=1$ to replace $c_q(nj)$ by $c_q(n)$. Summing over $n\le N_0$
against $1/n$ multiplies the error by $O(\log N_0)$ and leaves $o(b)$. The tail
$n>N_0$ is the Abel summation of step~4 of Section~\ref{sec:l5c}, and the points
where the series must be truncated are counted by Lemma~\ref{lem:fejer}. What
remains is $-b\log2+b\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\frac{c_q(n)}{\varphi(q)}
\mathrm{Re}\,\widetilde I(Nnt)$, which is $b(\log M(q)+\Phi_q(N|t|))$ by the
definition of $\Phi_q$ and the cyclotomic identity
$-\log2+\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\frac{c_q(n)}{\varphi(q)}=\log M(q)$ at
$t=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[How the profile may and may not be used]\label{rem:profileuse}
Lemma~\ref{lem:annulus} is an asymptotic equality, and the $o(1)$ is two-sided:
Remark~\ref{rem:profile} finds the measurement above the profile at about half
the sampled points. To use it as an upper bound one needs
$\Phi_q(x)\le-c<0$ for $x$ beyond the reach of the envelope. Writing
$\Phi_q(x)=R_q(x)-\log(2M(q))$ with
$R_q(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\frac{c_q(n)}{\varphi(q)}
\mathrm{Re}\,\widetilde I(nx)$, the bound $|\mathrm{Re}\,\widetilde
I|\le\min(1,2/x)$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:window} together with
$|c_q(n)|\le\varphi(q)$ gives $|R_q(x)|\le\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{3x}$, hence
\[
  \Phi_q(x)\le-\tfrac12\log(2M(q))\quad\text{for}\quad
  x\ge\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3\log(2M(q))},
\]
which is $x\ge12.0$ for $q=6$, $x\ge16.4$ for $q=10$ and $x\ge19.0$ for $q=4$.
The envelope of Proposition~\ref{prop:curv} reaches only $x=N|t|\approx1.5$.
\emph{The compact range in between is covered by neither.} It is a much smaller
gap than the one it replaces --- by Lemma~\ref{lem:closed} the quantity involved
is a running average of an explicit elementary function, with no arithmetic in
it --- so a rigorous finite computation settles it; but we do not claim it.
Numerically $\Phi_6$ is $-0.144$ at $x=1.5$, $-0.314$ at $x=2$ and below
$-0.549$ from $x=3$ onwards, so the profile does not return to zero. We record
the statement, and Proposition~\ref{prop:supnorm} below settles it.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Closed form of the profile]\label{lem:closed}
For $q\ge3$ and $x>0$,
\[
  \Phi_q(x)=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\Bigl(\frac1x\int_0^{x}
    \log\bigl|\Phi_q(-e^{-iv})\bigr|\,dv-\log\bigl|\Phi_q(-1)\bigr|\Bigr),
\]
where $\Phi_q$ on the right is the $q$-th cyclotomic polynomial. In particular
$\Phi_q(x)$ is a running average of $\log|\Phi_q|$ along the unit circle,
started at the point $-1$ and normalised to vanish at $x=0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\mathrm{Re}\,\widetilde I(nx)=\sin(nx)/(nx)=\frac1x\int_0^{x}\cos(nv)\,dv$, so
interchanging the sum and the integral,
\[
  \Phi_q(x)+\log\bigl(2M(q)\bigr)
  =\frac1x\int_0^{x}\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\frac{c_q(n)}{\varphi(q)}
     \cos(nv)\,dv .
\]
Writing $c_q(n)=\sum_{(r,q)=1}\cos(2\pi nr/q)$, pairing $r$ with $-r$, and using
$\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\cos(n\vartheta)=\log|2\cos(\vartheta/2)|$, the
inner sum equals $\varphi(q)^{-1}\sum_{(r,q)=1}\log|2\cos(v/2+\pi r/q)|$.
Finally $\prod_{(r,q)=1}(1+z\zeta_q^{r})=(-z)^{\varphi(q)}\Phi_q(-1/z)$ gives
$\sum_{(r,q)=1}\log|2\cos(v/2+\pi r/q)|=\log|\Phi_q(-e^{-iv})|$ for $|z|=1$, and
the constant is the value at $v=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why $\Phi_q\le0$ is false in general]\label{rem:phipos}
Lemma~\ref{lem:closed} settles the shape of the question and also shows that the
naive form of it is wrong. By Jensen's formula the mean of $\log|\Phi_q|$ over
the whole circle is $\log$ of the Mahler measure of $\Phi_q$, which is $0$. So
if $|\Phi_q(-1)|=1$ --- equivalently $M(q)=1/2$ --- the running average starts at
$0$ and returns to $0$, hence must be positive somewhere, and $\Phi_q\le0$
fails. Numerically $q=12,15,20,21,24$ are all counterexamples, with maxima up to
$+0.109$. This costs nothing: those are exactly the $q$ with the smallest peak,
and $\log M(q)+0.109=-0.584$ is still far below $\log(\sqrt3/2)=-0.144$. It is
the reason Proposition~\ref{prop:supnorm} is stated for
$\|\Phi_q\|_\infty$ rather than for the sign of $\Phi_q$ alone. For $q=6$, where $\Phi_6(-1)=3$, the statement
specialises to
\[
  \frac1Y\int_0^{Y}\log|1+2\cos v|\,dv\;\le\;\log3\qquad(Y>0),
\]
which is sharp only as $Y\to0^{+}$: numerically the supremum over $Y\ge1$ is
$0.981$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Numerical test of Lemma~\ref{lem:annulus}]\label{rem:profile}
The profile is a prediction with no free parameters, so it can be falsified. We
swept $t$ at $q=6$ for $k=40,60,100,140$ and compared the measured drop
$\tfrac1b\log|G_B(2\pi/6+t)|-\tfrac1b\log|G_B(2\pi/6)|$ against
$\Phi_6(Nt)$, computing the window exactly from $B$ rather than from its
smooth approximation, so that only the decoupling of equidistribution from the
$t$-oscillation is being tested. Over $36$ values of $x=Nt$ in $[0.25,40]$:
\[
  \begin{array}{l|cccc}
    b & 39 & 59 & 99 & 139\\\hline
    \text{max}|{\rm error}| & 0.107 & 0.082 & 0.054 & 0.059\\
    \text{RMS error} & 0.0354 & 0.0370 & 0.0202 & 0.0186\\
    \sqrt b\cdot\text{RMS} & 0.221 & 0.284 & 0.201 & 0.219
  \end{array}
\]
The last row is flat, so the error is of the square-root size $b^{-1/2}$ one
would expect, and it is an error in both directions --- the profile is an
asymptotic equality, not a pointwise majorant, and the measurement exceeds it at
about half the sample points. This is why the $o(1)$ in
Lemma~\ref{lem:annulus} cannot be dropped. The saturation value is measured at
$-0.52$ against the predicted $-\log2-\log M(6)=-0.549$, and at $x=0.25,1,1.75$
the agreement at $b=139$ is $-0.00308$ versus $-0.00310$, $-0.05233$ versus
$-0.05221$, $-0.18862$ versus $-0.18683$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The notch at $q=10$, and why it is a free test]\label{rem:notch}
For $d=2$ one has $5\in B$, so $G_B(2\pi/10)=0$ exactly by
Lemma~\ref{lem:vanish}. The vanishing is \emph{pointwise}: it removes one factor
at one point and leaves the surrounding peak intact. Lemma~\ref{lem:annulus},
applied to $B\smallsetminus\{5\}$ and multiplied by the explicit factor
$|\cos(5\theta/2)|=|\sin(5t/2)|$, predicts the height of the two maxima flanking
the notch with no free parameter. Maximising
$b^{-1}\log|\sin(5t/2)|+\tfrac{b-1}{b}(\log M(10)+\Phi_{10}(Nt))$ gives
\[
  \begin{array}{l|cccc}
    b & 39 & 59 & 99 & 139\\\hline
    \text{measured} & -0.446 & -0.404 & -0.366 & -0.351\\
    \text{predicted} & -0.429 & -0.394 & -0.361 & -0.345\\
    \log M(10)-\tfrac32(\log b)/b & -0.432 & -0.394 & -0.360 & -0.344
  \end{array}
\]
The third row is the closed form obtained by balancing $b^{-1}\log(5t/2)$
against the quadratic part of $\Phi_{10}$; it agrees with the full profile to
three decimals. So the flank height tends to $\log M(10)$ at the rate
$\tfrac32(\log b)/b$, and since $M(10)=5^{1/4}/2>1/\sqrt2=M(4)$, the
second-largest peak family for $d=2$ is asymptotically $q=10$ rather than $q=4$.
It remains far below $\sqrt3/2$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[A sub-product bound, and its range]\label{rem:subproduct}
One can also try to widen the certified envelope directly, by applying
Proposition~\ref{prop:curv} to the sub-product over $\{a\in B:a\le A_1\}$: its
factors have zeros spaced $2\pi/A_1$ apart, and $|G_B|$ never exceeds it, so the
only cost is the smaller curvature $V_0(A_1)=\sum_{a\le A_1}a^{2}/4$. The
counting decides where this is useful. An annulus exists only for
$q\lesssim(\log N)^{A}$, and there the admissible cutoff is
$A_1\lesssim qN/(\log N)^{A}$, leaving $\pi(A_1)\approx bq/(\log N)^{A}$
factors. For $q$ near the top of the band this is a positive proportion of $b$
and the argument works; for small $q$ --- including $q=6$, the case that
matters --- the exponent collapses by a factor $(\log N)^{A}$ and no bound of
the form $(\text{const})^{b}$ survives. We record the device for the thin band
where it applies and rely on Lemma~\ref{lem:annulus} elsewhere.
\end{remark}

\begin{problem}[Assembly]\label{prob:assembly}
Combine these to obtain $\varepsilon_d(k)\ll(\sqrt3/2)^{k}$, and hence
$\varepsilon_k\to0$ at a geometric rate.
\end{problem}

\noindent
We emphasise one negative result of the companion paper: there is \emph{no}
window analogue of the modulus-$4$ obstruction. Explicit odd sequences with
$\varepsilon_2=0$ exist at every size, so no lower bound for $\varepsilon_d$ can
be extracted from the aggregate residue-class spread. The route to
Problem~\ref{prob:assembly} must go through the upper bounds above.

\subsection{Three consequences worth stating}

\emph{First, $C_1$ is the only ineffective constant in the paper.} Its
ineffectivity is not an artefact of how we quote the theorem: for a
\emph{fixed} modulus the prime number theorem in arithmetic progressions is
effective, and what makes $C_1$ ineffective is that we need it uniformly for

zero whose location no known argument pins down. It is Siegel's constant, and
it propagates to exactly two places: Lemma~\ref{lem:twist} (hence
Lemma~\ref{lem:annulus}) and step 2 of Section~\ref{sec:l5c}. Everything else
--- the whole spectral chain of Sections~\ref{sec:m1}--\ref{sec:l5a}, the
envelope of Proposition~\ref{prop:curv}, and the entire sup-norm argument of
Section~\ref{sec:remains} --- is effective, and in the case of
Propositions~\ref{prop:curv} and \ref{prop:supnorm} and
Lemmas~\ref{lem:l1}--\ref{lem:l1small} there is no implied constant to name:
those statements are inequalities between explicit quantities.

worth keeping in view.} While the deep-minor bound was still carried as a
hypothesis, this paper had two deficiencies of quite different kinds: one that
writing more would cure, and one that writing more would not.
Proposition~\ref{prop:deepminor} has removed the first.
Theorem~\ref{thm:t2main} is now unconditional, and $C_1$ is its only remaining
deficiency --- a property of Siegel's theorem, not of our argument. Note that
the two were never located in the same place: Lemma~\ref{lem:twist} was proved,
not hypothesised, yet it carried $C_1$ throughout.

\emph{Third, the rate itself needs none of this.} By
Remark~\ref{rem:whatneeded}(i) the conclusion $\varepsilon_d\to0$ at rate
$e^{1/8}\sqrt3/2=0.98134\ldots$ per element uses only $C_4$, which is effective
and absolute. The improvement to the rate $\sqrt3/2$ is what costs $C_1$.

\section{Appendix: the window function}\label{sec:window}

Lemma~\ref{lem:annulus} needs one elementary object: the Fourier transform of
the interval $[0,N]$, normalised to $1$ at the origin.

\begin{definition}
$\displaystyle\widetilde I(x)=\frac1N\int_0^{N}e\Bigl(\frac{ux}{2\pi N}\Bigr)du
 =\frac{e^{ix}-1}{ix}$, extended by $\widetilde I(0)=1$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:window}
$\widetilde I$ is continuous, $\widetilde I(0)=1$,
$\mathrm{Re}\,\widetilde I(x)=\sin x/x$, and
\[
  |\widetilde I(x)|=\frac{2|\sin(x/2)|}{|x|}\le\min\Bigl(1,\frac{2}{|x|}\Bigr),
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Re}\,\widetilde I(x)=1-\frac{x^{2}}{6}+O(x^{4}) .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\int_0^{N}e^{iux/N}du=N(e^{ix}-1)/(ix)$; then
$|e^{ix}-1|=2|\sin(x/2)|\le\min(|x|,2)$, and the expansion is that of
$\sin x/x$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The primes are not uniform on $[0,N]$ but have density $1/\log u$, so the
window that actually occurs is
$\widetilde I_{\log}(x)=b^{-1}\int_2^{N}e^{iux/N}\,du/\log u$. The second mean
value theorem gives $|\widetilde I_{\log}(x)|\le\min(1,C\log N/|x|)$, which is
the same shape with a $\log N$ in the constant; since $\Phi_q$ is only ever used
through $|\widetilde I|\le\min(1,C/x)$, nothing downstream changes. In the
numerical test of Remark~\ref{rem:profile} we did not use either approximation:
the window was computed exactly as $b^{-1}\sum_{p\in B}e^{ipt}$, so that the
only thing being tested was the decoupling of equidistribution from the
oscillation in $t$.
\end{remark}

\section{Appendix: a self-contained one-interval Erd\H{o}s--Tur\'an bound}
\label{sec:fejer}

Step~3 of Section~\ref{sec:l5c} needs to count the points of a finite family
that land in one short interval. The Erd\H{o}s--Tur\'an inequality does this
uniformly over all intervals, at the price of the Beurling--Selberg extremal
function. For a single interval one can construct the majorant from the Fej\'er
kernel in half a page, which is what we do, so that the section incurs no
external reference.

Let $F_M(t)=\sum_{|n|\le M}\bigl(1-\tfrac{|n|}{M+1}\bigr)e(nt)
=\tfrac{1}{M+1}\bigl(\tfrac{\sin\pi(M+1)t}{\sin\pi t}\bigr)^{2}$ be the Fej\'er
kernel: a nonnegative trigonometric polynomial of degree $M$ with
$\int_{0}^{1}F_M=1$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:fejer}
Let $x_1,\dots,x_b\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, let $I$ be an interval of length
$2\delta$, and let $M\ge1$. Put $S(n)=\sum_{j\le b}e(nx_j)$. Then
\[
  \#\{j:x_j\in I\}\;\le\;4b\Bigl(\delta+\frac{1}{M+1}\Bigr)
   +4\sum_{n=1}^{M}\min\Bigl(2\delta+\frac{2}{M+1},\frac{1}{\pi n}\Bigr)\,|S(n)| .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $w=1/(M+1)$. For $w\le|t|\le\tfrac12$ one has $\sin\pi t\ge2t$, so
$F_M(t)\le\bigl(4(M+1)t^{2}\bigr)^{-1}$ and hence
$\int_{w\le|t|\le1/2}F_M\le\tfrac{1}{2(M+1)w}=\tfrac12$; consequently
$\int_{|t|<w}F_M\ge\tfrac12$.

Let $J$ be the interval with the same centre as $I$ and length $2\delta+2w$, and
set $P=2\,(\mathbf{1}_J*F_M)$. Then $P$ is a trigonometric polynomial of degree
at most $M$. If $x\in I$ then $x-J\supseteq(-w,w)$, so
$(\mathbf{1}_J*F_M)(x)=\int_{x-J}F_M\ge\tfrac12$ and $P(x)\ge1$; thus
$P\ge\mathbf{1}_I$ pointwise. Its coefficients are
$\widehat P(n)=2\,\widehat{\mathbf{1}_J}(n)\bigl(1-\tfrac{|n|}{M+1}\bigr)$, and
$|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_J}(n)|=\bigl|\tfrac{\sin(\pi n|J|)}{\pi n}\bigr|
\le\min\bigl(|J|,\tfrac{1}{\pi|n|}\bigr)$, while
$\widehat P(0)=2|J|=4\delta+4w$. Therefore
\[
  \#\{j:x_j\in I\}\le\sum_{j\le b}P(x_j)
  =\sum_{|n|\le M}\widehat P(n)\,\overline{S(n)}
  \le b\widehat P(0)+2\sum_{n=1}^{M}|\widehat P(n)|\,|S(n)|,
\]
which is the stated bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The constant $4$ is wasteful --- the Beurling--Selberg majorant replaces it
by~$1$ --- but Section~\ref{sec:l5c} only needs $o(b)$, and the bound is applied
with $\delta=\delta_0\to0$ and $M=N_0\to\infty$, so absolute constants are
immaterial. The numerical values of the resulting bound are tabulated in
Remark~\ref{rem:budget}.
\end{remark}

\section{Appendix: the companion paper's Problem on random comparison}
\label{sec:p5}

The companion paper asks whether $\mathrm{lm}_{P_k}/\mathrm{lm}_{R_k}\to0$,
where $P_k$ is the first $k$ odd primes and $R_k$ a random odd sequence of the
same length drawn from the same range, and reduces this to comparing
$\Gamma(P_k)$, which converges, with $\mathbb{E}[\Gamma(R_k)]$. The expectation
is exactly computable.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:p5exact}
Let $R_k$ be a uniformly random $k$-subset of the $N$ odd integers in
$[3,p_k]$, where $N=(p_k-3)/2+1$, and put $c=(N+1)/(k+1)$. Then
\[
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[\Gamma(R_k)\bigr]
   =\bigl(1-2^{-k}\bigr)+2c\bigl(2-(k+2)2^{-k}\bigr).
\]
In particular $\mathbb{E}[\Gamma(R_k)]=1+4c+O(k2^{-k})$, and since
$c\sim p_k/(2k)$, this diverges as soon as $p_k/k\to\infty$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Identify the odd integers in $[3,p_k]$ with $\{1,\dots,N\}$ via $a=2i+1$. For a
uniform $k$-subset the order statistics satisfy $\mathbb{E}[X_{(j)}]=j(N+1)/(k+1)$
exactly, so $\mathbb{E}[a_{(j)}]=1+2jc$. Hence
$\mathbb{E}[\Gamma]=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(1+2jc)2^{-j}
=(1-2^{-k})+2c(2-(k+2)2^{-k})$, using $\sum_{j\le k}j2^{-j}=2-(k+2)2^{-k}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
No prime number theorem is needed: only $p_k/k\to\infty$, i.e.\ that the primes
have density zero, which is elementary. Monte-Carlo agreement with
Theorem~\ref{thm:p5exact} is within one standard error at $k=100,400,800$
($z=-1.46,-0.38,+1.07$ over $20{,}000$ samples).
\end{remark}

For a statement in probability rather than in mean one needs a lower tail bound,
and the following suffices.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:p5tail}
Let $R_k$ be a uniform $k$-subset of $\{1,\dots,N\}$ and let $X_{(J)}$ denote its
$J$-th order statistic. For every integer $J\ge1$ and every $\delta>0$,
\[
  \mathbb{P}\bigl[X_{(J)}\le\delta N/k\bigr]
   \;\le\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi J}}\Bigl(\frac{e\delta}{J}\Bigr)^{J}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $m=\lfloor\delta N/k\rfloor$. The event $X_{(J)}\le\delta N/k$ says that at
least $J$ of the $k$ sampled points lie in $\{1,\dots,m\}$. Bounding the
probability of that event by the union over all $J$-subsets of $\{1,\dots,m\}$ of
the event that the whole subset is sampled,
\[
  \mathbb{P}\bigl[X_{(J)}\le\delta N/k\bigr]
  \;\le\;\binom{m}{J}\frac{k(k-1)\cdots(k-J+1)}{N(N-1)\cdots(N-J+1)}
  \;\le\;\binom{m}{J}\Bigl(\frac{k}{N}\Bigr)^{J},
\]
the last step because $(k-i)/(N-i)\le k/N$ for $0\le i<J\le k\le N$. Now
$\binom{m}{J}\le m^{J}/J!$ and, by Stirling, $J!\ge\sqrt{2\pi J}(J/e)^{J}$, so
\[
  \mathbb{P}\bigl[X_{(J)}\le\delta N/k\bigr]
  \le\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi J}}\Bigl(\frac{emk}{JN}\Bigr)^{J}
  \le\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi J}}\Bigl(\frac{e\delta}{J}\Bigr)^{J},
\]
using $m\le\delta N/k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:p5prob}
$\Gamma(P_k)/\Gamma(R_k)\to0$ in probability.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Since $\Gamma(P_k)\to5.3492879\ldots$ is bounded, say by $C$, it suffices to show
$\Gamma(R_k)\to\infty$ in probability: that for every $M>0$ and every $\eta>0$
there is $k_0$ with $\mathbb{P}[\Gamma(R_k)\le M]<\eta$ for all $k\ge k_0$.

Fix $M$ and $\eta$. The order of choice matters, so we set it out.

\emph{First choose $J$, depending only on $\eta$.} Take $\delta=\tfrac12$ in
Proposition~\ref{prop:p5tail}: the failure probability is
$(2\pi J)^{-1/2}(e/2J)^{J}$, which tends to $0$ as $J\to\infty$, so there is a
$J=J(\eta)$ with $(2\pi J)^{-1/2}(e/2J)^{J}<\eta$. \emph{Fix that $J$ for the
rest of the argument}; it does not grow with $k$.

\emph{Then choose $k_0$, depending on $J$ and $M$.} All terms of
$\Gamma(R_k)=\sum_{j}a_{(j)}2^{-j}$ are positive, so
$\Gamma(R_k)\ge2^{-J}a_{(J)}=2^{-J}X_{(J)}$. On the complement of the event of
Proposition~\ref{prop:p5tail} we have $X_{(J)}>N/(2k)$, whence
$\Gamma(R_k)>2^{-J-1}N/k$. Now $N=(p_k-3)/2+1$ and $p_k\sim k\log k$, so
$N/k\sim\tfrac12\log k\to\infty$; since $J$ is already fixed, there is
$k_0=k_0(J,M)$ with $2^{-J-1}N/k>M$ for all $k\ge k_0$.

\emph{Conclude.} For $k\ge k_0$ the event $\Gamma(R_k)\le M$ is contained in the
event of Proposition~\ref{prop:p5tail}, so
$\mathbb{P}[\Gamma(R_k)\le M]\le(2\pi J)^{-1/2}(e/2J)^{J}<\eta$. Hence
$\Gamma(R_k)\to\infty$ and $\Gamma(P_k)/\Gamma(R_k)\le C/\Gamma(R_k)\to0$, both
in probability.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The bookkeeping is worth one sentence because our earlier sketch made it look
harder than it is. We had written ``let $J\to\infty$ slowly'', which forces one
to balance two limits; in fact $J$ need not grow at all. For each tolerance
$\eta$ a single fixed $J$ suffices, and only $k_0$ then depends on the target
$M$. This is also why the factor $2$ discussed in the remark below is
irrelevant: $J$ is chosen before $M$ and never revisited.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Proposition~\ref{prop:p5tail} was checked over $48$ combinations
$k\in\{100,400,1600,3200\}$, $J\in\{1,2,4,8\}$, $\delta\in\{0.25,0.5,1\}$ with
$10^{4}$ samples each; it held in every case. Numerically the smaller quantity
$(e\delta/2J)^{J}$ also dominated the observed frequencies throughout, but this
reflects only the slack in the bound and not a sharper theorem: the expected
number of sample points below $\delta N/k$ is $\delta$, not $\delta/2$, so no
factor $2$ is available at this step. The stated form is the one the argument
proves, and the conclusion is unaffected in any case, since $J$ is chosen
after~$\delta$.
\end{remark}
